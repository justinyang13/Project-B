# THE LAKE THAT SWALLOWED THE SUN: story bible

Fantasy-engineering for readers 14-18 (High School shelf, author Yang, publisher Yang Ink). Anime art style (Shinkai/Ghibli, see Project-I/wiki/fantasy-style.md). A Taiwan-inspired highland lake with a sun-shaped east basin and a moon-shaped west basin, joined through a channel under Lantern Island by two ancient carved-stone gates, the Dragon Gates. In the old lake legend, two dragons stole the sun and the moon and played with them in the water until a brave couple climbed Elder Peak for the golden axe and the golden scissors. In this story the "dragons" are the gates, they have been jammed shut for thirty years, the Sun Basin is draining to mud, the Moon Basin is rising toward the village, and a lowland power company wants to replace the whole lake system with a concrete dam. Sixteen-year-old Shu Tsai, apprentice to her grandfather the lake's gatekeeper, has until the Mid-Autumn night (thirty days) to understand the lake, climb the mountain for the legend's tools, open the gates in the right order, and persuade the village that the old way and the new numbers can be one plan.

MAIN GOAL: the reader finishes wanting to measure water, understand levers and pumps, ask the old people who built a thing before changing it, and keep a promise to a place. Engineering is the hero. Every turn is solved by measurement, calculation and craft, shown in scene, never lectured.

LESSON: **Old knowledge and new numbers are one language. Keep what was built with care, and ask the people who live with a thing before you change it.** Dramatized through choices; one earned plain statement near the end (Granny Hua, ch24; Shu, ch25).

## CULTURAL HANDLING (user decision 2026-10-10: fictional, credited)
- The story is INSPIRED BY the Thao legend of Sun Moon Lake (two dragons play with the sun and moon in a lake; a young couple climbs a sacred mountain for a golden axe and golden scissors to free them). The lake people in the book are FICTIONAL with invented customs; no real Thao ritual, name, song or sacred object is depicted. The real Thao are a small Indigenous people (about 890 in 2024) whose language is nearly extinct and who were displaced when their lake was dammed; the book must never make the dam story a villain-vs-hero cartoon.
- Taiwan-inspired texture (use it, accurate, lightly): misty highland at ~750 m, camphor and bamboo, tea terraces, Taiwan blue magpies (long blue tails), cicadas, plum rain and the typhoon season, red lanterns, ferries, scooters on narrow roads, Mid-Autumn Festival (full moon, mooncakes, pomelo, grilling outdoors, lanterns), night-market food (scallion pancakes, taro balls, sticky rice, fish ball soup, bubble tea), temple lanterns, Hokkien/Mandarin kin words (A-gong grandfather, A-ma grandmother, Aunt, Uncle) used sparingly. Do NOT invent foreign words beyond these.
- An AUTHOR'S NOTE (end of book, in meta blurb text or last page) credits the Thao legend, says Sun Moon Lake is real, and points readers to Thao voices and sources. Never put invented lore in the note.

## Reading level and voice
SAT-level prose: precise, varied vocabulary (e.g. meticulous, tenuous, assiduous, equanimity, ostensibly, pragmatic, laconic, ephemeral, lucid, inexorable, discrepancy, corroborate, ingenuity, scrupulous, tenacity, prudent, redundant, perfunctory, resilient, stoic, equitable, deference, reverence), longer sentences with subordinate clauses, dry wit, interior thought. THIRD PERSON, PAST TENSE, close on Shu. Warm, funny, hopeful; nothing frightening (weather, deadlines, stubborn machinery only). Light clean romance (a look, a hand, sharing an umbrella). No em dashes.
Voice sample:
"*The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 1: Water always finds its level, and it is never impatient about it.*

On the morning the sun went out of the east half of the lake, Shu Tsai was standing in her green rain boots in the middle of what had been a fishing cove, holding a bamboo pole upright in the mud and explaining to forty-one stranded carp that none of this was her fault."

## The lake and its physics (keep exact)
- **Two-Light Lake**: a mountain lake at about 750 m, in a bowl of misty green ridges. Seen from above it is shaped like a sun (round, the east or **Sun Basin**, shallow, about 8 m deep, feeds the terraces and the mill) and a crescent moon (the west or **Moon Basin**, deep, about 25 m, where the mountain streams enter from **Elder Peak**). **Lantern Island** sits between them. Through the island runs a stone channel closed by two carved dragon-head gates: the **Sun Dragon** (east) and the **Moon Dragon** (west), each a stone sluice with a bronze-bound wooden gate and a vertical lever socket. Water flows from the higher Moon Basin through the channel into the Sun Basin; the Sun Basin drains to the terraces, mill race and the river below.
- **Jammed for thirty years.** The gates were shut during a great flood and never reopened; the lever pin sheared; silt and a drowned log have packed the channel. Result: the Moon Basin rises each wet season (now 1.4 m above the old marks, threatening the lower village, Mistwater Landing, and the Lantern Temple steps) while the Sun Basin falls (now 2.1 m below the old marks: stranded cove, bare mud, dry mill race). The difference in level (head) between the basins is now 3.5 m.
- **Deadline.** Mid-Autumn night, thirty days from the start. At full moon the autumn typhoon rains are due; the Moon Basin will overtop the low bank above Mistwater Landing unless the gates are opened in staged steps to share the water. The same night the village votes on the Company's concrete dam plan.
- **The legend tools.** The "golden axe" is a bronze gate-lever with an axe-shaped head (a big pry-lever and ratchet); the "golden scissors" is a pair of bronze dividers and shear-blade gauges used to cut and set the gate's guide rails and to measure marks. They lie in the keepers' chest in the stone shrine on Elder Peak (each new keeper fetches them: the climb is the apprenticeship; Grandfather never took Shu up).
- **Engineering used (accurate, in-scene):** communicating vessels and level lines; water level by tube and by bamboo staff; measuring head; hydrostatic pressure (grows with depth: gate force on a 1.2 m x 1.0 m gate 3.5 m below the surface ~ 25 kN, about 2.5 tonnes of force); Torricelli (v = sqrt(2 g h)) and flow Q = A v; V-notch weir flow gauge; siphons and priming; levers and mechanical advantage, ratchets and compound pulleys (block and tackle); seized pins and thermal expansion/penetrating oil; silt and sediment (dredging a channel with a bamboo-and-rope scraper and a barge); a floating pontoon to lift a log; staged opening to avoid a surge wave and seiche (a basin sloshing: period T = 2L / sqrt(g d)); a small Pelton water-wheel microturbine and a pumped-storage "battery lake" (pump water up at night with spare power, run it down by day) as the compromise plan with the Company engineer; water budgets (rainfall x catchment area).

## Cast (fixed looks; each has distinct skin, hair, main color, accessory, footwear, height)
- **Shu Tsai (Tsai Shu-ying), 16.** Hero, apprentice gatekeeper. Light warm-ivory skin, long straight black hair in one thick braid down her back with a red cord, indigo cotton work jacket with rolled sleeves, khaki trousers rolled to the calf, green rubber rain boots, a bamboo-handled brass plumb bob on a cord around her neck. Normal height (about 160 cm), taller than A-Bao, shorter than Han. Careful, dry, funny, stubborn; afraid of being the one who breaks what her family kept. Want: save the lake. Need: understand that keeping something means being willing to change it with care.
- **A-gong (Grandfather Tsai), 74.** The gatekeeper. Pale tan skin, white hair thin on top, a short white beard, a faded grey padded vest over a white undershirt, a straw hat, a bamboo cane, black cloth shoes. Gentle, laconic, tender; carries guilt for the gates. Lame in the left leg since the flood.
- **Liu Han, 17.** Ferry boy and a swimmer, Shu's friend then more. Tanned golden-brown skin, short messy black hair, tall and wiry, orange life vest over a white t-shirt, rolled grey trousers, black water shoes, a whistle on a lanyard. The tallest teen. Cheerful, brave, tells terrible jokes when nervous, afraid of nothing except being left out. Runs the ferry named "Magpie".
- **A-Bao (Tsai Bao), 11.** Shu's little brother. Fair skin, round face, bowl-cut black hair, a bright yellow raincoat, red sandals, a tiny duckling named Dumpling in a bamboo basket. Collects facts and snacks, names everything. Shortest.
- **Engineer Ouyang Min, 29.** From the lowland Taihe Power Company. Fair cool skin, silver-framed glasses, black hair in a high ponytail, a crisp white shirt under a yellow hard hat, grey trousers, white sneakers, a roll of plans under her arm. Brilliant, precise, honest; arrives to sell a concrete dam; becomes an ally and the book's bridge between old and new. Tall.
- **Granny Hua (A-ma Hua), 82.** Tea-terrace matriarch and the lake's storyteller. Tan skin, silver hair in a bun with a jade pin, a plum-colored quilted jacket, wide black trousers, embroidered slippers, a carved walking stick; tiny; sharp; knows every lake story and the real dates. Her late husband was the previous keeper's friend.
- **Aunt Meng, 50.** Runs the dock food stall (scallion pancakes, fish ball soup, taro balls). Warm tan skin, round, big laugh, hair in a net, a red apron, rubber slippers. Gossip and comfort.
- **Uncle Liu, 48.** Han's father: owns the ferry and the village forge. Burly, brown skin, shaved head, a scorched leather apron over a grey undershirt, heavy rubber boots, thick forearms. Quiet, kind, practical; once rowed through the flood night and has never forgotten who closed the gate.
- **Elder Chen, 60.** Village head and tea merchant, torn between the money and the lake. Pale skin, grey crew cut, a brown wool vest, glasses on a chain.
- **The dragons.** Never alive: carved stone gate-heads with bronze eyes; the legend retold by Granny Hua.

## Places
Mistwater Landing (docks, Aunt Meng's stall, the ferry pier, Shu's family house with a tin roof and a courtyard of drying tea); the Sun Basin cove; the Moon Basin shore and the low bank; Lantern Island with the Lantern Temple and the Dragon Gates in a stone channel; the tea terraces; the mill (water-wheel, dry); Elder Peak (camphor forest, bamboo grove, a stone shrine with the keepers' chest); the Company's survey camp.

## Arc (25 chapters)
Part One, THE SUN GOES OUT (1-5): the Sun Basin drains, Shu's first measurements, the legend, Ouyang Min arrives with the dam plan, the thirty-day deadline.
Part Two, THE TWO DRAGONS (6-10): levels, tubes and staffs; mapping the lake; diving to the gates with Han; seeing both dragons jammed; the silt and the log; A-gong's silence.
Part Three, THE GOLDEN AXE (11-15): the climb up Elder Peak; the camphor forest; a storm that strands Ouyang's survey team and Shu helps; the shrine and the chest; the logbook of a hundred and fifty years of levels; the midpoint plan.
Part Four, THE OLD KEEPER (16-20): A-gong's secret (the flood night, the lever pin, why he stopped), fabricating a new pin, a hoist and a scraper; Ouyang changes sides with a compromise design (microturbine and pumped storage); the practice open; the typhoon forecast.
Part Five, THE MID-AUTUMN NIGHT (21-25): the staged opening in the rain; the seized pin and the drowned log solved by ingenuity (siphon, pontoon, tackle); the surge managed; the vote; moonrise on a full lake; A-gong hands over the plumb bob; a lantern night and a quiet end.

## Themes and motifs
Water finds its level; old and new knowledge as one language; the plumb bob; the Gatekeeper's Rules (epigraph at the start of each chapter); blue magpies; the duckling Dumpling; red lanterns; mist; listening to the lake; keeping and changing.

## Things to avoid
Horror, gore, death of a main character, villain cartoons (the Company is people with a real problem), preachiness, long lectures, brand names, modern slang, real Thao rituals, names or sacred places, invented foreign words, stereotypes. Romance no more than a hand-hold and one kiss near the end. No guns.
