# PART ONE: THE SUN GOES OUT

## 1. The Carp in the Mud
Synopsis: On day one of thirty, Shu discovers that the east half of Two-Light Lake has drained away overnight, and measures how far.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 1: Water always finds its level, and it is never impatient about it.*
1. Open on Shu in her green rain boots in the middle of what was a fishing cove in the Sun Basin, a bamboo pole upright in the mud, the bright shining lake pulled back a hundred meters like a tablecloth; forty-one carp stranded in puddles; her little brother A-Bao in his yellow raincoat with the duckling Dumpling in a basket, asking what carp eat when there is no lake.
2. Shu reads the old keeper's mark carved on the cove's stone steps and measures the new water line against it with her plumb bob and a folding rule: the lake is 2.1 meters below the mark; she explains to A-Bao, without lecturing, why a lake never just "goes away"; the mist is burning off and the camphor-covered ridges stand out like folded green paper.
3. They carry buckets of carp across the mud to the deep channel, with Aunt Meng from the dock stall arriving with a net and a great deal of opinion about the weather; the whole of Mistwater Landing starts to gather on the bare lakebed, whispering that the lake has swallowed the sun.
4. Shu looks west across Lantern Island to the Moon Basin and notices it looks too high, its water lapping at the stone wall below the Lantern Temple steps; a thought she cannot yet name: one half full, one half empty; her grandfather, A-gong, appears on the shore with his cane and his straw hat, looks at the mud, and says only "Go home and eat."
5. At home in the courtyard with drying tea leaves, A-gong sits very still and refuses to explain; Shu writes 2.1 m in her notebook, then underlines it twice; behind the old keeper's chair hangs the brass key on its cord that she has never seen him open anything with.

## 2. Aunt Meng's Weather Report
Synopsis: Shu seeks answers at the dock and meets Han, who rows her to Lantern Island to see the Dragon Gates up close.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 2: Write it down, and then write down how you know it.*
1. Next morning the dock is a parliament: Aunt Meng's stall steaming with scallion pancakes and fish ball soup, fishermen arguing about the end of the world, a ferry that cannot dock because the pier now stands over mud; Shu eats a pancake and listens, collecting theories: dragons, the dam company, the plum rain.
2. Liu Han, seventeen, orange life vest, whistle on a lanyard, steers the ferry Magpie into the one deep channel and tells terrible jokes; he and Shu have known each other forever; he offers her a ride across the Moon Basin if she holds the rope and stops frowning.
3. From the boat they see the Moon Basin high against its banks: brown, swollen, restless; Han says it was a hand's width higher this week; Shu measures with the ferry's painted depth marks and discovers the gap between the two halves of the lake is larger than yesterday's guess.
4. They land on Lantern Island; the Lantern Temple's red lanterns hang in the mist; behind the temple, in a mossy stone channel, the two carved dragon gates face each other like old arguers: the Sun Dragon and the Moon Dragon, bronze eyes green with age; Shu has seen them all her life and never once looked at the machinery.
5. She lays a hand on the cold stone and feels no vibration at all: no water moving through the channel. Han says everybody knows the gates are decorative. Shu says nobody builds a carved gate to be decorative. A bell rings across the water: Granny Hua is going to tell the dragon story tonight.

## 3. Granny Hua's Dragons
Synopsis: At the Lantern Temple, eighty-two-year-old Granny Hua tells the legend of the two dragons, the sun and the moon, and the golden axe and scissors.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 3: A story is a map that somebody walked.*
1. Evening: the whole village on the temple steps with paper cups of tea and taro balls; Granny Hua in her plum jacket with a jade pin and carved stick takes her stool by the lantern; A-Bao sits at her feet with Dumpling; A-gong stands at the back with his hat in his hands.
2. She tells the old tale: the sun and moon vanished one day, a young couple went looking, found two dragons playing with them at the bottom of the lake; an old man enslaved by the dragons told them that a golden axe and golden scissors were buried on Elder Peak and could free them.
3. The couple climbed for seven days through camphor and mist, found the shrine, took the axe and scissors, came down, and cut the dragons loose; the sun and moon rose, the lake took the shape of both; Granny Hua says that stories are made of what people were afraid to forget.
4. Shu asks Granny Hua, politely and stubbornly, what the dragons really were, and who the old man enslaved by them could have been; the village laughs; Granny Hua does not: she looks past Shu toward A-gong at the back and says "Ask someone who has held the key."
5. A-gong has gone. Shu finds him alone on the dock facing the empty cove, and he tells her one true thing: the gates are not decorative, they were the most important thing in the valley, and he cannot open them. He will not say why. Han's ferry whistle sounds across the dark water.

## 4. The Woman with the Plans
Synopsis: An engineer from the lowland Taihe Power Company arrives with a plan to replace the lake's old system with a concrete dam, and the village sets a vote for Mid-Autumn night.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 4: Ask who built it before you ask who will rebuild it.*
1. A white ferry of strangers: Engineer Ouyang Min, in a yellow hard hat, silver glasses and a white shirt, steps over the mud onto a plank walkway with a roll of plans and two surveyors; Aunt Meng whispers that she is "very tall and very efficient"; Shu decides to dislike her at once and fails.
2. In the Lantern Temple hall, Elder Chen hosts the meeting; Ouyang unrolls her plan: a concrete dam and spillway at the narrows to hold one steady lake level all year, a small power station and a new road; she says it politely, with real numbers, and the village leans forward.
3. Shu challenges one number: the plan says the lake's catchment is a certain size; she asks whether the plan counts the Moon Basin's high water; Ouyang pauses, makes a note, and says "Good question"; Shu is annoyed to find that she likes being taken seriously.
4. Elder Chen announces the vote: on Mid-Autumn night, thirty days from the first dry morning (twenty-nine now), the village will choose; Granny Hua taps her stick twice on the floor: "Thirty days is long enough for a lake to change its mind."
5. After the meeting Ouyang finds Shu on the steps and says she has read the old regional records and cannot find any mention of what the Dragon Gates were for; Shu says that is because nobody wrote it down in a place where engineers look; Ouyang says "Then show me." Shu, surprised, says she will.

## 5. Twenty-Nine Days
Synopsis: Shu resolves to measure the whole lake before the vote, and her friends sign on.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 5: A deadline is only a measurement of what you have not yet done.*
1. Night at home: Shu lays out a paper calendar on the courtyard table with twenty-nine red lanterns drawn and begins a list: how high, how low, how fast, why; A-Bao adds "who," "what are carp eating," and "snacks"; A-gong watches from the door and goes to bed.
2. At dawn Shu finds Han and asks whether he will lend her the ferry and two arms and some bad jokes; he says the ferry is his father's, but his arms and jokes are free; they stand on the dock making a plan: tubes, poles, ropes, markers, a sounding line.
3. Uncle Liu, Han's father, a burly man with a scorched leather apron, hears the plan from the forge door, says nothing for a long time, and then hands Shu a coil of clear plastic tubing from his shelf; "Your grandfather once borrowed this," he says. "He never brought it back." The way he says it makes Shu look twice.
4. Aunt Meng packs rice balls; Granny Hua sends a message by A-Bao: "Measure first. Believe later."; the Magpie leaves the dock with Shu, Han, A-Bao and a duckling aboard, the mist lifting off the water like a sheet being pulled from a piece of furniture.
5. Shu holds her brass plumb bob over the side and watches it swing and settle: the one thing in the world, her grandfather taught her, that always tells the truth about down. She says "Day two," and writes the first measurement in the book.

# PART TWO: THE TWO DRAGONS

## 6. A Level Made of Water
Synopsis: Shu builds a water level from clear tubing and bamboo staffs and discovers just how uneven the lake is.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 6: Water in a tube cannot lie about level.*
1. Shu shows A-Bao how to make a level: fill a long clear tube with water (no air bubbles), hold the two ends against two bamboo staffs, and the water in each end will always stand at the same height no matter how the tube bends; A-Bao tests it, delighted, and tries to prove it wrong with a tube tied in a knot.
2. They set the staffs on opposite sides of the Sun Basin's bare mud and read the difference: she marks heights in centimeters on a bamboo staff with her pencil and an old ruler; Han asks why not use a laser; Shu says she does not own a laser and has opinions about people who do.
3. They carry the tube across Lantern Island and compare the Sun Basin's surface to the Moon Basin's: the difference is three and a half meters; Han whistles; Shu does the arithmetic twice and writes: HEAD = 3.5 m; water wants to fall that far and cannot.
4. Shu explains to A-Bao why a three-and-a-half-meter difference is like a hand pushing very hard on a very strong door: the deeper the water, the harder it pushes; the gates are holding back a huge force; she does the first simple pressure calculation and is startled at the answer.
5. At dusk Ouyang's survey team on the far shore waves; Ouyang walks over, looks at the tube and the staffs, and says, "That is a better level than the one my people brought." She asks to see the numbers. Shu hesitates, then shows her. They compare the Moon Basin reading; Ouyang says nothing for a long moment and writes it into her own notebook.

## 7. Bamboo Soundings
Synopsis: With a weighted knotted rope and a ferry, Shu and Han map the Sun Basin's bed and find the old channel mouth.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 7: A map is a promise about what is under the surface.*
1. Han's ferry, a weighted rope with a knot every half meter, a bamboo pole and a paper grid on a clipboard; A-Bao is the scribe; they cross the Sun Basin in straight lines like a farmer plowing a field, dropping the rope every ten meters and calling the depth.
2. Han keeps the line straight by lining up a red lantern on the temple with a camphor tree on the ridge, and he turns out to be much better at this than he lets on; Shu notices that he is quietly brilliant and says nothing; he notices that she noticed.
3. As the contour lines grow on the paper, a pattern appears: the Sun Basin is a shallow bowl with a long ancient trench running toward Lantern Island; A-Bao names it "the Dragon's Hallway"; Shu realizes that it must be the old channel bed that once carried water from the island.
4. Dumpling the duckling escapes the basket and paddles around the drained cove; the whole boat is occupied for a quarter of an hour recovering a small yellow bird; the ferry drifts; in the drift Shu measures the current for the first time and finds there is none at all.
5. Aunt Meng's pancakes at the dock, the map pinned to the stall wall with chopsticks; fishermen crowd round; an old man points at the trench and says his grandfather said the lake used to "breathe through the island." Shu writes it down.

## 8. Under the Island
Synopsis: Han dives with a safety line to the Sun Dragon's mouth, and the friends see the gate for the first time.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 8: Never go where the water has not been asked.*
1. Shu insists on rules before any dive: a line, a whistle code, a depth limit of three meters, and her standing in the boat holding the line; Han agrees to every rule with a salute and a joke; A-Bao is appointed Official Whistle Counter.
2. Han slips over the side in a wet suit that is far too big, with a snorkel, a lantern in a glass jar, and a rope; the water is cold and green; Shu counts the seconds; the line pays out.
3. Han returns gasping and delighted: the Sun Dragon's mouth is a stone arch under two meters of water, its carved head half buried in silt, its bronze eyes green with age, and across the arch is a bronze-bound wooden gate jammed shut with a gray silt plug and what looks like a tree trunk.
4. They make a second dive together with Shu in a borrowed mask: she sees the dragon's jaw, the vertical lever socket, a sheared bronze stub where a pin ought to be; she touches the stub and feels every year of its stillness; she surfaces with tears in her mask that she blames on lake water.
5. A-gong is waiting on the dock when the Magpie returns, leaning on his cane, white with a fear that has nothing to do with the cold; he says only: "You went under the island." He turns away and she hears him say, to nobody, "I told them not to ask the lake."

## 9. The Weir and the Flow
Synopsis: Shu builds a weir to measure flow, argues respectfully with Ouyang over methods, and learns the difference between a number and a measurement.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 9: A number without its method is a rumor.*
1. At the dry mill race below the Sun Basin, Shu and Han build a simple V-notch weir from planks and a sheet of tin so that water flowing through the notch will tell them how much is moving; the dribble from the sun basin's one remaining outlet is almost nothing.
2. Ouyang's surveyors arrive with a flow meter on a pole and a tablet; the numbers disagree with Shu's weir by a factor of two; they argue pleasantly about method; Shu insists on measuring the same thing three times; Ouyang says that is exactly right and wishes her own team did.
3. They find the error: Ouyang measured the flow at the middle of the channel where it is fastest, and Shu measured it over the notch; they reconcile with a simple correction; Ouyang laughs for the first time; Han is startled that an adult engineer admits to being wrong in public.
4. Ouyang explains how a power station turns a drop of water into electricity (a falling stream turns a wheel; the faster and higher the fall, the more energy); Shu asks how much the lake's head of three and a half meters could make; Ouyang says "enough to light Mistwater Landing, if the gates worked."
5. As the sun lowers, Ouyang looks at the lake and says quietly that her company thinks of the lake as a problem to be held still; Shu says the lake has been moving for a thousand years and was not asking to be held; Ouyang writes that down too.

## 10. A-gong's Silence
Synopsis: Shu pushes her grandfather for the truth and finds the thread that points her up Elder Peak.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 10: Some silences are only locked doors, and a door has a key.*
1. At dinner A-Bao announces that the Sun Dragon has a sheared pin, that he has named it Stubby, and that Dumpling has laid claim to a puddle; A-gong's chopsticks stop moving; Shu's mother (away at the tea market) is spoken of; the quiet in the room is a measurement of its own.
2. Later Shu finds her grandfather in the small family altar room, looking at an old photograph of three men standing on the island in front of the gates; one is A-gong thirty years younger, one is Han's father as a boy, one is a tall man she does not know.
3. She asks about the photograph, the pin, the key. A-gong says that the gatekeeper's chest is on Elder Peak, that keepers climb to fetch their tools at the start of their service, and that he never took her because he hoped she would never need to; the family has had no real keeper for thirty years.
4. Granny Hua arrives with her carved stick and a bag of oranges, says that old people tell the truth the way a tea terrace gives its tea, slowly, and leaves behind the information that the climb to the shrine takes two days and the camphor forest is the hard part; she looks at A-gong and he looks at the floor.
5. Late that night Shu stands in the courtyard under a sky of cold stars and decides: she will climb Elder Peak, fetch the golden axe and scissors, and open the gates herself; her grandfather's cane taps on the floor of the house behind her, once, and stops.

# PART THREE: THE GOLDEN AXE

## 11. The Trail to Elder Peak
Synopsis: Shu, Han and A-Bao set out on the climb, learning how a path is a kind of engineering.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 11: A path is a promise that the ground will hold.*
1. Before dawn: packs of rice balls, boiled eggs and tea; Aunt Meng's blessing; A-gong gives Shu his cane and says nothing, then adds the one practical instruction he has: "Keep your feet where the stones are older than the moss."
2. The trail through the tea terraces: Shu notices how the terraces are stepped to slow the water, each stone wall a tiny dam; A-Bao counts the walls and arrives at an absurd number; Han carries most of the weight and complains with great artistry.
3. Blue magpies with long blue tails flash through the bamboo; the air smells of rain and crushed leaves; Shu uses her plumb bob and the folding rule to measure the slope of the path and works out, to Han's alarm, how many hours of climbing remain.
4. At the first rest shelter they meet Ouyang's survey team heading the same way, studying the mountain streams to calculate the lake's inflow; Ouyang offers a map; Shu hesitates, then accepts; the two parties decide, reluctantly and mutually, to walk the same trail without being friends.
5. By late morning the terraces end and the camphor forest begins: a green dimness, tall trunks wrapped in moss, a thin white mist; A-Bao falls silent for the first time in his life; Han whispers, "I think the mountain is listening," and nobody tells him he is being silly.

## 12. The Camphor Forest
Synopsis: In the mist of the old forest the group learns to navigate by contour and by water.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 12: When you are lost, follow the water down; when you are found, follow it up.*
1. The path vanishes into roots and ferns; Shu reads Ouyang's contour map, matches a steep ridge to its tightly packed lines, and realizes that where lines are close, the slope is steep; she teaches A-Bao the idea by drawing contours in the mud with a stick.
2. Han climbs a rock to look for the trail and finds instead a stream they must cross; they test the depth with a pole and decide where to wade; Shu insists that they rope together; Han mutters and then does it.
3. A-Bao eats the last rice ball too early and discovers hunger as a concept; the group shares a flask of tea on a mossy log; Dumpling is allowed out to swim in a puddle and returns with a leech; a short, comic scene of leech removal with salt.
4. Evening mist: Shu uses her plumb bob and the angle of the sun through the canopy to estimate the time; they hear water ahead and follow it uphill; Ouyang's team has gone a different way; they are alone and a little afraid.
5. They reach a small stone cairn marked with a faded red cloth, the keepers' trail marker; Shu touches it and recognizes the same knot her grandfather ties on the lake's old rope; the cairn leads toward a roaring sound: a mountain stream swollen by the afternoon rain.

## 13. The Storm on the Ridge
Synopsis: A sudden storm strands Ouyang's team on the far side of a flooded stream, and Shu uses a rope and block and tackle to bring them across.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 13: Strength is borrowed from the shape of the tool.*
1. Thunder: the stream is waist-deep and loud; on the far bank Ouyang and two surveyors stand with their packs and a stranded tablet; they have been trying to cross and have been turned back; the bridge of two fallen logs is under foam.
2. Shu thinks fast: they have a long rope, three pulleys from Han's ferry kit and two strong trees; she rigs a block and tackle to make a rope-way, explaining mechanical advantage in four short sentences as she works; A-Bao is the knot inspector.
3. Han crosses first on the rope with a harness of knots, fighting the current, with his whistle blowing for courage; he secures the far end; a basket on a pulley carries the packs first, then the surveyors one by one.
4. Ouyang crosses last, white-faced, and on the bank she is completely silent for a minute; then she says, "I have designed bridges for ten years and have never been so glad of a rope." She shakes Shu's hand formally, and then helps her pull down the rope-way.
5. The storm passes; the whole party, now one party, makes camp in a stone overhang; Ouyang looks at Shu across the fire and says "I was wrong about one thing: I thought you were just a girl who did not want a dam." Shu says, "I was wrong about the same thing: I thought you were just a dam."

## 14. The Shrine of the Keepers
Synopsis: At the top of the climb they find the stone shrine and the keepers' chest, and the legend's golden tools.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 14: Gold is only what a tool looks like after a hundred years of the right hands.*
1. Dawn on the ridge: the mist breaks and the whole lake is spread below them, the sun shape and the moon shape, bronze in the morning, so clear and small that Shu laughs; Han says "It looks like a map somebody drew while happy."
2. The shrine: a low stone hut with a slate roof and a bronze bell, moss-covered, with a carved dragon over the door; inside, an altar, a dry bundle of incense, and a heavy iron-bound chest; Shu takes A-gong's brass key from her pocket where he slipped it that last night; it fits.
3. In the chest, wrapped in oiled cloth: a great bronze lever with an axe-shaped head and a ratchet cog, heavy as a small anvil; a pair of long bronze dividers with shear-blade tips, which A-Bao calls "the scissors"; both polished by use until they glow like honey; the legend's gold is real, and made of work.
4. Under them, a thick logbook bound in oilcloth, and a folded note in an old keeper's hand: OPEN IN STEPS. NEVER ALL AT ONCE. WATCH THE LAKE BREATHE. Ouyang, reading over Shu's shoulder, says very quietly, "That is a surge-control instruction. That is a hundred and fifty years old."
5. They sit among the incense smoke and eat the last boiled egg; A-Bao asks why a lake needs a keeper at all; Shu does not have an answer, but she realizes she has been asking the question wrong: not "why does it need a keeper" but "what did the keepers know."

## 15. A Hundred and Fifty Years of Water
Synopsis: Reading the keepers' logbook, Shu discovers the rule that held the lake in balance for a century and a half, and the midpoint plan forms.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 15: A record kept faithfully is a gift to a stranger.*
1. By lantern light in the shrine they read: daily entries in a dozen hands, lake levels in both basins, rain, temperature, gate settings, the date each year the mist first thickened; the handwriting shifts from brush to pen to ballpoint; A-Bao finds an entry in which a keeper has drawn a duck.
2. Ouyang, with her tablet and Shu's pencil, plots the century and a half of levels together on one chart; the lines are two intertwined rivers that never drift more than thirty centimeters apart until thirty years ago, when they split like a fork and never came back.
3. They compute the water budget of the lake from the logbook: how much rain falls, how much the streams carry, how much leaves; Shu and Ouyang trade formulas and corrections like two people playing a game they both love; Han and A-Bao keep score on a stone.
4. The last full entry before the split is in A-gong's handwriting, very young and very steady; the page after it is blank for twelve years, then a few thin lines in a hand Shu does not recognize; the next to last line says only: THE PIN BROKE. I AM SORRY. Shu closes the book.
5. The midpoint plan, spoken at the fire: go down; fabricate a new pin; clear the channel; test the lever at low head; then open the gates in steps, with the lake's own logbook as the schedule; Ouyang says she will run the numbers for the staged opening; Han says "And I will bring the jokes," and for once nobody laughs, but everybody smiles.

# PART FOUR: THE OLD KEEPER

## 16. Down the Mountain
Synopsis: The climbers return with the tools, and A-gong sees the axe-lever and must finally explain.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 16: The way down is also part of the climb.*
1. The long descent: the group moves slower, carrying the heavy lever on a bamboo pole between Han and Shu; Ouyang offers to take a turn and is allowed; A-Bao carries Dumpling and the dividers and sings a song about snacks.
2. Mistwater Landing in the afternoon: villagers waiting on the dock, a hush when they see the bronze axe-lever; Granny Hua taps her stick and says, "So. The old man's story was true," with a look of great satisfaction; Aunt Meng bursts into tears and feeds everyone.
3. Elder Chen draws Shu aside and says, with an apologetic cough, that he has been offered a large sum for the village's water rights and does not know what to do; he asks her whether the gates can really be opened; she says she does not know yet, and that is exactly why she is going to measure.
4. In the courtyard, A-gong stands up slowly from his chair when he sees the lever; he touches the bronze axe-head with trembling fingers; he looks at Shu and says "You climbed it." She says yes. He says, "I should have taken you." She says "Tell me about the night of the flood."
5. A-gong sits, A-Bao pours tea, Ouyang and Han hover at the gate, and the old keeper says "All right. All right. Sit down, all of you." The lanterns hum. Behind him the plumb bob on the wall swings to a stop.

## 17. The Night of the Great Flood
Synopsis: A-gong tells how, thirty years ago, he closed the gates in a flood to save the lower village and how the pin snapped.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 17: The kindest thing a keeper can do is to tell the lake the truth.*
1. A-gong tells it plainly: thirty years ago, after four days of typhoon rain, the Moon Basin rose in the night; the lower village was in danger, and the keepers' rule said to open the gates in steps; but the Sun Basin's outlet was blocked by a fallen tree and the surge would have flooded the farmland below.
2. He chose to close the gates entirely and let the Moon Basin rise a little to protect the fields; in the dark, with the ferry Magpie's lamp bobbing on the water, he hauled on the lever, and the bronze pin sheared with a sound like a bell; the gate stuck half shut and a drowned log jammed the channel.
3. Uncle Liu, then a young man, had been out in the ferry rescuing a family from the water and nearly capsized in the surge; the gate stopped moving just in time to calm the wave; nobody died; the village was safe; but A-gong never forgave himself for what he had broken.
4. He stopped climbing the mountain; he stopped teaching; he told himself the gates were better closed than broken again; the lake slowly changed its levels and nobody noticed; the family carried on being the gatekeepers in name only.
5. Shu kneels in front of him and says, in her plainest voice: "You did not break the lake. You saved a village and a pin broke. A pin can be made." A-gong looks at her for a long time and, for the first time in thirty years, laughs, a small cracked sound, and says "Make a better one."

## 18. A New Pin
Synopsis: Uncle Liu's forge and Ouyang's steel make a new pin, and everyone learns how to make a thing strong.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 18: A thing is only as strong as the part you did not think about.*
1. At the forge: Uncle Liu in his leather apron and a small fire the color of a mango; Shu brings the sheared bronze stub, Ouyang the steel bar from her camp; Han pumps the bellows; A-Bao is assigned to stand very far back and announce the time.
2. Shu calculates the load on the pin: the gate's weight, the water pressure at 3.5 meters of head, the lever's mechanical advantage; Ouyang checks it; the pin must resist a shear force of several tonnes; they choose a steel core in a bronze sleeve, which will not rust and will not shear.
3. Uncle Liu shapes the steel, quenches it with a great hiss of steam, and tests it by hanging a sack of rice and then sacks of bricks; the pin holds; he says, "I should have made one for your grandfather thirty years ago. He would not let me."
4. In the evening A-gong comes to the forge on his cane and examines the pin; he does not say a word; he runs his thumb along it, nods once, and asks Uncle Liu for a cup of tea; the two old friends sit together in the firelight without needing to speak.
5. Late at night, with the new pin wrapped in oilcloth on the table, Shu and Ouyang sit on the dock with their feet over the mud; Ouyang says she called her company: they were not pleased; Shu says "What did you say?" and Ouyang says "That I want to design a better answer." They watch the moon, small and yellow, thicken.

## 19. The Company's Better Idea
Synopsis: Ouyang proposes a compromise: use the lake's own two basins as a pumped-storage battery, with the gates as the heart, and no concrete dam.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 19: The best machine is the one that learns from the old one.*
1. In the temple hall the whole village squeezes in while Ouyang stands before her old plan, folds it in half, and unrolls a new one drawn in pencil overnight: keep the Dragon Gates, add a small microturbine in the channel, and use spare night power to pump water from the Sun Basin up into the Moon Basin and run it back down by day.
2. Shu helps her explain the idea to the village with two buckets, a hose and a small pump: water pumped up stores energy, water running down gives it back; they calculate the energy, E equals m g h, and the whole hall hushes at how much a lake holds.
3. Elder Chen asks whether the water level would still go up and down; Ouyang says by about a hand's width, within the old keepers' mark; she shows it on Shu's own chart; there is a murmur: this is the first plan that keeps the lake looking like itself.
4. A voice from the back: a tea merchant says the concrete dam would be cheaper; Ouyang says yes, it would, and that cheap is not a reason to forget who the lake belongs to; Granny Hua taps twice and says "She is learning."
5. After the meeting Han tells Shu quietly that he has decided he wants to learn how to run a turbine; Shu says "You would be good at it," meaning it, and he flushes red to the ears; A-gong, outside in the dark, listening to everything, quietly smiles.

## 20. The Practice Open
Synopsis: They test the lever, the new pin and the staged opening at low head, and then the typhoon forecast arrives.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 20: Rehearse in the shallows; the deep will not wait.*
1. On Lantern Island at dawn: the new pin slides into the Moon Dragon's lever socket; the bronze axe-lever fits into the cog; Shu, Han and Uncle Liu pull together; nothing moves; Shu realizes the channel is packed with silt and a drowned log; she asks for a siphon.
2. Han dives again with a hose; they prime a siphon from the Moon Basin over the low island wall into the Sun Basin to equalize the pressure on the gate (a little water begins to flow, silently, over a stone lip), and the gate loosens by a finger's breadth; a plume of brown silt boils up and the water begins to speak.
3. They test the staged opening: five centimeters; a pause; ten; each time they measure how the water moves, how long the basin takes to settle, how the surface sloshes back and forth in a slow rhythm; Shu calculates the seiche period and realizes they must wait for each wave to pass before the next step.
4. The first real flow in thirty years: a gentle sheet of water slides through the Dragon's mouth; Aunt Meng bangs a pot, the fishermen cheer; Granny Hua weeps quietly; A-gong sits on a rock by the channel with his eyes closed, listening, and says "Ah. There she is."
5. Ouyang's phone buzzes with the weather service: A typhoon is due to arrive on Mid-Autumn night with heavy rain over the mountain; ten days of rain in one; the Moon Basin will rise by a meter and a half in six hours; the gates must be fully open by moonrise or the lower village floods. Shu holds the plumb bob and feels the string go quite still.

# PART FIVE: THE MID-AUTUMN NIGHT

## 21. Day Thirty
Synopsis: The village prepares for the storm and the staged opening on the festival night.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 21: Prepare for the worst, and then do the next small thing.*
1. Dawn: low gray clouds, a warm wind, mooncakes on every doorstep; the village moves everything of value to the high ground; Aunt Meng sets up a soup tent at the temple; Granny Hua takes the best seat by the Lantern Temple door with her stick and a lantern the color of a plum.
2. Shu lays out the plan on the temple floor in chalk: five steps of opening for each gate, a lookout at every pier, signal lanterns, a whistle code, Han in the ferry as a safety boat, A-Bao as the runner, Ouyang on the water gauge, Uncle Liu on the lever, A-gong at the Sun Dragon where the water first comes through.
3. A-Bao is deeply honored with his title of Official Runner and promptly forgets his job for a quarter of an hour in a dispute with Dumpling; Han and Shu share a quiet moment on the dock, with an umbrella between them and a plain bun in his hand; he says, "Whatever happens tonight, you did all the hard parts."
4. Rain begins at four in the afternoon, soft, then not soft; the Moon Basin creeps up its bank an inch every ten minutes; Shu watches the water-level staff: the numbers climb; her plumb bob steadies; she says "Everyone to their places."
5. At dusk the mist thickens and the full moon, invisible behind clouds, is a pale glow; the temple lanterns are lit, red and warm in the rain; the village stands at the water's edge in coats and raincoats, silent; Shu takes her grandfather's hand and says, "Ready, A-gong?" He says "Ready, keeper."

## 22. Staged Opening
Synopsis: The first four steps open smoothly, and then the Moon Dragon jams.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 22: Open in steps, and listen between them.*
1. Step one: five centimeters; Uncle Liu hauls the lever; the gate rises; water slides through; Ouyang calls the level from the staff; Shu waits until the lake's slow sloshing has gone quiet before saying "Step two."
2. Steps two and three go cleanly; the wind picks up; rain drums on the temple roof; the Sun Basin begins to rise visibly in the dark; fish leap in the lantern light; Han's whistle signals the pier crews; everyone grows calmer with each step.
3. Step four: at twenty centimeters the Moon Dragon's gate stops with a groan; Uncle Liu strains; Shu feels the lever bind through the soles of her boots; the gauge shows the Moon Basin climbing again; the typhoon is not waiting.
4. Han dives with a line and a lamp and returns to say the drowned log, loosened by the first steps, has turned broadside in the channel and wedged against the gate's guide rail; it is a ton of old camphor wood, and it will not move.
5. Shu looks at the gauge, at the clock, at the rain, and then at her grandfather; he says in a perfectly quiet voice, "What does the tool want?" She closes her eyes, and thinks about every tool they have, and begins to draw in the wet chalk on the temple floor.

## 23. The Drowned Log
Synopsis: With a siphon, a pontoon and a block and tackle, the friends and the village free the log and open the gate.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 23: A hard problem is usually three easy problems standing close together.*
1. Shu's three-part plan: first, relieve the pressure on the gate by priming a second siphon from the Moon Basin over the island wall; second, float the log free by lashing empty oil drums to it as a pontoon; third, pull it out along the channel with a block and tackle anchored to the temple's stone pillars.
2. Aunt Meng's husband and a dozen fishermen carry drums through the rain; Han and Uncle Liu work in the cold water, lashing, with Ouyang calling instructions from the bank; a siphon hisses and starts, sending a rope of water through the dark; the gate trembles.
3. The block and tackle takes the strain: eight strands, a pull of eight to one; the entire village lines up on the rope like a tug-of-war team on a festival day, A-Bao at the end chanting "heave"; Granny Hua beats time with her stick; the log groans, and then slides.
4. The log shoots out of the channel and is caught in the net of the Magpie with a splash that drenches Ouyang; the gate lurches upward under Uncle Liu's lever; the Moon Dragon opens by a whole step and the Moon Basin gives a long, slow, grateful sigh.
5. Shu leans against the lever and finds that her hands are shaking only now; Han's hand finds hers on the cold bronze; A-gong, soaked, on the rock, is laughing and crying and saying "Again, again, from step five," and she says "Step five," and the night continues.

## 24. The Water Meets
Synopsis: The staged opening finishes at moonrise, the lake levels, and the village makes its decision.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 24: A good decision is made with the numbers in one hand and the people in the other.*
1. Steps five through ten: the gates open fully; the two basins find each other through the channel, and a long smooth swell rolls across the lake, slow and tall as a breath; the Moon Basin falls, the Sun Basin rises, and the two levels creep together toward the old keepers' mark.
2. The rain stops. The clouds tear open over the ridge and the full moon rises, enormous and orange, and for the first time in thirty years it sits in a lake that is whole; the whole village goes silent; then the temple bell rings, and the cheering starts.
3. In the temple hall, wet through, the village votes by a show of lanterns: the vote is for the keepers' plan and Ouyang's pumped-storage design, with the old gates at its heart; Elder Chen announces it with a voice that cracks; Ouyang bows to Shu, very low, in front of everybody.
4. Granny Hua stands, with her stick, and says her plain sentence: "The old knowledge and the new numbers were never two languages. They are one language, and it took a lake to make us hear it. Keep what was built with care. Ask the ones who live with it. That is all the law there is."
5. Shu is too tired to speak; Han drapes a coat over her; A-Bao falls asleep standing up, leaning on Dumpling's basket; A-gong rests his hand on her head and says, in front of everyone, "Keeper Tsai." The word sits in the air like a lantern lifting off the water.

## 25. Lantern Night
Synopsis: The lake is whole, the plumb bob changes hands, and a night of lanterns closes the story.
Header: *The Gatekeeper's Rules, Rule 25: The lake keeps nothing for itself. It gives its level to everything it touches.*
1. Morning after: the sun comes up over a lake with both halves full of light; the carp are back in the cove, forty-one of them and many more; Aunt Meng is selling scallion pancakes to a line that runs round the dock; the whole valley smells of rain and tea and fried onion.
2. A-gong takes the brass plumb bob from the wall and puts it, with a small bow, in Shu's hands, even though she already wears her own; "This one has seen every night of the lake," he says; she wears both, one on each side; Uncle Liu gives Han the ferry keys and a look of terrible pride.
3. Ouyang says goodbye at the ferry pier with a roll of final plans and a handshake that turns, halfway, into a hug; she promises to return in spring to build the microturbine with the villagers, not above them; A-Bao gives her Dumpling's first feather, which is not a feather, it is a bit of fluff.
4. That night the village floats red paper lanterns on both halves of the lake, hundreds, drifting toward the island; Han and Shu stand on the pier holding hands, with the whole sky reflected in the water; they watch the moon sit on the Moon Basin and the sun-colored lanterns gather in the Sun Basin; he says "I told no jokes tonight," and she says "I noticed," and kisses him quickly on the cheek.
5. Final image: Shu on the temple steps at midnight with her notebook, writing the day's measurement: Moon Basin and Sun Basin, level; head, zero; and underneath, in smaller letters, the last of the Gatekeeper's Rules, the one she has made up herself. She closes the book. The lake breathes. The lanterns drift.
