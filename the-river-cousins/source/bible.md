# THE RIVER COUSINS — story bible

Warm, funny, suspenseful summer adventure for ages 10-12, set in a farming village in the Korean countryside during the last rainy weeks of the summer school holiday. Twelve-year-old Jun, a city boy from a fourteenth-floor apartment in Seoul who knows everything a phone can tell him, is sent to spend three weeks with his grandparents and his eleven-year-old cousin Nari, a farm girl who knows everything a river can tell her. Each is sure they are right and the other is wrong. When the summer rains swell the river and the two are carried away in their grandfather's little wooden boat for a day and a night, being right turns out to matter much less than working together. No villain, no real cruelty: the stakes are a night on a river, a family's first red peppers of the year, and a cousin friendship. The book should make a reader smell wet earth and frying scallions, feel a warm cousin rivalry become real friendship, and want to try one new thing with someone very different from them.
IMPORTANT (user rule): the words "Korea", "Korean", "Chinese", "Japanese", "Taiwanese" must NOT appear in the title, subtitle, cover text, tagline or blurb, and should not appear in the story text at all. Culture comes through names, food, customs, places and language inside the story. Say "Seoul" naturally.

## Voice
THIRD PERSON, PAST TENSE. Chapters alternate close point of view: odd chapters are close on JUN (his phone, his lists, his city ideas, his loneliness, his fear of getting things wrong), even chapters are close on NARI (the river, the weather, her hands, her pride, her secret wish to see Seoul). The outline marks each chapter's POV. Warm, quick, funny, sensory (cicadas, wet earth, frying scallions, river smell, the sound of rain on a tin roof), like a very good read-aloud. Humor comes from the cousins' rivalry (Jun's app versus Nari's sky), Halmoni's "Aigoo!", Harabeoji's few dry words, and Jun's white sneakers meeting mud. Feelings show in hands, breath and small actions, not named and then explained. Short and long sentences mixed; the real emotion arrives plainly.
Every chapter opens with one italic line, on its own line, then a blank line, in this form: "*Jun's phone says: 32 degrees and sunny. Nari's river says: not yet.*" Both halves are short and change every chapter: what Jun's phone says (weather, battery, signal, a map) and what Nari's river (or sky) says (color of the water, sound, level, smell). Early chapters they disagree; later ones the two halves complete each other; the last chapter's line answers the first ("*Jun's phone says: home. Nari's river says: home.*"). The Header line is added by the script: never write it on any page.
Voice sample:
"*Jun's phone says: 32 degrees, sunny, arrival at 2:10. Nari's river says: not yet.*

On the fourteenth floor of a tall grey building in Seoul, Jun had lived for twelve years without once being asked to carry anything heavier than a backpack, and now his mother was zipping a suitcase the size of a small refrigerator.

"Three weeks," she said. "Your grandparents' house. Clean air. Ice-cold watermelon."

"I have hagwon," said Jun, which was the name for the after-school classes he went to every afternoon.

"Hagwon is closed for the summer break, and I checked," said his mother, who had checked."

## The premise
It is the first week of the summer holiday. Jun (12), an only child from a Seoul apartment whose parents work long hours, has never spent more than a weekend in the village. His mother packs him off by intercity bus for three weeks with his grandparents, Halmoni and Harabeoji, who live with Nari's family on a farm in a green river valley. Nari (11) is Jun's cousin (their fathers are brothers): a sunburnt, strong, quick farm girl who has never been to Seoul and does not plan to admit she wants to. She meets him at the bus stop with a wheelbarrow for his suitcase. They dislike each other at once, in the way cousins can: he thinks she is stubborn and stuck in the past; she thinks he is helpless and can only do things through a screen. Jun keeps a score sheet in his notebook of "who was right". The rainy season (jangma) arrives, three days early, and Jun's weather app is wrong and Nari's sky is right; Jun fixes Harabeoji's dead radio and Nari is not. The village meets in the hall on the night the river rises: the adults go to check the levee and the sluice gate at the lower fields; Halmoni goes to cook for them; the cousins are told to stay home. But Uncle's first red peppers of the year are drying in the far field across the river, under a tarp that has blown loose, and the boat, the Pear, is tied at the near bank. In a dry pause in the rain both decide, for once together, to cross in life vests to cover the peppers. On the far bank each ties the rope their own way, arguing, and half-ties both; a rush of water comes down from a dam upstream; the boat slips its rope with both cousins aboard and one oar is lost. They are swept down the river through dusk: Harabeoji's rule is "stay with the boat"; Jun's phone shows the bends and a sandbar; Nari reads the current; they steer to the sandbar and tie up with a knot made of both their knots. On the sandbar they eat Halmoni's packed rice rolls, talk about hagwon, loneliness, and a wish to see Seoul, and send one message on the last bar of signal. The rain returns before dawn, the sandbar shrinks, and they use a floorboard as a paddle (Jun's idea, Nari's technique), pass an old weir by a side channel and reach the Lower Landing, where a fisherman, Mr. Cho, boils them corn and calls the village. Uncle's truck brings them home; Halmoni cries and scolds in the same breath; Harabeoji says little. The peppers were saved by the neighbors. Harabeoji sits both cousins down: "A boat with two good rowers pulling different ways goes nowhere." Jun leaves at the end of the holiday; the cousins tear up the score sheet and write one line: "Us: 1".

## Cast (keep looks and habits EXACTLY consistent)
- **Jun**, 12: thin, neat, round black-framed glasses, short black hair with a side part, warm light skin (pale from indoors), a crisp white T-shirt with a small robot picture, light grey shorts, bright white sneakers (new and soon muddy), a black crossbody pouch for his phone; wheeled suitcase. Always checks his phone; keeps a small notebook with a score sheet; over-explains; kind underneath, lonely, afraid of getting things wrong. Habit: pushes his glasses up when he is thinking or embarrassed.
- **Nari**, 11: strong, sunburnt (tanned skin), one thick long black braid tied with a red rubber band, a faded sunflower-yellow T-shirt, dark green work trousers rolled to the knee, white rubber slip-on shoes, a straw hat hanging on her back on a string, scabbed knees. Reads the weather and the river; proud, quick, stubborn, funny; secretly wants to see Seoul (the tall buildings, the subway). Habit: sniffs the air and squints at the sky before she answers.
- **Halmoni** (Nari's and Jun's father's mother), 70s: small, round, short tightly curled grey hair (a perm), a bright floral loose top with matching baggy trousers, a purple sun visor, rubber slippers; cooks everything, says "Aigoo!" at everything, loves loudly. Refrain: "Eat first. Talk later."
- **Harabeoji** (grandfather), 70s: thin, weathered, a faded green baseball cap, a striped short-sleeved shirt, grey trousers, tall black rubber boots, a thin white moustache; says few words; keeps the river rules. Refrain: "Stay with the boat."
- **Uncle** (Nari's father, Jun's father's older brother), 40s: broad, quiet, a straw hat, a navy work shirt, muddy trousers, rubber boots, drives a small blue farm truck. **Auntie** (Nari's mother), 40s: tall, brisk, hair tied back with a scarf, a lilac work apron.
- **Mr. Cho**, the fisherman at the Lower Landing: round, cheerful, a wide-brimmed fishing hat, a vest with many pockets, waders; boils corn.
- Places: Pine Ford Village (a hundred houses in a green valley; tin and tile roofs; rice paddies, corn, sweet potatoes, red peppers; the village hall; a bus stop with a rusty shelter); the Long River (wide, brown-green, sandbars, stepping stones at the ford, an old weir downstream); the farmhouse (low, with a wooden porch (maru), a yard where peppers dry on straw mats, a tin-roofed shed, a pump); the boat called the Pear (a small flat-bottomed wooden boat, faded pear-green paint, two oars, two orange life vests kept under the seat); the far field across the river; the Lower Landing (a small concrete pier).
- The Korean farmhouse floor: warm ondol floor (in summer unheated; cool woven mats), shoes off at the door, low table, sleeping on thin mats (yo) unrolled at night.

## Cultural notes (each web-verified 2026-09-26; keep the book consistent with these)
- Rainy season (jangma): typically late June or early July, lasting three to four weeks, near-daily heavy rain, humidity above 80%; beneficial for farmers who grow rice in paddies. THIS book sets the rains a little late and long, at the start of the school holiday (which in Korea runs about mid/late July to mid/late August).
- Flash floods: rapid rises in river level are dangerous and can sweep away anything; even if it is not raining where you are, rain upriver can flood you. Rural areas are hardest to monitor. Book rule: real danger is respected; the children wear life vests, stay with the boat, and nobody is hurt.
- Rivers: the Han runs through Seoul (a flood system of dams, levees, pumps protects the city); the Nakdong is the longest at 510 km. The book's river is a fictional "Long River" in an unnamed valley.
- School and hagwon: about three quarters of elementary students attend a hagwon (private academy) most days in the afternoon or evening, even in holidays; students get vacation homework. Jun has hagwon most afternoons and a packed schedule; his parents work long hours.
- Rural life: elder-heavy villages because young people moved to cities; red peppers (gochu) dry on mats in yards and on roofs in late summer, sun-dried 7-10 days, then ground to gochugaru for kimchi; pepper-drying time is communal; red pepper flakes are central to kimjang (winter kimchi-making) and to the kitchen.
- Home: ondol (heated floors, over a thousand years old); shoes off inside; sitting and eating on the floor at low tables; sleeping on thin mats (yo) on the floor.
- Food: kimchi, rice, bibimbap (rice bowl with mixed vegetables, egg and chili paste), gimbap (rice and vegetables rolled in seaweed and sliced), watermelon (everywhere in summer; also chilled watermelon punch, subak-hwachae), Korean melon, cold buckwheat noodles (naengmyeon) in summer, patbingsu (shaved ice with sweet red beans), pajeon (scallion pancakes) eaten on rainy days because the sizzle sounds like the rain; farmers used to fry pancakes when heavy rain kept them from the fields. Do not put alcohol on the page: Harabeoji may drink barley tea; makgeolli is not mentioned.
- Family words: Halmoni (grandmother; father's mother), Harabeoji (grandfather; father's father); mother's parents would be oe-halmoni and oe-harabeoji (not used); Eomma (mom), Appa (dad). "Aigoo" (many meanings: surprise, worry, fatigue, joy, frustration, sympathy). Jeong (deep warm attachment that grows between people; hard to translate; show it, never explain it more than once). Other words used sparingly: hagwon, kimchi, gimbap, bibimbap, pajeon, ondol, jangma, hangul (the Korean alphabet; Jun could mention he types in it), Chuseok (autumn harvest holiday, mentioned as "coming in the autumn"), gochu.
- Address: children address grandparents as Halmoni and Harabeoji; they bow slightly to greet elders and take food and gifts from elders with two hands; elders are served first.
- Language rule: use only the words above, glossed in context, never invent others. Do not spell out sentences in Korean.

## Vocabulary and learning (build in naturally, never as a lecture)
- Target English words (in context, ~3 per chapter): forecast, current, drift, sandbar, levee, sluice, tarp, drought/flood (flood), swollen, murky, stubborn, confess, apologize, rivalry, tease, trace, steer, paddle, bail, knot, loop, stamina, patience, argue, compromise, shelter, upstream, downstream, bend, weir, landing, reunion, grateful, humid, drizzle, cicada.
- Facts the reader learns: how a rainy season works and why it matters for rice; how to read a river (color, speed, sound, floating debris, bends and sandbars); life-vest and "stay with the boat" rules; what a knot needs to hold; how red peppers are dried; what a hagwon is; how to bow and eat politely; what "being right" costs when you will not listen.
- Kid-doable idea (optional, woven into the last chapters): "two-column thinking": before you argue, write what you are sure of in one column and what the other person knows in the other, and see what the boat needs.

## The plan of the story
PART ONE: TWO WAYS OF KNOWING (ch 1-5): Jun sent to the village; the bus and the wheelbarrow; the house, shoes and food; the river and the boat and its rules; the score sheet begins. PART TWO: RAIN (ch 6-10): the app is wrong and the sky is right; three days of rain and Jun mends the radio; reading the water; the village meeting; the crossing to save the peppers and the two knots. PART THREE: THE RIVER (ch 11-15): the rope slips and the boat is swept away; stay with the boat; reading the bend; the night on the sandbar; the water comes up. PART FOUR: HOME (ch 16-20): the weir and the side channel; the Lower Landing and Mr. Cho; the truck and Halmoni; what Harabeoji said; the last day and the torn score sheet.

## Motifs
Jun's phone (battery percent falling: 100 at the start, 8 by the last night); Nari's sniff-and-squint at the sky; Jun's score sheet ("Nari 3, Jun 2"); the header's two voices; Halmoni's "Aigoo!" and "Eat first. Talk later."; Harabeoji's "Stay with the boat."; the two knots that become one; the orange life vests; the white sneakers turning brown; cicadas; watermelon; the red peppers on their mats; the boat called the Pear.

## The lesson (dramatize; NEVER preach)
Teamwork beats being right. Jun and Nari are each right about something (the map, the sky) and each too proud to use the other's knowledge, and being right alone gets them nowhere: the two knots fail because each is half-tied; the sandbar is reached only when Jun's map and Nari's reading of the current agree; the paddle works when Jun designs it and Nari uses it. No one is humiliated for being wrong. One plain line near the end at most (Harabeoji: "A boat with two good rowers pulling different ways goes nowhere."). Jun is not a fool and Nari is not backward: city knowledge and river knowledge are both real. The ending is honest: the cousins are safe, in trouble, and friends.

## Things to avoid
No injuries or deaths; the river is dangerous and the book respects that (vests on, stay with the boat, the peril is being lost and cold and afraid, and it is never gory). Do not glamorize disobeying safety rules: the children make a decision together, it goes wrong partly because they argue, they are scolded, they accept it. No romance. No exoticizing, tourist-guide voice, "quaint" or "backward" village, or jokes at anyone's expense; treat the farm, the food and the elders with respect and ordinary warmth. No mention of alcohol. Do not describe real disasters or name real rivers or towns; the river and the village are fictional. Never invent Korean words: only the verified list above. Never write "Korea" or "Korean" in the story text. No stacked similes; avoid "It wasn't X. It was Y."; avoid "heart hammered", "stone in the throat", "butterflies". No em dashes. IMAGE lines: always name the characters (Jun, Nari, Halmoni, Harabeoji, Uncle, Auntie, Mr. Cho) and restate their clothes; when the children are in the boat they wear bright orange life vests over their clothes; Jun's phone screen is blank in pictures; no writing anywhere.
