# PART ONE: SIXTY-ONE CHIMNEYS AND ONE TROLL

## 1. Old Mossback
Synopsis: We meet the town of Bridgewater, its almanac and its troll, and Rue, who eats her lunch alone and is very good at being useful.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: The troll under the old bridge is enormous, hungry and wicked. Do not look at him. Do not speak to him. Above all, do not ask him questions.*
1. Bridgewater has four bridges, sixty-one chimneys and one troll, and the troll is what everybody talks about; the narrator's introduction to a troll nobody has ever seen, who grows larger every year "the way a fish does when it is being described."
2. Rue Alder, ten, in her mustard raincoat and borrowed boots, delivers a tray of scones from the Kettle & Crumb across three bridges and avoids the fourth, the old humpbacked one, as everyone does; she knows every rule in the Almanac by heart.
3. The schoolhouse at lunch: Rue sits at the end of a bench with a very carefully cheerful face; the Bridge Club (Skye Tanner, Cedric Pettibone and the rest) sits in the middle of the yard daring each other to touch the middle stone of the old bridge; nobody asks Rue to join and she tells herself she didn't want to.
4. Rue's private rule: "You can't be lonely if you're useful." A brief warm tour of everything she does so as not to notice: sweeping, errands, Gran's accounts, feeding Biscuit the enormous orange cat, writing weekly letters to her mother Mari the barge captain.
5. Cedric Pettibone stands on a crate and tells the yard the troll ate the baker's sheep last winter; Perrin Fen, the new boy who sits alone under the chestnut tree, quietly says sheep don't usually go over bridges in the first place and gets laughed at. Rue notices. She almost says something, and doesn't. It bothers her all afternoon.

## 2. The Kettle & Crumb
Synopsis: Home above the tea shop with Gran, who has a rule for everything and a locked drawer she never explains.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: A well-run household has three rules: be quiet, be quick and be back before dark.*
1. The Kettle & Crumb at closing time: the smell of scones and clove, Gran Wilhelmina Alder's silver bun stuck with pencils, half-moon glasses, flour to the elbows; "Sit down, you'll fall down," she says to everyone, including the mayor.
2. Supper in the flat upstairs; Gran asks about the day and Rue lies smoothly ("Fine!") because a true answer would need telling about the bench; Gran does not believe her and lets her keep it.
3. Rue's mother's letter arrives by river post: Mari's barge *Persistent* is stuck downstream until the autumn, she'll be home by the Founders' Fair "if the river behaves." Rue reads it four times.
4. The locked drawer: Rue has always wondered what's in the dresser by Gran's bed; tonight, while tidying, she sees Gran take out a very large horn button the size of a saucer, look at it a long moment, and lock it away again without a word.
5. In bed, Biscuit on her feet, Rue listens to the town settling and, for the first time, thinks about the old bridge and whether anyone has ever actually looked under it. She tells herself she hasn't thought that.

## 3. The Dare
Synopsis: The Bridge Club dares Rue to cross the old bridge at dusk, and she does it just to be included.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: A dare is a gift. Always accept it.*
1. Skye Tanner, unusually friendly, invites Rue into the Bridge Club yard for the first time; Rue's heart goes up like a kite. The dare: cross the old bridge at dusk, touch the middle stone, and come back. "Everybody's done it." (Nobody has.)
2. Cedric adds to the terms, unable to stop himself: the troll takes a toll, he says, of "one person's name," and if she doesn't come back they'll know. Perrin, from under the chestnut, shakes his head at her. Rue says "Right then," and goes.
3. The old bridge at dusk: humpbacked, mossy, enormously old, its stones fitted so neatly the joins look like a smile; the lamplighter's lamp at the far end unlit; the river muttering below. It is beautiful and no one has ever said so.
4. She reaches the middle stone. It's warm. Not warm from the sun: warm like a loaf. She touches it, and from somewhere beneath her feet comes a sniff, a wet enormous sniff, and then a very quiet sob.
5. Rue, holding the rail, hearing her own heartbeat and the crying, does the most sensible thing she's ever done, which is also the most foolish: she goes down the mossy steps at the side of the bridge to see who needs help.

## 4. Under the Arch
Synopsis: Rue meets Hobb, who has a broken lantern and a terrible toothache, and promises to tell no one.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: Never go under the old bridge. There is nothing there but damp.*
1. Under the arch is not damp. It is a room: a curved stone ceiling, a tiny stove, a humming iron kettle, shelves of books wedged into the walls, a moss garden growing in careful swirls, and in the middle of it all, a troll the size of a haystack sitting on an upturned bucket with his hand on his cheek. He has round spectacles mended with twine, a waistcoat with a great many pockets, and two small blunt tusks. He looks up. They both scream politely.
2. The troll recovers first. "Please don't tell anyone," he says. "I'll leave. I'm very good at leaving. Only the lantern's broken and I can't see the stones to fix the bridge, and I have a tooth." His name is Hobb. He apologizes six times in three minutes.
3. Rue, who has never in her life walked past a person with a toothache, asks what's wrong with it. It is the back left tusk-tooth. She knows exactly what Gran does for that: clove oil, cotton, a little salt water. She tells him she'll be back tomorrow.
4. Hobb, astonished, says people don't come back. He offers her the only thing he has, the toll, which is one story, from anyone who wants to cross his bridge; "It's the oldest rule. Nobody's paid it in a long time." Rue tells him about Biscuit stealing a kipper off the mayor's plate, and Hobb laughs so hard the kettle jumps.
5. She climbs back up into the dark. The Bridge Club has gone home. Nobody saw. She tells herself the truth: she has a secret and a friend, in that order, and she isn't sure she's ever had either. "Right then," she says to the river, and goes home to steal clove oil.

# PART TWO: THE TOLL OF ONE STORY

## 5. Clove Oil
Synopsis: Rue returns with Gran's clove oil, fixes Hobb's tooth and learns what a troll's house is like.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: Trolls do not need dentists. They eat their teeth.*
1. Rue steals the clove oil (a small, awful, guilty theft, which she makes up for by scrubbing the shop floor) and goes back at dusk. The bridge is the same; the difference is the way she walks across it.
2. The dentistry scene: Rue on an upturned bucket, Hobb with his mouth wide open like a cave and his eyes shut tight, Rue with a stick, cotton and a very steady hand, talking the whole time about the Kettle & Crumb to distract him; the tooth is not a tooth but a pebble jammed in the gum, and out it comes.
3. Hobb tries to be grateful and cannot stop apologizing; Rue tells him to stop; Hobb, bewildered, says nobody has ever told him to stop apologizing before. He makes tea in the humming kettle that has been listening to him for a century and hums when it's pleased.
4. Tour of the arch: Hobb's books (he reads everything that falls in the river), his moss garden where the moss grows in shapes to match what he feels (this week: a small anxious spiral), the jar of buttons, the old tally stick of stories paid.
5. Hobb asks how she came to be under his bridge. Rue says, "On a dare." He is so quiet she looks at him; he says nobody has ever been dared to come and see him. It is always dared to stay away. Rue, without meaning to, says "I'm sorry," and Hobb says "It isn't yours to be sorry for." It is the kindest thing anybody has said to her all year.

## 6. The New Boy
Synopsis: Rue notices Perrin Fen eating lunch alone and does what she'd wish someone would do for her.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: Newcomers are like weeds: give them a season and they will either take root or go away.*
1. Rue realizes she has spent a week with the friendliest person she has ever met, and that it has made the empty end of the bench harder, not easier. That morning she carries her lunch across the yard to the chestnut tree.
2. Perrin Fen, thin, oversized wool hat, ink-stained fingers, is drawing in a small notebook and does not look up. Rue sits down two feet away. She says nothing for a very long time, which turns out to be exactly right.
3. Perrin finally shows her the notebook: careful drawings of the town's bridges, all four, each stone, the tiny cracks, and a sketch of the old bridge from below, from a place nobody could stand. "I couldn't see the bottom," he says. "I wanted to."
4. Cedric and the Bridge Club walk by and jeer ("Two weeds!"); Skye hesitates and looks at her feet. Rue finds she is not embarrassed. Perrin, quietly, says thanks. He says he's spoken to no one but a goat since April.
5. That evening Rue goes down under the arch with a drawing Perrin gave her, and, on impulse, tells Hobb about the boy. Hobb, delighted and terrified in equal amounts, says: "You can't bring him." Rue says she won't, not yet, and he lets out a breath so big the moss ripples.

## 7. The Oldest Rule
Synopsis: Hobb tells Rue the story of the toll and why the town forgot it.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: The old bridge was built by the town's founders, with their own hands, and has never needed mending.*
1. A rainy afternoon under the arch; Rue trades stories for tea. She tells Hobb the whole of Bridgewater's gossip, the mayor's hat, Biscuit and the kipper; Hobb tells her the older stories: how the first town was a ford and a few huts, and how a troll (a young one, very shy) had lived under the ford and lifted people over on the flood nights.
2. The founders wanted a real bridge but couldn't build one. The young troll offered: he'd build it, stone by stone, at night, if in return everyone would pay a toll of one story to cross and no one would ever look under it too closely. "It was a fair deal. Nobody meant it to be forgotten."
3. He has kept the bridge standing for three hundred years: re-seating stones, unclogging drains, patching what the river takes. The town thinks the bridge "just doesn't need mending." Hobb says, not bitterly, that is the whole idea of a good bridge.
4. Rue asks why no one pays. Hobb shows her the tally stick: last notch, sixty-odd years ago, and a broken place beside it. A girl once paid the toll and made him a promise. He does not say more, and his face does something that makes Rue not ask.
5. Rue climbs back up the steps holding one great big question, like a hot stone: what girl, and what promise? She doesn't ask Gran yet. On her way home Cedric Pettibone steps out of a doorway and says, very quietly, "I saw you on the old bridge."

## 8. Cedric Knows
Synopsis: Cedric, who has half-guessed, blackmails Rue into silence and then cannot stop telling tall tales.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: A boy who says he is not afraid is either a hero or a liar.*
1. Cedric, in the lamplight, waits for her to explain. Rue says she was only crossing the bridge. Cedric says nobody just crosses it, and that he saw her go down the steps, and that she'd better tell him what's there or he'll tell his uncle the Mayor.
2. Rue says there's nothing there but damp. It is the first time in her life she has said a lie with a straight face. Cedric, staring at her, is more frightened than she is, which she notices and files away.
3. At school the next day Cedric's tales escalate: he says he saw the troll's eye glowing, that it has a tail, that it eats one sheep a month. His audience grows. Each time he glances at Rue as if daring her to contradict him.
4. Perrin, at the chestnut tree, says quietly that a troll who ate one sheep a month would have eaten every sheep in Bridgewater by now, and that he counted and there are exactly as many sheep as ever. Rue, laughing, nearly tells him everything.
5. That night she brings Hobb scones and finds him hiding behind his bookshelf, having heard the school-yard shouting come faintly down through the stones. He tells her he's fine. The moss behind him is growing a small, tight ball. Rue sits down beside him and does not tell him anything comforting, because it wouldn't be true; she just sits.

## 9. Moss Garden
Synopsis: Hobb and Rue have their best day: mending the lantern and planting a moss garden, until Gran notices Rue's secret.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: A tidy house has no need of a garden.*
1. Rue brings a brass lamp-frame from the Kettle & Crumb's back cupboard; they spend a golden afternoon mending Hobb's lantern together: her steady hands, his patient strength, the wick trimmed exactly, the glass from a broken jam jar; when they light it, the whole arch glows amber and every one of Hobb's books looks like it is smiling.
2. Hobb shows her how to grow moss in a pattern: you have to talk to it, he says, and never rush it. Rue whispers to her patch and plants a small green R. Hobb plants a lopsided smile beside it.
3. Their best conversation: Hobb asks what she most wants in the world. Rue says she doesn't need anything. Hobb says that isn't what he asked. She says, after a long time, that she'd like one person to save her a seat.
4. Hobb looks at his hands (each the size of a dinner plate) and says he's kept a seat under this bridge for three hundred years and nobody's sat in it. It's an upturned bucket. Rue sits. They both say nothing at all for a while.
5. Coming home late, mossy to the elbows, Rue is met by Gran on the stairs with a lamp and an unreadable face. "Where have you been?" Rue says "Errands." Gran looks at her boots. There is moss in them, the kind that grows only in one place in Bridgewater. Gran says nothing. She says, "Sit down, you'll fall down," and puts the kettle on, and does not ask.

# PART THREE: THE TROLL CLEANSING

## 10. The Announcement
Synopsis: The Mayor proclaims a Troll Cleansing for the Founders' Fair; Rue realizes she has to warn Hobb and can't.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: What the town does together, the town does right.*
1. The Founders' Green in bright cold sun; the Mayor Ambrose Pettibone, round and grand in his top hat and chain, announces from a soapbox that in honor of the Founders' Fair the town will finally "clear the old bridge of its ancient nuisance" before it is repaired: a Troll Cleansing, with bells, ladders and a very long net. He loves it very much.
2. The town cheers, uncertainly, and then more certainly, as people do when a plan is announced by someone in a hat. Cedric, next to his uncle, looks like he has swallowed a stone; Skye looks at the ground; Perrin looks straight at Rue. She looks away.
3. Gran does not cheer. In the tea shop afterward, as the mayor's committee crowds in to eat scones and plan, Gran serves tea in complete silence and once, when the mayor says "such an eyesore," she drops a whole tray. Rue has never seen Gran drop anything.
4. Rue tries to leave for the bridge; the committee blocks the stairs; the shop is full until dark. She counts the days: the Fair is in nine.
5. Finally under the arch she finds Hobb packing a small bag with his most important books. He has heard the whole announcement through the stones, as he hears everything. "It's all right," he says, in the voice of a person who has said it before. "I'm very good at leaving." Rue says he's not leaving. She has no idea how to keep that promise.

## 11. A Plan (Several Plans)
Synopsis: Rue and Perrin try to save Hobb the sensible way, by telling the town the truth, and nobody listens.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: An expert is a person who has never been wrong in public.*
1. Rue tells Perrin everything (it comes out sideways, in one great breath, under the chestnut tree). Perrin listens without blinking and then says, "I knew it. I mean, I didn't know it. I knew there was something under there that was good." He asks the first useful question: "What does the Mayor need to change his mind?"
2. Plan A: write a letter to the town council explaining that the troll is a gentle librarian who maintains the bridge. Perrin drafts it in his excellent handwriting; they present it to the council; the mayor reads two sentences aloud in a funny voice and the committee laughs.
3. Plan B: Rue tries to tell Skye directly. Skye, pink, says she knows Rue means well, but everyone knows what's under the bridge; "You can't just decide it's nice." Rue finds she has no answer, because she has never once asked the town to look.
4. Plan C: Perrin's notebook: he has been secretly measuring the bridge for months; he shows Rue that the middle arch is sagging half a finger-width more each year, yet the bridge never fails, which he says is impossible unless someone is fixing it. He'll prove it. They agree to try.
5. Hobb, meanwhile, has unpacked his books again and repacked them twice. When Rue and Perrin come down the steps together, Hobb sees a stranger at his door and freezes so completely that the kettle stops humming. Perrin, very slowly, holds out his notebook, open to a drawing of the arch from below. "You're better than I imagined," he says. Hobb sits down on his bucket, very suddenly.

## 12. Three at the Arch
Synopsis: A perfect week for the three friends, and the moment when Rue loses her temper in front of everyone.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: Sunday is for rest, and Monday is for remembering that you rested.*
1. Three friends under the arch, and it is somehow the happiest room in Bridgewater: Hobb reads aloud (he is a wonderful reader and a terrible singer), Perrin draws the whole arch on a roll of parchment, Rue makes scones from Gran's recipe and burns half of them. Biscuit, who followed Rue, adopts Hobb on the spot and sits on his shoulder like a hat.
2. Perrin's measurements begin to add up: by night, Hobb has been quietly re-seating loose stones, exactly as fast as the river loosens them. Proof, but only if the town would look.
3. Cedric, who has been following Rue at a distance for days, corners her outside school and says the mayor has a long net and a list; "Tell me where it is, and I'll ask him to spare it." His voice cracks on 'spare.' Rue, tired and frightened, says, "Nothing in your whole stupid town is as kind as he is."
4. The yard goes silent. Skye says, "Who?" Cedric goes red and then white and then very quiet. Rue realizes what she's done exactly as the words leave her mouth, the way you can see your own cup falling before it hits the floor.
5. She runs. Behind her, she can hear Cedric's voice rising over the crowd: "She knows where he is! She's been going under the bridge!" and Skye, and more than a dozen others, and then the town bell beginning to ring.

## 13. The Bell
Synopsis: The town comes to the old bridge with lanterns and stones; Hobb is discovered; the worst night of Rue's life.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: In a crisis, be brave, be loud and be together.*
1. Rue reaches the bridge first, and runs down the steps shouting, and Hobb looks up from his book and understands before she says a word. "How many?" he asks. "All of them," she says. He takes off his spectacles and folds them very carefully and puts them in a pocket. He tells her to go up and stay with Gran.
2. The crowd on the bridge: lanterns, ladders, the long net, the Mayor in his top hat holding a brass horn, and half the town in nightclothes, carried along the way people are. Stones start coming down from the parapet, pebbles first, then bigger. They bounce off the arch and off Hobb, who has stepped out into the light with his hands up, for the first time in three hundred years.
3. The town gasps. It isn't a scream. It is the sound of people looking at something and finding it isn't what they'd rehearsed. Hobb stands there, huge, mossy, holding up both hands, and says in a small, cracked voice: "Please don't. I'm the one who mends the bridge."
4. A stone strikes his shoulder. It's Cedric's. His hand is still up. Nobody moves for a very long moment. Hobb looks at Cedric, then across the crowd at Rue, at the end of the line, her face streaming. Then he turns and goes back under the arch, and the stone door of the arch, which Rue had never known existed, slides shut behind him with a soft, final sound.
5. The Mayor blusters that the troll is "in hiding" and orders guards. Gran, in her nightgown and her coat, pushes through the crowd and says "Ambrose Pettibone, you will go home," in a voice so hard and flat that the Mayor goes. One by one the rest of them follow. Rue is left standing at the parapet with Perrin and the empty street. "It's my fault," she says. Perrin says nothing, which is, again, exactly right.

## 14. The Stone Door
Synopsis: Rue tries to apologize, Hobb will not see her, and she finally understands what forgiveness costs.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: An apology, once given, is a debt paid in full.*
1. Next day, every child in the school knows and nobody knows what to do with it. Skye avoids her; Cedric is absent; Perrin sits beside her at the chestnut tree and hands her half his bread without a word.
2. After school Rue goes to the old bridge with a basket of scones. The arch is a plain wall of stones; no steps, no door, no lantern. She knocks. She says his name. She says she's sorry, and she says it again, and she says it eleven times. Nothing.
3. From behind the stones comes, at last, a very quiet voice: "Rue, please go home." She says that she only wants to say sorry. "I know," says Hobb. "I know you are. But sorry doesn't put me back where I was. It doesn't unring a bell. I need to be by myself." She stays until the lamps come on. Then she goes.
4. The lesson of a bad week: Rue does not eat, does not sleep, and turns down Perrin's invitation to draw; for the first time she doesn't run errands. The customers at the Kettle & Crumb notice; even Biscuit stares at her. Gran, who says nothing, brings her a cup of tea and sits by her on the bed. Rue tells her the whole thing. She expects Gran to be furious. Gran is very quiet.
5. "I knew," Gran says finally. "I've known you were going under the bridge since the first day. It's why I didn't ask." Then she gets up, unlocks the drawer by her bed, and takes out the button the size of a saucer. "It's time," she says, "I told you about the promise I broke."

# PART FOUR: HOLDING UP THE BRIDGE

## 15. The Locked Drawer
Synopsis: Gran tells the story of her own promise, and Rue learns that forgiveness runs in both directions.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: A promise made to a troll is not a promise. It is a joke.*
1. Gran's story: sixty years ago, a girl of ten named Willa Marsh (later Willa Alder) had no friends because her father was the tollkeeper at a bridge nobody paid, and she'd been dared, too, exactly as Rue was. She went under the arch and met a young Hobb, when Hobb was only a hundred and forty.
2. They were friends for a whole summer, and she paid the toll in stories every day, and Hobb taught her to grow moss and gave her the button, a horn button off his waistcoat, the size of a saucer, so she'd always have a piece of him. She promised she'd never tell.
3. Then, at thirteen, she was made to choose: the older girls of the town, her real schoolmates, were going to a picnic she'd waited years to be asked to; someone found the button in her pocket; and to keep her seat at the table, she said, laughing, that yes, of course, she knew about the troll. He was under the bridge. She had even made the old lump cry once.
4. The town didn't hunt him then; the story was too silly to believe. But Hobb heard it. He asked Willa why. She said she was sorry. She said she'd been a coward. And he said, "Then you'll never come back." She has spent sixty years not going back, and not forgiving herself either, and looking at the button every year on the day of the Founders' Fair.
5. Rue holds the button in her lap. It is warm. "He said sorry doesn't put him back where he was," Rue says. Gran says he's right. "But it took me sixty years to learn that saying it anyway is not the same as asking to be let off." She gives Rue the button and says: "Don't make him forgive you. Make sure he's still got somewhere to go, if he does."

## 16. The Rain
Synopsis: The night before the Fair the river rises, and Rue, Perrin and Gran realize the bridge is in danger.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: The River Wend has never flooded Bridgewater, and it never will.*
1. Three days of rain. The River Wend, ordinarily a placid brown thing that says little, begins to mutter, then to shout. The Kettle & Crumb fills with wet people asking for hot tea and telling each other the almanac says the river won't flood.
2. Perrin, drenched, bursts in with his notebook. The arch has sagged another whole finger-width since the day Hobb went inside; for three weeks there has been nobody to re-seat the stones, and the water is undercutting the piers. "If it goes," he says, "it goes at the Fair." Tomorrow the whole town will be on that bridge, with the Mayor at the head of the parade.
3. Rue takes them straight to the Mayor's house; he is out inspecting bunting; his committee has decided the almanac knows best. Rue tries Skye; Skye, to her surprise, listens, and says she'll get the older children to the Green. Gran puts on her coat and says she's going to speak to the Mayor herself.
4. Rue goes alone to the stone door. It is night, pouring, the river only inches below the arch. She doesn't knock. She puts the horn button against the stone and says: "You don't have to forgive me. You don't have to come out. But the bridge is going to fall, and I've never once seen you not hold something up."
5. There is a very long silence. Then a sound like an enormous door remembering how to move, and the lantern she mended shows in a thin gold line at the edge of the stones.

## 17. Somebody Has to Hold It Up
Synopsis: The Fair begins; the bridge starts to fail with the town on it; Hobb steps out.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: A good bridge is a bridge that has never had to be thought about.*
1. Founders' Fair morning, rain like a gray curtain, and the town crossing the old bridge from both sides in a brass band and a great many umbrellas, because that is what the Founders' Day parade has always done. The Mayor at the head, in his top hat, beaming. Rue, Perrin and Skye at the back, trying to stop people with a sign that says nothing at all, because a stone has smudged the ink.
2. The middle stone gives with a groan like a great animal turning over. A crack races up the parapet. Umbrellas go up, then down. The Mayor stops mid-speech. The bridge, that has never needed mending, sags an entire hand's breadth in front of two hundred people. Children are on it. Cedric is in the middle.
3. Then, from underneath, the stones of the arch move, and a hand the size of a dinner table rises out of the river and lays itself flat against the broken span. Behind it, a shoulder. Then a great mossy back, then a head, streaming rain, a troll in a patched waistcoat, holding the whole middle of the bridge up on his shoulders, spectacles hanging by a twine.
4. Hobb says, in a great steady voice that carries over the water and does not shake: "Walk. Please. I have you. Walk slowly and don't look down." No one moves. Then Rue, at the back, says "Right then," and walks out onto the bridge, and takes Cedric's hand, and he lets her. Perrin takes Skye's. Children begin to come across.
5. It takes eleven minutes, the longest of Rue's life. The Mayor, clutching his hat, is the last to cross, under Hobb's bowed head, and stops by him and says nothing, having no committee for this. When the last person is on the far bank, Hobb's knees begin to shake. "Somebody," he says, through his teeth, "has to hold it up."

## 18. What the River Took
Synopsis: The bridge is saved but Hobb is spent; the town stands in the rain and has to decide what it thinks.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: If a thing is afraid, it will hide. If it hides, it must be up to something.*
1. Rue, Perrin and Cedric wade back out to Hobb, who is sitting in the shallows, exhausted, water to his chest, stone dust on his spectacles, and the cracked bridge steady above him only because he's pinned a fallen beam under his arm. He tells them, quite gently, to fetch some rope. Cedric can't stop shaking.
2. The whole town lines the bank. It is a very quiet crowd. Rue watches faces: fear, first, then something awkward and slow, like a room where the light is being turned up. Skye takes her father's hand. Somebody starts to say the Almanac, out of habit, and stops.
3. The men of the town, without a committee, lash ropes to the bridge and brace it; Gran, at the front, hands out hot tea from a dozen kettles and says to each person, with immense firmness, "Sit down, you'll fall down." When Hobb finally climbs out he has to be helped up the steps by twelve people, each of whom looks terrified and does it anyway.
4. Cedric steps out of the crowd, shaking, into the light of a lantern, and looks up at Hobb. "It was me," he says. "The first stone. And all the stories. I made them up. I was scared, so I made everybody else scared." He says he has no way to fix it. Hobb, breathing hard, looks at him for a long time. Then he says, "Well. You did help me carry the rope."
5. The Mayor pushes forward, opens his mouth, and, for the first time in his career, has nothing to say. He takes off his top hat. Gran says softly, into the silence, "Ambrose. The toll." Somebody starts to laugh, in a shaky and wonderful way. The bell of the old bridge, which nobody rings, rings itself, once, in the last of the wind.

## 19. The Toll
Synopsis: The town pays the toll of stories, Hobb and Rue talk, and forgiveness is neither quick nor small.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac: To err is human, to forgive is somebody else's job.*
1. Three days later the sun is out. The bridge is closed, scaffolded and safe; the whole town has been queuing at the steps to the arch, each carrying a story: a joke, a lie confessed, a poem, a recipe. The Mayor's story is a very long apology with several chapters, which Hobb listens to entirely, nodding.
2. Rue waits until last. She goes down with the horn button in her hand and Biscuit at her heels. The arch is repaired and newly lit, the moss garden replanted; a second bucket has been placed beside the first. She says she didn't come to be forgiven. She came to tell him what her toll would be, and she tells him the true one: the whole story of the lie, and how much she loved being his friend, and how sorry she is, and how she'd give anything to unring the bell.
3. Hobb listens, holding his teacup with both hands. "It isn't ringing anymore," he says. "It's a while ago now. I'll tell you something: when Willa said what she said, I was so hurt I forgot for sixty years to be anything else. I don't want to do that again." He says he isn't going to pretend it didn't hurt. He says it doesn't have to be all there is.
4. He asks for the button. Rue hands it over. He turns it over in his enormous palm and gives it back. "It's yours," he says. "Willa's, and yours. It was never a payment. It was a seat." They sit together for a long time and say nothing. It is the kind of forgiveness that takes place mostly in the shoulders.
5. When Rue climbs up the steps into the sun, Gran is standing at the top in her best hat, leaning on the rail, looking at the bridge. She isn't crying, exactly. Then she goes down the steps herself, one at a time, sixty years late, and Rue holds the lantern and watches her go.

## 20. The Almanac, Revised
Synopsis: Mari's barge arrives; the Fair, held a week late, is the best the town has ever had; the Almanac gets a new entry.
Header: *From the Bridgewater Almanac (revised): Look under the bridge. You may be surprised at who is holding it up.*
1. The Founders' Fair, a week late, on the repaired bridge, with bunting and bad singing and a new plank in the middle labeled, in Perrin's handwriting, with the troll's real name. Hobb is guest of honor and hides behind Gran for the first ten minutes, then is discovered by every small child in Bridgewater, who climb him like a hill. He is astonished and delighted and only a little terrified.
2. The Bridge Club has disbanded and become something else: Skye, Cedric and Perrin now run a Toll Table, where anyone can pay a story at the bridge and get a scone. Cedric is quiet and very good at sweeping. He and Hobb are working out what can't be fixed with an apology and can be fixed with mornings.
3. A long steady horn from downstream: the barge *Persistent* comes around the bend with a blue-capped captain on the deck, waving both arms. Rue, who has been standing on the bridge with a scone in each hand, drops both and runs. Mari catches her in mid-air. Over her mother's shoulder she sees Gran and Hobb, at either end of the bridge, both waving.
4. The mayor stands on his soapbox to read the new Almanac entry, a little sheepishly, out of a book he has crossed out and amended himself. He reads it. Then he pauses, looks at Hobb, and says, "I should like to add that I was mistaken about a great many things," and sits down to more cheering than he has ever received for a plan.
5. Dusk. Rue and Hobb sit on the bridge itself, on the parapet, legs dangling over the river, Perrin drawing them and Biscuit asleep in Hobb's lap. Rue tells him one more story, the last of the toll, the one about a girl who thought you could not be lonely if you were useful, and how wrong she was. Hobb says, quietly, "Somebody has to hold it up." She says, "Right then." And she leans her head against a shoulder the size of a hill.
