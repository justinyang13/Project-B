# PART ONE: DRY

## 1. The Sound of Nothing
Synopsis: On the 114th dry morning Mei and her little brother Tao carry water in a village where nothing grows, and Nainai says the dragon should be waking in six days.
Header: *Days without rain: 114. Buckets from the spring: 14.*
1. Mei wakes to the sound of nothing: a roof that expects rain and is disappointed. Nainai cuts the day's notch in the bamboo stick by the kitchen door; it looks like a tiny fence. Nainai explains the name to the reader (your father's mother). Tao clumps in wearing yellow rain boots "for luck".
2. Breakfast: Nainai makes dragon's-whisker noodles, thin as hair, because in six days is the second day of the second month, when the dragon raises his head. "Some dragon," says Tao. Nainai says the old people say the dragon wakes and the rain follows; she says it in the voice of someone who has said it for a hundred and fourteen days.
3. Mei takes her small bamboo pole and two small buckets to the last spring by the Dragon King shrine. The stream bed on the way is a road of grey stones; Tao counts frogs (none). The line at the spring: neighbors, each waiting with a pole, everyone polite and quiet the way people are when they are worried.
4. Mei sees the terraces above: rice seedlings in their nursery beds have gone yellow; tea bushes curl like brittle paper. Uncle Shi with his steel thermos pours a thin stream of tea onto a seedling bed by mistake in his thoughts and looks embarrassed. Mei thinks it is the saddest gift she has ever seen.
5. Home with 14 buckets' worth of water (only just), Mei presses her thumb into her palm the way she does. Tao asks if the dragon is even real. Nainai, wringing out a cloth, says: "Water finds a way. Sometimes it needs help finding the way." Mei looks at the pale blue sky. There is not one cloud.

## 2. The Girl Who Could Not Speak Up
Synopsis: At school Mei freezes when asked to speak, Hua shines, and a voice message from Mama and Baba makes Mei swallow her worries again.
Header: *Days without rain: 115. Buckets from the spring: 13.*
1. The one-room school: Teacher Luo, glasses and a red scarf, asks who will read the class report on the dry spell. Hua, tall, neat, red jacket and yellow ribbon, is already standing. She reads it beautifully and corrects Tao's spelling from two rows away.
2. Teacher Luo asks Mei one small question: what does she think the village should do? Mei's mind goes white as paper. Her thumb presses into her palm. She opens her mouth and nothing at all comes out; twenty children wait; someone coughs. "Take your time," says Teacher Luo, which makes it worse.
3. After school Mei climbs the hill to the signal rock, the only place a phone gets signal, with Tao. Mama and Baba's voice message plays from the city factory: Mama cheerful, Baba clearing his throat. "Are you eating? Is Nainai's knee better? Is there any rain?" They mean the questions kindly; Mei can hear the noise of machines behind them.
4. Tao records a long chatty answer. Mei holds the phone, opens her mouth, and says only, "We are fine." She does not say the wells are almost empty, the seedlings are yellow, that she is worried. She sends it and feels like a bucket with a hole in it.
5. Hua passes on the path, looking over the dry valley. She says, not unkindly, "You know you could just say it, Mei. Sentences are not that hard." Mei nods. She wants to say, "They are for me." She says nothing again, and watches a lone hawk circle a sky with nothing in it.

## 3. The Rain-Asking
Synopsis: The whole village gathers at the Dragon King shrine to ask for rain with drums, willow branches and a paper dragon, and the sky stays empty.
Header: *Days without rain: 116. Buckets from the spring: 11.*
1. Uncle Shi with his steel thermos rings the school bell: everyone will go to the shrine. Yeye Gu, very old with long white eyebrows, brings out the little stone Dragon King's offerings: tangerines, a bowl of rice, tea. The villagers wear willow branches on their heads; Tao's is as big as a bush.
2. The rain-asking begins: the drum, a small gong, the villagers kneeling in the dry dust, and Yeye Gu speaking the old words in a cracked, steady voice. Mei kneels behind Nainai, who cannot kneel easily because of her knees and leans on her stick.
3. Teacher Luo and the children carry a paper dragon made from red and gold paper on bamboo sticks and dance it around the shrine. Tao is the tail and keeps forgetting to dance. It is beautiful and funny and everyone laughs a little and then stops laughing, because the sky is still cloudless.
4. They wait. An hour passes. The drum slows. A small breeze lifts a little dust off the ground and sets it down again. Auntie Fen from the shop whispers that maybe the Dragon King is just very busy. Uncle Shi's thermos lid rattles in his hands.
5. Yeye Gu stands, slowly, with his cane, and says: "The Dragon King's servants are many. The old rain-asking was different. It was sent to someone." He looks around, then up at the far mountain wrapped in a thin ribbon of mist. Nobody understands him, but Mei feels her thumb press into her palm.

## 4. The Old Story
Synopsis: Yeye Gu tells the story of the dragon of Black Pool and the forgotten custom of sending a gift and a message each spring.
Header: *Days without rain: 117. Buckets from the spring: 10.*
1. That evening the village crowds into Yeye Gu's courtyard. He lights an oil lamp and tells them there was once a dragon at Black Pool, high on the mountain, who was the village's own: not the great Dragon King of the seas but a small, gentle river dragon. Tao asks how small. "Small enough to be shy," says Yeye Gu.
2. Every spring, the old story says, the villagers sent a gift of the year's first tea and a message up the Cloud Ladder to Black Pool, to say: We are here, we are grateful, please come again. One child was chosen to carry it. And the rain always came, and the rain always came late in the night with a great rolling roar.
3. Nobody remembers when the custom stopped. Maybe it was the year the road was paved. Maybe it was the year the young people left for the cities. Yeye Gu shrugs: "We forgot to say we are here. So perhaps he thinks we are gone."
4. Uncle Shi shakes his head. Stories do not fill buckets, he says. He has already asked the county for water trucks, but the road is washed out. Yeye Gu says, softly, that it is not a story about rain but about being remembered. Auntie Fen clucks. Half the courtyard is nodding, half is frowning.
5. Nainai, at the back on her stool, says quietly that she knows the path to Black Pool. She climbed it as a girl. Her knees would not manage the first hill now. Everyone looks at her. Mei, in the shadows beside her, realizes with a jolt that she is holding her breath.

## 5. The Meeting
Synopsis: The village argues about who could go, and Mei stands up and says two small words that turn out to be very large.
Header: *Days without rain: 117. Buckets from the spring: 9.*
1. The village meeting is held at the school after dark, on children's benches too small for everybody. Uncle Shi lists the problems on the blackboard in chalk: no water trucks, the well down to a brown puddle, the rice seedlings dying. He looks like a man carrying a bucket he can't set down.
2. Ideas: dig a new well (no money), carry water from the far river (a two-hour walk each way), pray again (they already did). Someone suggests sending a grown man to the pool. Someone else says it is a half-day climb up a path only the very old and the very young have used, and the path has grown over.
3. Uncle Shi says it should be a grown-up. No one steps forward: the farmers must stay with the seedlings; the men are working far away in the cities; the old cannot climb. Silence. Yeye Gu murmurs that in the old story it was always a child, because a child has nothing to hide.
4. Mei's thumb is pressing so hard into her palm it hurts. She thinks of the yellow seedlings and of the voice message where she said We are fine. Her throat closes. She stands up anyway. Her voice comes out like a leaf: "I'll go." Nobody hears. Twenty faces turn. Hua opens her mouth to say something clever.
5. Mei says it again, a little louder, holding on to the back of her bench: "I'll go. I will carry the tea and the message. I am small enough to be shy, too." A few people laugh, kindly, not at her. Nainai, at the back, smiles like sunrise. Uncle Shi puts his thermos down for the first time all evening.

# PART TWO: THE MOUNTAIN

## 6. Nainai's Rules
Synopsis: The village packs for the climb, and Nainai gives Mei three rules and a bamboo tube of tea; Tao insists on coming and Hua is sent to check the map.
Header: *Days without rain: 118. Buckets from the spring: 8.*
1. Before dawn, Nainai's kitchen: steamed buns, a jar of pickled vegetables from the earthenware urn, a bag of dry noodles, and a small cloth bundle of the year's first tea from the old bush behind the house. Mei carries the tea, wrapped in red paper, the gift. Nainai has a written message too, in her careful, old handwriting: We are here.
2. Nainai's rules: one, stay on the path; two, eat when you are hungry and rest when you are tired; three, "If you are afraid, keep walking, but slowly." She presses a small bamboo tube of tea into Mei's hand for the dragon. Tao appears wearing his yellow rain boots. "I'm coming." Nainai looks at him, looks at Mei, and says the path is safe if you stay together.
3. Uncle Shi is at the door with his thermos and a hand-drawn map on the back of a calendar page. He says Hua will go as far as the tree line to keep the map straight, and Old Grey the water buffalo will carry the packs to the foot of the Cloud Ladder. Hua arrives in her red jacket with a notebook, a compass and a second map, "in case the first is wrong".
4. Mei and Nainai stand at the door. Nainai says she cannot go, and that this is the hardest part of being old: knowing where the path is and not being able to walk it. She says that Mei's name means plum blossom, a flower that blooms in snow when nothing else dares. "You do not have to be loud to be brave."
5. They leave in the grey dawn, four of them counting the buffalo: Mei with the tea, Tao with his boots, Hua with two maps, Old Grey with the packs and his little brass bell. Behind them the village yawns awake; ahead the mountain is wrapped in mist. Mei looks back once and Nainai lifts her stick.

## 7. The Bamboo Path
Synopsis: The children enter the bamboo forest and learn to walk together; Hua turns out to be as scared of one thing as Mei is of another.
Header: *Days without rain: 118. Buckets from the spring: 8.*
1. The bamboo forest is a green cathedral of tall stalks that creak and click in the small wind. The light comes down in stripes. Tao asks why bamboo has knots, why it creaks, whether pandas live here, and what would happen if a panda were afraid of bamboo. Hua answers the first two correctly.
2. Hua reads the map aloud in her class-report voice and corrects Tao: "It is not a shortcut, it is a trail". Mei follows with Old Grey, listening to the grey bell ring. She notices that Hua's hands hold the map very tightly. Hua says it is because the paper is old.
3. At a dry stream crossing they find one small pool of water the size of a bowl with a single tadpole in it. Tao insists on carrying it in a cup to somewhere wetter. Mei thinks of Nainai's Water finds a way. They pour the tadpole into their water bottle; Tao names it Captain.
4. They stop to eat steamed buns on a mossy rock. Hua admits she has been reading everything she can about the mountain, every map, every trail, because she wanted to be sure. "I don't like surprises." Mei says she doesn't like being looked at. They realize they are more alike than either thought.
5. The bamboo thins, and the path curves. Above them, through a gap in the trees, they see a ridge of grey rock and a long thread of mist. Hua stops. "The bridge is next," she says, in a strange, small voice. She takes out the second map and does not open it.

## 8. The Bridge
Synopsis: A rope-and-plank bridge over a gorge tests Hua's fear of heights, and Mei finds she can speak when someone needs her.
Header: *Days without rain: 118. Buckets from the spring: 8.*
1. The gorge is a long cut in the mountain with a thread of dried river at the bottom, very far down. The bridge is wooden planks and two rope rails, and Old Grey has walked it a hundred times; he crosses, bell ringing, like a calm hill going for a stroll. Tao counts the planks aloud and reaches thirty-one before he is halfway across.
2. Hua does not move. Her ponytail is very still. Her face has gone the colour of rice paper. "I'll wait here," she says, "and read the map." The map is upside down. Tao, from the middle of the bridge, shouts that it is not scary, the planks are just wood. It doesn't help.
3. Mei comes back and sits down beside Hua at the edge. Without thinking about it she starts to talk, softly, in a quiet voice that does not shake: about the bridge, the planks, the buffalo who is calm because he has done it before. She tells her to look at Old Grey's back, not down. She tells her to breathe in for four counts.
4. Hua steps out. The first plank trembles; her knees shake so hard her yellow ribbon quivers. Mei walks backwards in front of her, keeping her voice steady: one, two, three, four, breathe. At the middle Hua freezes again. Mei says, "You do not have to stop being afraid. You just have to take one more step." Hua takes it.
5. On the far side Hua sits down with her hands over her face and laughs and cries at once. Tao says he did not think she could be scared of anything. Hua says it is an awful thing to be afraid of a bridge when you have read forty books about them. Mei realizes that she has just been talking, aloud, for ten minutes, to someone else, and did not shake once.

## 9. The Cloud Ladder
Synopsis: The path climbs into the clouds on a very long staircase, night falls in a tea-pickers' hut, and something sighs on the mountain.
Header: *Days without rain: 118. Buckets from the spring: 8.*
1. Old Grey stops at the foot of the Cloud Ladder, a very long flight of old stone steps disappearing into cloud, too steep for a buffalo. They unload the packs, and Old Grey lies down under a tree to wait. Tao hugs his nose. The bell rings once, goodbye.
2. The steps are worn in the middle from a thousand years of feet. Tao counts them until he loses count, then starts again. The mist is thick and cool and smells of stone and wet leaves, which after 118 dry days is the most wonderful smell the children have ever smelled. Hua stops to breathe it in. "It's damp," she says. "It's actually damp."
3. Near sunset they reach a tea-pickers' hut, a small stone room with a tile roof and a wooden bed and a single old lantern. Hua checks the map, checks the compass and declares it safe. They eat the last of the buns and drink a little of the water. Tao says he's not afraid of the dark, but he wants the lantern lit anyway.
4. In the dark, Tao whispers whether bats can get into huts. Mei whispers that bats are more afraid of Tao than Tao is of them. Hua says that is scientifically accurate. Tao pulls his rain boots on, in bed, and says they are for luck. The children lie in the small yellow circle of the lantern, listening to the mountain.
5. In the deep quiet Mei hears a long, low sound, far above them, like an enormous someone letting out a shaky breath. The lantern flame leans away, then back. The mist outside the window curls slowly in the shape of something very long. Nobody says anything. Tao whispers: "Was that the dragon?" and Mei realizes that whoever it is, they are also awake, and also afraid.

## 10. Black Pool
Synopsis: At dawn they reach the almost-dry mountain lake, and something very large hides from them very badly.
Header: *Days without rain: 119. Buckets from the spring: 7.*
1. The last steps lead out of the cloud and into a high bowl of stone and pine: Black Pool. It is a small dark lake, low and nearly empty, mud cracked around its edges like an old plate, a dead tree at one side. The water that is left is very still and very dark. The mist hangs over it like a lid.
2. The children stand at the edge with the tea. Mei's thumb presses into her palm. She has practiced her message a hundred times on the way up and now every word is gone. Hua whispers that they should read it off the paper. Tao whispers that the pool is looking at them.
3. A ripple. A very small one. Then nothing. A long coil of green, the colour of bamboo in mist, slides quietly from one rock to another and stops, as if it has just remembered it is seen. Something too large for the rock it is hiding behind is trying to be invisible: an antler here, a tail there.
4. A rumble goes through the ground, small and nervous, a hiccup of thunder. The mist swirls. A voice, deep and worried and very polite, says from behind the rock: "I am very sorry, but nobody is here." Tao asks who is talking, if nobody is here.
5. A single enormous amber eye peers over the rock, blinks, and goes wide. It looks at the children. It looks at the tea. It looks, for a long moment, at Mei. "Oh dear," says the eye. "You are real." The whiskers tremble. "So am I. I apologize for that."

# PART THREE: THE DRAGON

## 11. The Dragon Who Said Sorry
Synopsis: Yun comes out from behind the rock, apologizes to everything, and explains that he cannot bring rain because rain begins with his thunder.
Header: *Days without rain: 119. Buckets from the spring: 7.*
1. The dragon slowly lifts his head over the rock. He is long, slender and sea-green with a pale gold belly, branching antlers like a stag, white whiskers, big amber eyes, and a small bump on top of his head. Mist trails from him like a scarf. He is a good deal bigger than a house and looks as though he would like to be a good deal smaller.
2. He introduces himself, very carefully: "My name is Yun. That is the word for cloud. I am nearly four hundred years old, which for a dragon is about twelve." He apologizes to the children for hiding, to the rock for hiding behind it, and to the dead tree for stepping on it once. Tao asks if he breathes fire. Yun looks alarmed. "Certainly not. I have a cold as it is."
3. Mei presents the gift. Her voice shakes but she gets it out: "We are the village of Bamboo Ridge. We have come with tea and a message." She holds out Nainai's red paper. Yun takes it with a claw as gentle as a leaf, unfolds it and reads: We are here. His eyes fill with something between gladness and grief.
4. He drinks the tea in a single sip and shuts his eyes. He says it is the best thing he has tasted in a hundred years. Then he asks, very quietly, if the village is dry. Hua takes out her notebook and reads the drought report: 119 days. Yun makes a small sound like a kettle just before the whistle.
5. "You want me to bring the rain," says Yun. The children nod. "I cannot. I am sorry. I would if I could. But the rain starts with the thunder. And I do not do thunder." He curls his long body into a nervous knot and pulls the mist over his head. The pearl under his chin glows a dull, heavy grey.

## 12. Things That Could Go Wrong
Synopsis: Yun tells the story of his first thunder and shows the children his list of Things That Could Go Wrong.
Header: *Days without rain: 119. Buckets from the spring: 7.*
1. Yun unwinds slowly and, when Tao asks, shows the children his list: written in the dust of a flat rock with a claw, Things That Could Go Wrong: 1. Thunder. 2. Everyone hears it. 3. Everyone screams. 4. Someone cries. 5. It is my fault. It goes on for two whole flat rocks.
2. He tells them what happened. Long ago, when he was young, he rose over the village for his very first rain. He was so proud and so nervous that his first thunder was a great deal louder than he meant. He tells it plainly, with his whiskers drooping: doors banged, a baby howled, the ducks ran, and all the villagers covered their ears and ran inside. Nobody came out to look.
3. Hua asks the practical question: did anyone get hurt? Yun says no. He did not stay to find out. He had never seen anything so horrible as all those faces turned toward him in fear. He hid in the pool that night, and made himself a promise: never thunder again.
4. He explains what the promise did. Rain without thunder is like a sneeze you will not let out: it stays in, and gets heavier and heavier. He shows them his pearl, grey and swollen as a storm cloud. It aches, he says, all the time. "The more I hold it, the harder it is to let go." The pool, he says, is shrinking because there is no cloud to fill it.
5. Mei looks at the enormous, kind, trembling dragon and sees herself in the middle of the classroom with twenty people waiting and nothing coming out. She says, without deciding to: "You are afraid of your own voice." Yun stares. "Yes," he whispers. "How did you know?" And Mei says: "Because so am I."

## 13. Small Thunders
Synopsis: The children coach Yun through tiny thunders with breathing practice, silliness and a lot of clapping.
Header: *Days without rain: 119. Buckets from the spring: 7.*
1. Mei says they can start small. Tao says the smallest thunder in the world is a sneeze. Hua says thunder is a sudden expansion of air, if you want the science. Yun says he would rather not, thank you, but he is willing to try. He takes a deep breath and the children lean in. A tiny hiccup, a sound like a drawer sliding shut.
2. Mei teaches him the breathing she used on the bridge: in for four, hold for one, out for four. Yun follows, nose twitching, whiskers puffing. On the second try he produces a low rumble no louder than a cat purring on a warm roof. Tao cheers so loudly that Yun leaps two feet in the air and apologizes to the sky.
3. Hua analyzes his technique: "You're clenching your throat. That's why it gets stuck." She stands beside him and shows him what a relaxed throat looks like, and Yun tries to copy her, and both end up with their jaws hanging open. Tao laughs until he falls off a rock. The little valley of Black Pool rings with sound for the first time in a hundred years.
4. They practice for the whole afternoon: a purr, a low growl, a proper small rumble that rolls once around the rocks and comes back. Yun looks astonished. He does it again. A little wind stirs the mist. The pool ripples, just slightly. But when Tao begs for a "big one", Yun goes stiff, shakes his head, and the rumble dies.
5. The dragon hides his head under a wing of mist that he does not have. "There," he says. "You see. It is not that I cannot. It is that I will not. I see their faces every time." Mei sits down beside him on the cold stone and says nothing, which turns out to be exactly the right thing to say.

## 14. Mei's Secret
Synopsis: At night beside the pool Mei tells Yun about her silent voice, and he learns why thunder matters.
Header: *Days without rain: 119. Buckets from the spring: 7.*
1. Night falls on Black Pool. The children curl in their jackets against the dragon's warm green flank; Tao is asleep with his boots on, Hua is asleep with her notebook. Yun keeps watch and doesn't sleep. Mei doesn't either. The stars are very close and very many. The dragon's pearl glows a soft, tired grey.
2. Mei tells him about the classroom, the voice messages, the thumb in the palm, the way her mind goes white. She tells him about We are fine. Yun listens the way only a dragon that has hidden for a hundred years can listen: completely. He says, "But you said 'I'll go.' In front of everyone." "It came out like a leaf," says Mei. "Then it was a leaf that crossed a mountain," says Yun.
3. Mei says something else, thinking about Nainai's stick: in the spring, when the Awakening of Insects arrives, the old people say that the thunder wakes every sleeping thing: ants, frogs, seeds in the ground. She says thunder is not just loud, it is a knock on the door of spring. The dragon lifts his head. "A knock?"
4. Yun says that he has never once seen what happens after thunder. He always hid too fast. He never saw the frogs, or the seeds, or the green coming up the terraces. "I thought I was a noise that frightened people," he says slowly. "I did not know I was a knock." He is quiet a long time. His whiskers tremble.
5. They make a promise, in the dark, the way children do. Mei will say the words aloud, in front of the whole village, when it matters. Yun will make a thunder, a real one, when the time comes. Neither of them says they are not afraid. The pearl glows a little brighter, and above them a first small cloud drifts across the stars.

## 15. The Long Way Down
Synopsis: The children start home with Yun following in the mist, and the mountain's mood changes.
Header: *Days without rain: 120. Buckets from the spring: 6.*
1. At dawn Hua checks the compass twice, folds both maps and says, briskly, that they have twelve hours of daylight and the way down is faster. Tao insists on carrying the empty tea tube. Yun wonders aloud whether he ought to come at all. He makes a list. He makes it longer. Mei says, "You can walk behind us. You do not have to be seen."
2. Yun turns into a low cloud, or nearly: he drapes himself along the mountainside in a long ribbon of mist that follows the children down the Cloud Ladder. He is not very good at it: a tail keeps peeking out. Tao waves at it. Yun freezes into the shape of a rock, and is absolutely not a rock.
3. On the Cloud Ladder the mist thickens until the children hold hands in a chain. Yun's voice comes very softly from the fog, worried, apologizing to a step. Hua narrates the map, pace by pace. Mei counts breaths. Tao counts stairs and gets a different answer every time. For once nobody minds.
4. At the foot of the ladder Old Grey is waiting under the same tree, chewing calmly, as if no time had passed. The bell rings. The buffalo looks past the children at the fog, at the long green shape in it, and does not move a single whisker. "He is not surprised," says Hua. "He is a buffalo," says Tao. "Nothing surprises him."
5. They load the packs and start toward the bridge, with a dragon in the mist behind them. Hua, going first for once, takes a deep breath and says nothing about heights at all. Far below, at the bottom of the valley, the roofs of Bamboo Ridge are small and brown and dry. Above them, a single dark cloud has begun to gather over the shrine.

# PART FOUR: THUNDER

## 16. The Bridge, Again
Synopsis: Hua crosses first, Yun nearly panics on the bridge, and the children reach the valley to find the whole village waiting.
Header: *Days without rain: 120. Buckets from the spring: 6.*
1. Hua stops at the edge of the gorge. She looks at the planks a long time. She says, in her class-report voice, that she is going to cross first, because that is the correct thing to do. Then in a very small voice: "Will you both count with me?" Tao and Mei count. Hua crosses. The ribbon on her ponytail trembles the whole way. She does not once look at the map.
2. Mei follows, then Tao and Old Grey, the bell ringing. The mist gathers at the gorge's edge and stops. A large green shape is peering over it, pulling back, peering again. Yun says the bridge is too small for a dragon. Hua says he does not have to walk it. Yun says he does not have to fly it either, but he would like to be sure he knows how.
3. He rises into the air, awkward and marvelous: a long river of green lifting off the ground with a tremendous whooshing of mist, and just for a heartbeat he is beautiful. Then he looks down, the whole gorge yawning beneath him, and his rumble slips out of him in a startled hiccup that echoes off both walls. The children shout with delight; Yun apologizes to the gorge.
4. They come down out of the bamboo at last into the dry stream. The children see the village first, small and grey; then they see something else. People. All of Bamboo Ridge is gathered at the shrine, in the last of the evening light, faces turned toward the mountain. Uncle Shi's steel thermos gleams. Nainai is at the front, leaning on her stick.
5. Tao, in his yellow boots, is the first to run. Nainai opens her arms. Mei stops, halfway, because behind her the mist has thickened and pooled among the bamboo. She turns and whispers into it: "You can wait here for a minute." From the mist, an anxious voice: "I shall be very quiet. I shall be so quiet you will not know I am here."

## 17. The Whole Village Listens
Synopsis: Mei tells the village the truth about the dragon and asks them for something hard: not to cover their ears.
Header: *Days without rain: 120. Buckets from the spring: 6.*
1. Nainai hugs both children so hard her stick falls over. Uncle Shi asks in a rough voice whether they found the pool. Yeye Gu asks whether they found the dragon. The whole crowd falls silent. Hua nudges Mei with an elbow and whispers, "You have to say it."
2. Mei climbs on the low stone wall of the shrine. All those faces, twenty, fifty, a hundred, turned toward her. Her mind goes white. Her thumb presses her palm. Then she thinks of the bridge and of Yun, and breathes in for four. "The dragon is real," she says, in a voice that trembles like a leaf but carries. "His name is Yun. He is afraid."
3. She tells them everything, in order, plainly: the pool, the promise, the sneeze that won't come out, the baby and the ducks and the faces a hundred years ago. She tells them the dragon can bring the rain, but only if he thunders, and he will only thunder if he is not afraid of them. A murmur runs through the crowd like wind in bamboo. Some people look at each other.
4. Uncle Shi says, carefully, that a dragon who is afraid of thunder is not the kind of dragon he was expecting. Someone laughs. Yeye Gu says that is exactly the kind of dragon he remembered. Auntie Fen wonders aloud how one is supposed to greet a thunder. Mei says: not with hands over ears. "With drums. With our voices. We tell him, 'We are here.'"
5. A very long silence. Then Teacher Luo says the school has a drum. Auntie Fen says the shop has three pots. Old Grey shakes his bell. Uncle Shi puts the lid on his thermos, looks at the sky, and says, "Well. It is better than nothing." Nainai catches Mei's eye across the crowd, and her face is like a lamp.

## 18. The Answer
Synopsis: The village prepares its answer to the thunder while the dragon rises above the shrine and loses his nerve.
Header: *Days without rain: 120. Buckets from the spring: 6.*
1. Preparations at dusk: Teacher Luo hangs the school drum in the shrine yard, Auntie Fen hands out pots and wooden spoons, the children make a clapping rhythm, Old Grey shakes his bell. Hua stands on a bench and organizes the whole village into groups with the calm of a class monitor who has finally found a proper class. Tao gets a drumstick and a solemn look.
2. Night falls. The stars appear one by one. It is the second day of the second month, Yeye Gu remarks, mildly: the day when the dragon raises his head. Nainai says the stick has a notch for it. Everybody looks up. Mei goes to the edge of the bamboo and whispers: "Yun? Everyone is ready."
3. A long green coil rises slowly out of the bamboo grove, pale gold in the light of the lanterns. The villagers gasp, and go very still. Nobody screams. Nobody runs. Yeye Gu takes off his hat. A small child says, out loud, "He is so pretty." The dragon looks down at a hundred faces, and stops, frozen, shivering.
4. Yun rises higher, above the shrine, up into a small dark cloud that is gathering. The pearl under his chin glows and pulses. Mei sees that his whole body is trembling, right to the tip of his tail. She sees the first flicker of light in the cloud. She sees him shut his eyes. And she sees, in the stillness of the crowd, one man raise his hands halfway toward his ears.
5. Mei steps forward into the empty yard in front of the shrine, alone. She feels a hundred eyes. Her thumb presses her palm. She breathes in for four. And she says, out loud, clearly, in the biggest voice she has: "Yun!" The village goes silent. The dragon opens his eyes. "We are here."

## 19. Thunder
Synopsis: Yun makes a real thunder, the village answers, and the rain comes at last.
Header: *Days without rain: 120 (until tonight).*
1. The cloud above the shrine boils. Yun breathes in for four. He holds. Mei watches him holding, his whole long body shaking, the pearl blazing. She says, "Out for four." Beside her Tao lifts his drumstick. Hua raises her hand. Nainai's stick thumps once on the ground. The man who was raising his hands to his ears lowers them, slowly, and lifts a wooden spoon instead.
2. The thunder comes. It is enormous: a great, rolling, joyful roar that rolls across the terraces and down the valley and up the mountain and back, that shakes the tiles and rattles the pots and makes every hair on every head stand up. It is not frightening at all. It sounds like the whole sky clearing its throat, and then laughing.
3. The village answers. The school drum booms, pots clang, spoons rattle, children shout, Old Grey rings his bell. Uncle Shi, tears running down his cheeks, shouts "We are here!" and the whole village shouts it too, a hundred voices and one buffalo bell, for the first time in a hundred years. Yun stares, astonished, his whiskers standing straight out.
4. Then the first drop hits the dust. Then a hundred. The smell of rain on dry earth rises like the smell of every good thing: warm, dusty, sweet. The rain comes down on the tiles, on the terraces, on the cracked stream bed, on the upturned faces. Tao shouts that his boots are finally useful and stomps into the first puddle in a hundred and twenty days.
5. Nainai walks stiffly into the middle of the yard, lifts her face to the rain and laughs like a girl. She takes the bamboo stick out of her jacket and, very solemnly, cuts no notch at all. "Water finds a way," she says. Mei, soaked to the skin and shaking with cold and joy, stands in the rain and says the biggest sentence she has ever said: "Thank you, Yun." And overhead, softly, a dragon answers: "Thank you, Mei."

## 20. Grain Rain
Synopsis: Weeks later, the terraces are green, the tea is picked, and Mei speaks in front of everyone and does not mind who hears.
Header: *Days without rain: 0. Buckets from the spring: more than anyone can count.*
1. It rained all night. By morning the stream was a chattering brown river, the frogs were singing in the terraces so loudly Tao could not sleep, and Nainai had cut a new notch in the other end of the stick: Days with rain: 1. Mama and Baba's voice message played on the signal rock, and this time Mei told them everything: about the dragon, the bridge and Hua, the yellow seedlings, and the thunder. Mama went quiet, and then said, "Say it again."
2. A month later, on the day of the Grain Rain, the whole village was out picking tea, the way they do in the south on that day, the new leaves bright as green flames. Seedlings stood straight in the flooded paddies. Old Grey ploughed in his slow, calm circles. Hua, in a red jacket, walked along the terraces with a clipboard, explaining tea to everyone, and did not mind being interrupted.
3. Nainai trimmed Mei's bangs at the kitchen table, for Dragon Head luck, a little late. "Now you can see," she said, and Mei found she could see the whole room without squinting: Nainai's smile, Tao's boots, the notched stick on the wall. "You do not have to speak louder," Nainai said, "just where people can hear you."
4. At the school, Teacher Luo asked who would like to read the class report on the rain. Twenty hands did not go up. Mei's did. She stood, and her thumb pressed against her palm, and her voice shook, and she read it all the same. Nobody coughed. When she sat down Hua whispered, "Sentences are not that hard," and then, "Sorry." Mei whispered, "They are, a little." They both grinned.
5. Every dawn, if you know where to look, a long green shape drifts over Bamboo Ridge in the mist, high above the shrine, checking the terraces. Sometimes it rains a little. Sometimes a very small, polite rumble rolls once around the valley. And every time, somewhere in the village, a child drums on a pot with a spoon and shouts: "We are here." Mei does too.
