# MEI AND THE DRAGON WHO FEARED THUNDER — story bible

Warm, funny, lyrical village fantasy for ages 10-12, set in a mountain village in Sichuan, southwest China, during a long dry spring. No rain has fallen for 114 days. The wells are nearly empty, the rice seedlings are yellowing and the tea leaves are curling. Eleven-year-old Mei, who freezes whenever she must speak up, is sent up the mountain to fetch the help of the old rain dragon and finds a dragon who is terrified of his own thunder. Nothing scary or violent: no villain, no real danger beyond a rope bridge and a long climb. The stakes are the village's water and two frightened hearts. The book should make a reader feel a mountain village, love a shy dragon, and want to try one brave thing with a shaky voice.
IMPORTANT (user rule): the words "Chinese", "Taiwanese", "Japanese", "Korean" must NOT appear in the title, subtitle, cover text, tagline or blurb. Culture comes through names, food, customs, places, stories and language inside the story. (The story may name Sichuan and the mountains once or twice, naturally.)

## Voice
THIRD PERSON, PAST TENSE, close on MEI. Warm, gently funny, sensory and a little lyrical, like a very good read-aloud: mist, bamboo, the sound of water not falling. Humor comes from Tao (age 7, never stops asking questions, wears yellow rain boots "for luck" in a drought), Hua's know-it-all corrections, and the dragon's worrying (he keeps lists of Things That Could Go Wrong and apologizes to rocks). Mei notices what other people miss: how a person's voice sounds before they speak; what water is doing. Her feelings show in her hands and throat, never named and then explained. Short and long sentences mixed; real emotion arrives plainly; nothing is exotic to the children, it is simply home.
Every chapter opens with one italic tally line from NAINAI'S BAMBOO STICK, on its own line, then a blank line, in this form: "*Days without rain: 114. Buckets from the spring: 14.*" Nainai (Mei's grandmother) cuts one notch in a bamboo stick every dry day. The day count rises 114 to 120; buckets shrink; the last chapter's tally answers the first. The Header line is added by the script: never write it on any page.
Voice sample:
"*Days without rain: 114. Buckets from the spring: 14.*

On the morning of the hundred-and-fourteenth dry day, Mei woke to the sound of nothing. It was a very particular nothing. It was the sound of a roof that expected rain and was, once again, disappointed.

Her grandmother was already up, cutting a notch in the bamboo stick that hung by the kitchen door. Nainai (which is what you call your father's mother) cut one every morning, and the stick was now so notched it looked like a tiny fence."

## The premise
Zhou Mei (Mei), 11, lives in Bamboo Ridge Village, a hundred-odd households on terraced slopes above a dry stream, with her grandmother Nainai and her little brother Tao. Her parents (Mama and Baba) work at a factory in a distant city and come home only at New Year; they send voice messages that Mei can play only on the "signal rock" up the hill. Mei is shy: whenever she must speak in front of people, her voice goes small and her mind goes white, and she keeps every worry to herself. The village holds a rain-asking at the little Dragon King shrine, and it fails. Old Yeye Gu remembers the village's old story of the dragon of Black Pool, high on the mountain, and how each spring the village once sent a gift and a message, a custom forgotten. Nainai remembers the path but her knees cannot climb it. At the village meeting Uncle Shi, the village head, says stories do not fill buckets; the adults argue; and Mei, with a voice like a leaf, says "I'll go." With Tao, and the confident, know-it-all Hua (who is secretly terrified of heights) and old Grey the water buffalo as far as the tree line, she climbs through bamboo, over a plank bridge and up the Cloud Ladder to Black Pool, almost dry. There she finds Yun (yún = cloud), a young, sea-green, kind dragon who cannot make rain because rain begins with his thunder, and a hundred years ago his first thunder scared a whole village (children screamed, a baby cried, everyone covered their ears and ran), so he swore never to thunder again. Rain without thunder is stuck inside him like a sneeze he will not let out; his pearl is heavy with it. Mei recognizes his fear: hers is her own voice. The children practise small thunders with him; Mei tells him the old saying that thunder is what wakes every sleeping thing in spring, a knock on the door of the year, and he realizes that he hid before he ever saw what happened after. Together they go down the mountain; the village, led by Uncle Shi, is waiting at the shrine. Mei tells everyone the truth and asks them not to cover their ears but to answer the thunder with drums and their own voices ("We are here"). On the night of the second day of the second lunar month (Longtaitou, the day the dragon raises his head) Yun rises over the village, is terrified, and Mei stands in front of the whole village and speaks to him aloud. He thunders; the rain comes. Epilogue: Guyu (Grain Rain), tea-picking, frogs singing, the tally stick reversed, Nainai trims Mei's bangs for Dragon Head luck so she can see, Mei speaks at school.

## Cast (keep looks and habits EXACTLY consistent)
- **Mei (Zhou Mei)**, 11: small, thin, warm light skin sunburnt across the nose, two short black braids tied with red string, big dark eyes, a faded blue padded jacket with a patched elbow, a wide straw hat, cloth shoes; carries a child-size bamboo shoulder pole with two small buckets. Her name means plum blossom, the flower that blooms in winter snow. Shy, watchful, kind, stubborn underneath. Habit: presses her thumb into her palm when scared; counts her breaths.
- **Tao (Zhou Tao)**, 7, Mei's little brother: round face, gap-toothed, a green T-shirt with a tiger on it, and bright yellow rubber rain boots he refuses to take off ("for luck"). Endless questions; hungry; afraid of bats and the dark and never admits it.
- **Nainai**, 60s, paternal grandmother: small, straight-backed, grey hair in a bun with a wooden pin, a dark blue cotton jacket with cloth knot buttons, cloth shoes, a bamboo walking stick, bad knees. Dry humor, deep calm, makes dragon's-whisker noodles. Keeps the notched bamboo stick. Refrain: "Water finds a way. Sometimes it needs help finding the way."
- **Hua (Wu Hua)**, 11: tall, neat, a red jacket, a yellow ribbon on her ponytail; class monitor, always right, always reading; speaks beautifully in class; corrects everyone; secretly terrified of heights. Becomes Mei's friend by the bridge.
- **Uncle Shi**, the village head: round, tired, a grey jacket, a blue cap, always holding a battered steel thermos of tea; a practical man who worries for the whole village and hides it.
- **Yeye Gu**, very old shrine keeper and storyteller: long white eyebrows, a brown padded vest, a walking cane, cloudy but bright eyes. **Auntie Fen** runs the village shop (everything on shelves, gossip in the air; purple apron). **Teacher Luo**: young, glasses, a red scarf, patient. **Old Grey**: the village water buffalo, huge grey and calm, wide curved horns, a small brass bell, patient as a hill.
- **Yun the dragon**, "a few hundred years old, which for a dragon is about twelve": long and slender, sea-green like bamboo in mist with a pale gold belly and carp-like scales; soft branching antlers like a stag's; long white whiskers; large worried amber eyes; a small round bump on top of his head (his chimu, which lets a dragon rise into the sky: "the one thing I like about me"); no wings; a round grey-white pearl under his chin heavy with unshed rain. Gentle, polite, apologizes constantly (even to rocks), keeps lists of Things That Could Go Wrong. Not scary: mist curls around him like a scarf. Speaks in full, careful, slightly old-fashioned sentences.
- Places: Bamboo Ridge Village (terraced rice fields and tea bushes on steep slopes, tile-roofed farmhouses, a bamboo grove, a dry stream, the little stone Dragon King shrine by the last trickling spring, the school with one bell, the shop, the signal rock); the bamboo forest path; the plank-and-rope bridge over a gorge; the Cloud Ladder (long stone steps into cloud); a tea-pickers' hut; Black Pool (a mountain lake almost dry, mud cracked at the edges, mist).

## Cultural notes (each web-verified 2026-09-25; keep the book consistent with these)
- Dragons (lóng) in China are benevolent water spirits tied to rain and harvest, unlike fire-breathing western dragons. Classic look ("nine resemblances", Han dynasty): antlers like a stag, head of a camel, eyes like a demon, neck of a snake, belly of a clam, scales of a carp, claws of an eagle, soles of a tiger, ears of a cow. No wings: a Chinese dragon flies through cloud and qi. A luminous pearl under the chin or in the claws symbolizes wisdom and the essence the dragon governs. A dragon needs a chimu (a bump on the head) to ascend. (Use a few in Mei's descriptions; do not list all nine.)
- Dragon Kings (Long Wang) rule rain and water; many villages have a small Dragon King temple or shrine where villagers made offerings during festivals and droughts; if prayers failed, the statue might be carried out into the sun to feel the drought. Rain-praying rites include processions, drums and gongs, kneeling, and wearing willow branches on the head; dragon dances descend from rain ceremonies with a clay or paper dragon.
- Longtaitou ("Dragon Raises Its Head"): the second day of the second lunar month; the dragon, who governs rain, is thought to wake and rain begins to fall on farmland. Customs: haircuts (reward for not cutting hair in the first lunar month), eating foods with dragon names (dragon's-whisker noodles, dragon's-ear dumplings, dragon-scale pancakes), sprinkling water at the door to welcome the dragon home.
- Jingzhe (Awakening of Insects, about 5 March): the third solar term; the old saying is that spring thunder wakes every creature sleeping through winter; in some places people fry or roast beans and grains so the sizzle startles crop pests. Guyu (Grain Rain, about 20 April): the last spring solar term, rain that helps the grains grow fast; in the south, people pick tea that day.
- Mountain village life in Sichuan: steep terraced fields (titian) for rice, tea bushes, bamboo groves, tile-roofed farmhouses with courtyards; water carried on a bamboo shoulder pole with buckets; water buffalo plough the paddies; every household keeps earthenware urns of pickled vegetables (paocai, fermented with ginger, star anise and chili); steamed buns (mantou), noodles, rice, tea. In droughts, terraces dry out first, seedlings and tea suffer, and people carry water long distances.
- The plum blossom (mei) is one of the "three friends of winter" with pine and bamboo: courage, perseverance, overcoming misfortune; it blooms in the snow before other flowers, herald of spring. Chinese people call the flower "mei".
- Dragon-thunder lore: thunder is often a god (Lei Gong) who beats a drum. In THIS book the thunder belongs to the dragon (a fantasy invention); do not mention Lei Gong.
- Family: many rural children live with grandparents while parents work in far-off cities and come home at New Year; realistic and gently sad, not tragic. Address words: Nainai (father's mother), Yeye (grandfather), Mama, Baba, Laoshi (teacher, use "Teacher Luo"), Uncle/Auntie for village elders. Names: family name first (Zhou Mei).
- Language (use only these, glossed in context, never invent others): lóng = dragon, yǔ = rain, léi = thunder, yún = cloud, xièxie = thank you, Nainai, Yeye, Mama, Baba.

## Vocabulary and learning (build in naturally, never as a lecture)
- Target English words (in context, ~3 per chapter): drought, parched, terrace, seedling, brittle, trickle, tremble, whisper, hesitate, reluctant, courage, bravery, gorge, mist, shrine, offering, ancient, custom, ritual, rumble, echo, lantern, steady, stubborn, generous, cautious, apologize, ascend, cloudburst, grateful.
- Facts the reader learns: what a drought does to a village and its food; how terraces and tea grow; what lóng means and how Eastern dragons differ from fire-breathing ones; Longtaitou, Jingzhe and Guyu; why spring thunder matters to sleeping seeds and insects; how to help a friend with a fear by staying beside them and doing one small step.
- Kid-doable idea at the end (optional): a "whisper-to-thunder" breathing practice (breathe in for four, say your sentence low, then a little louder) described inside the story, not as a rule box.

## The plan of the story
PART ONE (ch1-5) DRY: the dry morning and the notched stick; school and Mei's frozen voice; the rain-asking that fails; Yeye Gu's story; the meeting where Mei says "I'll go." PART TWO (ch6-10) THE MOUNTAIN: packing and Nainai's rules; the bamboo forest; the bridge and Hua's fear; the Cloud Ladder and the hut night; Black Pool and the first glimpse of an amber eye. PART THREE (ch11-15) THE DRAGON: meeting Yun and his list; the old thunder story; practising small thunders; Mei's secret and the spring-thunder saying; the walk back down. PART FOUR (ch16-20) THUNDER: the descent in fog; the village at the shrine; the drums and the promise; Longtaitou night, Mei speaks, the thunder and the rain; Grain Rain morning.

## Motifs
The bamboo tally stick and its notches; Nainai's "Water finds a way"; Mei pressing her thumb into her palm; Tao's yellow rain boots (finally used in the last chapters); Uncle Shi's steel thermos; the sound of nothing (no rain); mist as Yun's scarf; the pearl growing heavier; plum blossoms; counting breaths; "We are here."

## The lesson (dramatize; NEVER preach)
Courage is not the absence of fear; it is doing the thing while you are afraid. Mei speaks with a shaky voice; Hua crosses a bridge with wobbling knees; Tao goes into the dark; Uncle Shi admits he has run out of ideas; Yun makes thunder while his whole body trembles. No one becomes fearless; they become people who did it anyway. One plain line near the end at most (Nainai or Mei). Fear of your own voice shrinks when someone stays beside you and listens. Thunder is not only a loud thing: it is a knock on the door of spring.

## Things to avoid
No fire-breathing western dragon, no princess, no knight, no villain. No violence, no real danger to children beyond a rope bridge (always safe: hand rails, adults know the path). No mention of Lei Gong or other gods beyond the Dragon King shrine. No exoticizing, tourist-guide voice, stereotypes or jokes at anyone's expense; treat rural life, the shrine and customs with respect. No sad-orphan tone: parents are far away for work and call. Never invent Mandarin or Sichuan words: only the verified list above. Do not put Chinese/Taiwanese/Japanese/Korean in title, cover or blurb. No stacked similes; avoid "It wasn't X. It was Y."; avoid "heart hammered", "stone in the throat", "butterflies". No em dashes.
