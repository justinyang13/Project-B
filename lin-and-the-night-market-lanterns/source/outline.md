# PART ONE: STEAM

## 1. Eighty-Four Bowls
Synopsis: On Winter Solstice night Lin introduces the night market, Ama's stall and the family's forty years of soup, and the market association posts news that could change everything.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 1. Bowls sold: 84. Weather: cold and steamy. Ama's mood: fine.*
1. Lin explains that you smell Lantern Lane Night Market before you see it: Uncle Feng's oyster omelets, sugar burning at Auntie Ping's tea machine, and under everything the deep brown smell of Stall 19, star anise, soy sauce, ginger and beef, and forty years of her grandmother refusing to write anything down. Lin is eleven; she loves the market; she only doesn't love smelling like it at school.
2. The stall at 5 p.m.: four small tables and plastic stools, one enormous pot on a gas burner, Ama's towel over her shoulder, the old dented brass ladle. Ama greets everyone with "Chia̍h-pá-buē?" (Have you eaten yet?), even the mailman, even people who have plainly just eaten. Lin's jobs: wipe tables, count bowls, carry, and keep the ledger.
3. It is the Winter Solstice, the shortest day, and Ama says a person becomes a year older tonight. Between customers Lin eats a bowl of sweet tangyuan, pink and white rice balls in ginger syrup, and Ama tells her the roundness means the family stays together. Lin counts the balls (nine), out of habit.
4. Busy night: Uncle Feng shouts a joke across the aisle; Auntie Ping hands Lin a free bubble tea "for the accountant"; Old Mr. Gao's shrimp pond splashes; a tourist with a camera points at the pot and Ama ladles a taste and says "Taste first. Talk later." By 9 p.m. Lin has written 84 in the ledger, the best night this month.
5. At closing time the market association chairman pins a notice to the temple board at the end of the lane. Lin reads it over an old man's shoulder: a big new mall, Sky Mall, opens after the New Year, and after the Lantern Festival the city will review whether Lantern Lane may stay open. Lin looks at the steam rising from the pot and, for the first time, feels the whole lane hold its breath.

## 2. The Girl Who Smelled Like Soup
Synopsis: Lin is teased at school about the smell of the stall and tries to scrub it away.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 3. Bowls sold: 71. Weather: drizzle. Times Lin washed her hair: 3.*
1. Morning at school: Lin's class 5-2, Ms. Wu at the board; Lin sits between Hui-Ting, tall with two long braids, and the window. Jun, spiky-haired and loud, leans over and sniffs: "Chen Yun-Lin smells like beef noodles." Half the class laughs. Lin sniffs her sleeve secretly and goes red.
2. Lin has always loved the smell of the stall and now cannot un-notice it. That night she washes her hair three times with Ma's fancy lavender shampoo and the bathroom smells like a flower shop that has lost an argument with a soup pot. Ma, a receptionist at a dental clinic with a hair clip and glasses, leans on the door and says quietly, "Star anise is not a shame."
3. A new girl joins the class, Yi-Chen, from Taipei, in a pink puffy vest and neat shoes, who lives near where the new mall is going up. She says the night market sounds "so old-fashioned" and that in Taipei everything is "clean and digital". Lin wants very badly to be the kind of person who could say something clever back and says "Oh." instead.
4. At the stall, Lin sees it with Yi-Chen's eyes: the plastic stools, the tarp, the chipped bowls, the smoke. In her notebook she draws a redesign: sleek white counters, a menu screen, a number ticket machine. She hides the page when Ama shuffles past with a stack of bowls.
5. Ama hums the old song she always hums while she cooks, and Lin realizes the song is the only thing in the redesign she cannot draw. She tears out the page, folds it into a small square and puts it in her pocket. It stays there for weeks.

## 3. A Handful
Synopsis: Lin tries to write down Ama's recipe and discovers that Ama does not measure anything.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 6. Bowls sold: 79. Weather: cold. Star anise: a handful. Which handful: unknown.*
1. Lin decides, as a scientist and future owner of a sleek white counter, to write down the recipe: "if this stall is going to survive, somebody has to know how it works." She waits by the great pot with her notebook and the pencil from behind her ear.
2. Ama, without looking, adds soy sauce ("until it is the right brown"), a piece of ginger ("this big"), star anise ("a handful"), sugar ("a bit"), and salt ("taste first, talk later"). Lin asks how much a handful is. Ama holds out her hand. It is the size of a small shovel. Lin writes: HANDFUL (AMA'S) and draws the hand.
3. Ama lets Lin taste the broth and asks what is missing. Lin says "salt?" and Ama shakes her head. "Wait." Lin tastes again: what is missing is something sharp and bright. Ama drops in a thin curl of dried tangerine peel. It is, Lin discovers, exactly what was missing. "How did you know?" "My tongue told me."
4. The eight-spice pouch: Ama shows Lin the small cloth bag that goes into the pot, made for Ama every month by Mr. Tsai, the spice merchant by the temple. Star anise, cinnamon, fennel, Sichuan peppercorn, licorice root, tangerine peel, bay leaf and clove. Lin sniffs it and for a moment forgets to be embarrassed about the smell.
5. When Ama lifts the pot lid, Lin notices her right hand tremble, just slightly. Ama says the steam is in her eyes, though the steam is nowhere near her hand. Lin writes in the corner of the notebook, small: ASK MA.

## 4. The Wrist
Synopsis: Ama hurts her wrist lifting the great pot, and Lin volunteers to run the stall.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 9. Bowls sold: 0 (stall closed). Ama's wrist: 1. Ama's mood: not fine.*
1. On Night 9 it happens in a second: Ama lifts the great pot to drain the bones, there is a crack like a twig, and she sets the pot down on the ground very carefully and says a word in Hokkien that Lin has never heard and understands perfectly. Uncle Feng and Auntie Ping run over. The night market stalls go quiet for a breath.
2. At the clinic where Ma works, the doctor says a bad sprain and a small crack: no lifting for three weeks. Ama refuses to hear it. "Nineteen has never been closed on a Saturday in forty years." Ba is away on a long delivery route and can only be reached by phone; he says on speaker, "Ama, please, sit."
3. At the apartment table, the family argues in two languages. Ma says close the stall for three weeks. Ama says the regulars will forget her. Lin hears herself say, "I can run it." Everyone stops. She has to say it again. She lists what she knows: the ledger, the tables, the pot, the tangerine peel. She does not list what she does not know.
4. Uncle Feng and Auntie Ping, who have drifted in the door with a bag of oranges, offer help: Feng will lift the pot, Ping will send tea, and everybody in the lane will keep an eye out. Ama stares at the ceiling for a long time. She says she will sit in the boss chair and supervise. She says it like a general handing over a fortress that is on fire.
5. That night Lin lies awake and rehearses the steps of a stall she has done a hundred times as an assistant and never once as the boss. Her sleeve smells of star anise. This time she does not sniff it away.

## 5. The Boss Chair
Synopsis: Lin's first night in charge is a mess, and then one bowl goes right.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 10. Bowls sold: 23. Bowls spilled: 4. Bowls praised: 1.*
1. Lin lights the burner, and the flame goes out. She lights it again. Ama, in the boss chair with a wrapped wrist, says "Not so much gas" and "More" in the same breath. Uncle Feng lifts the pot with a grunt. Lin ties the red-and-white checked apron twice around her, which turns out to be one time too many.
2. The first customers: a family of five and a man in a suit. Lin forgets the noodles, and they turn to a gluey lump; she spills a bowl onto the man's shoe; she puts sugar in the soup instead of salt and Ama, sniffing from her chair, says quietly, "Taste, Lin." Lin tastes it. She wants to sink into the broth.
3. Four bowls spilled, twenty-three sold, a queue that mostly leaves. Lin turns her face to the wall of the stall where the steam is thickest so nobody can tell if she is crying. Hui-Ting appears with her grandmother, who says nothing but sits down at table two and waits patiently to be sad on Lin's behalf.
4. Old Mr. Gao, the shrimp-fishing man in his straw hat, comes late for his tenth bowl of the week. He slurps, thinks, slurps again. "Tastes like your Ama," he says. "A little slower." Lin says that is not a compliment. Mr. Gao says at his age it is the best one there is.
5. When the last customer leaves, Ama, without a word, hands Lin the old dented brass ladle, the one that was her mother's. It is warm from the pot. "Again tomorrow," Ama says. Lin writes 23 in the ledger. It is the worst number she has ever written and the one she is proudest of.

# PART TWO: SIMMER

## 6. The Recipe Project
Synopsis: Lin measures Ama's handfuls with a spoon and finds the recipe changes with the weather.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 14. Bowls sold: 52. A handful: 31 grams (approximately). Ama says: stop weighing my hands.*
1. Lin borrows a kitchen scale and a set of measuring spoons from Ma and starts the Recipe Project. Every time Ama, from the boss chair, calls out "a handful", Lin catches the handful in a tin bowl and weighs it. Ama watches her weigh the star anise as if Lin were counting her teeth.
2. The numbers do not agree: a handful of star anise is 31 grams on Tuesday, 22 on Wednesday and 38 on Thursday. Lin is outraged. "Which one is right?" Ama says, "The one the weather wants." When it is colder she adds more ginger and cinnamon; when it is humid she uses less sugar.
3. Lin finally understands that the recipe is not a list, it is a conversation: what the day tastes like, what the customers need, how the beef is that week. She writes in her notebook: RECIPE = LISTENING. She underlines it twice.
4. Ama says the first thing Lin ever gets right is her nose, and Lin's nose tells her the broth needs something before her head does. Ama shows her how to skim the pot: the foam on top must be scooped away slowly, otherwise the broth gets cloudy. Lin practices, and skims a whole spoon of beef by mistake and eats it, out of respect.
5. Customers begin to ask for "the girl's bowl" versus "Ama's bowl". Lin pretends she is not thrilled. Ama pretends not to hear. On the ledger, in her own careful pencil, Ama writes a small tick beside the bowl count: a new column. Lin asks what it means. "Good," Ama says, and turns away to wipe a table that is already clean.

## 7. Hui-Ting Can't Eat Here
Synopsis: Hui-Ting explains why she has never eaten at the stall, and Lin has her first idea of her own.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 18. Bowls sold: 60. Vegetarian customers turned away: 1. Lin's mood: bad.*
1. Hui-Ting, Lin's tall quiet best friend, walks around the market with her every week, and has never once eaten at Stall 19. On Night 18, the first day of the lunar month, Hui-Ting stops in front of the stall, hungry, and Lin realizes why: she eats vegetarian on the 1st and 15th of every lunar month, because of her grandmother, and often more than that.
2. Hui-Ting explains, gently, that she does not just avoid meat: her grandmother's kind of cooking also avoids garlic, onions, chives and leeks, "the five pungent things", because in her family's faith they are said to stir up the senses and make it harder to be calm. Lin had thought vegetarian meant "no beef". It means, she now sees, a whole other way of cooking.
3. Lin looks at her own stall's menu: beef noodles, beef noodles with tendon, beef noodles with extra beef. She has to send her best friend to the far end of the lane for plain rice and pickled vegetables while she eats her bowl at the table across from her, and the tangerine peel suddenly tastes like guilt.
4. Lin's mind starts to race. What if there were a broth with dried mushrooms and kelp and roasted tomatoes, and the eight-spice pouch, and no beef and no onions at all? She whispers it to Hui-Ting, who says carefully, "I would come every week."
5. She takes the idea to Ama, who says, in the same tone as one who says the sun is cold: "Beef noodle soup without beef is a fish without water." Lin says that it isn't beef noodle soup, it's something new. Ama says something new is how you lose something old. Lin closes her mouth and walks away, and neither of them hums that night.

## 8. Shrimp, Rings and Balloons
Synopsis: A night off at the games with Hui-Ting and Jun ends with Jun's secret.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 25. Bowls sold: 66. Shrimp caught: 2. Prize won: one pink plastic whistle.*
1. Ma tells Lin she is working too hard and sends her out on a Saturday evening to play. Lin and Hui-Ting stroll along the games end of the lane: balloon darts, air-rifle shooting, a ring toss where the rings are exactly one millimeter too small for the bottles, and Old Mr. Gao's shrimp-fishing pond glowing under its strings of bulbs.
2. Jun appears out of nowhere, loud as ever, and says he is here "for research", which means he wants to play too. They rent three rods and fish for shrimp in a shallow tank for a whole ticket. Hui-Ting catches two immediately, with a quiet, terrifying patience; Lin catches a shoe; Jun hooks his own sleeve. They shriek with laughter.
3. Mr. Gao grills the two shrimp right there on a little grill with salt; the shrimp are smoky and sweet. Jun eats his half with his eyes closed. He says, out of nowhere, that he used to come here every night with his father, who had a fried-chicken stall at the corner until last year. It closed. He says "closed" like a word he does not want to touch.
4. Lin has been laughing at Jun's noise in class and never thought of the reason. Jun says he teased her about the soup smell because he was jealous: her stall is still open. Hui-Ting says nothing, and just quietly slides the whistle prize into Jun's hand. Jun blows it. It makes an awful, wonderful sound that makes every stall keeper turn round.
5. Walking home, Lin thinks that shame looks very different from the inside than the outside. She takes the crumpled redesign from her pocket and looks at it under a streetlamp. Then she puts it back. She isn't ready to throw it away yet, and she is not sure why.

## 9. The Reunion Dinner
Synopsis: The whole family gathers for Lunar New Year's Eve, and Lin receives a red envelope and a lesson in speaking Ama's language.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 47. Market closed. Bowls sold: 0. Dumplings eaten: too many. Red envelopes received: 1.*
1. The market closes for the New Year. Lin and Ma clean the apartment top to bottom; Ba arrives home from his route in a huge delivery truck at last, tired and sunburned in winter, carrying a bag of oranges and a fish. The small ancestor shelf at the end of the corridor gets fresh fruit, incense and Agong's photograph.
2. The reunion dinner (nián yè fàn, Lin says, because Ma says it): a whole steamed fish, "surplus"; dumplings folded by all four of them at the table, "wealth"; and sticky rice cake, "getting higher every year". Ama supervises the dumpling folding with a critical eye and folds forty faster than Lin can fold five. Ba tells a story about his truck and a runaway goat.
3. Ba and Ama talk to each other in Hokkien over the fish, fast and warm, with a laughing rhythm Lin loves and cannot follow. She understands most of it, but every time she tries to answer in Hokkien her tongue goes stiff. Ama waits, patiently, and Lin says in Hokkien, badly, "Ama, I want to learn." Ama laughs so hard she has to put down her chopsticks. It is not a mocking laugh.
4. Ama gives her a red envelope with both hands, the way you are meant to, and Lin takes it with both hands, the way you are meant to. Inside there is money, an even, lucky amount, and a folded slip of paper with two characters in Ama's shaky handwriting: 食飽, chia̍h-pá, "eat until you are full". It is what Ama says instead of I love you, and it took her an hour to write.
5. Lin sits under the lantern on the balcony with the fireworks popping over the roofs and thinks that she never told Ama that the smell of the stall is the best smell in the world. She tells herself she will say it tomorrow. She doesn't know that tomorrow will not be the right day to say it.

## 10. The Man Who Made the Pouch
Synopsis: The market reopens, and Mr. Tsai announces he is retiring, which could end Ama's recipe.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 50. Bowls sold: 88. Mr. Tsai's shop: closing. Ama's mood: (she would not let me write it).*
1. The market reopens for the new year, and the lane is at its most glorious: red banners, extra lanterns, families in new clothes. Stall 19 sells 88 bowls, and Ama is out of the boss chair for the first time, standing with her wrist in a brace, supervising in a very loud voice.
2. In the middle of the rush, Mr. Tsai, the spice merchant, shuffles up to the stall, in his cardigan and thick glasses with his abacus in his pocket, and quietly puts down a fresh cloth pouch on the counter. It is the last one of the month. He says he is eighty-four, his hands are tired, his son in the city wants him to come and live with them. The shop closes at the end of the month.
3. Ama does not say anything. She holds the pouch and looks at it as if it might disappear. Lin sees her hand tighten on the cloth. "For forty years," Ama says at last. "You did not tell me you would stop." Mr. Tsai says he did not know how to say it, and Ama says, "You just did."
4. That night Lin, wiping the counter, asks Ama what will happen to the stall without the pouches. Ama says "I will find another spice shop." Lin says no one makes it like Mr. Tsai. Ama says nothing. It is the first time Lin has ever heard her grandmother sound afraid.
5. Lin looks at the dozens of tiny fragrant things in the last pouch: star anise, cinnamon bark, fennel, a peppercorn, a licorice root, tangerine peel, a bay leaf, a clove. Eight things. She thinks, if I know what's in it and Mr. Tsai knows how much, then maybe. She writes in the notebook: ASK MR. TSAI. Then, after a pause: DON'T TELL AMA YET.

# PART THREE: BOIL OVER

## 11. The Abacus
Synopsis: Mr. Tsai teaches Lin his blend, and she learns it is not a secret but a lifetime of small changes.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 52. Bowls sold: 74. Spices pounded: 4 kilos. Lin's fingers: orange.*
1. Lin visits the tiny herb shop across from the temple at five in the morning before school; it smells of licorice and old wood and a hundred labelled drawers. Mr. Tsai is sitting on a stool clicking his abacus. When Lin says, straight out, "Please teach me the blend", he laughs and says he has been waiting forty years for someone to ask.
2. He shows her the old ledger, a brown book in three different handwritings, his father's, his own and one that is Ama's, from when she first came to the shop as a young woman with her mother's list. The proportions change on almost every page: a little less cinnamon in 1988, more licorice in 1993, a new clove in 2004. "There is no secret," he says. "There is only what the pot wanted that year."
3. He teaches her to roast: toasting the peppercorns and fennel in a dry wok until they smell like sunshine on a hot road; to pound: the cinnamon bark in a heavy stone mortar until her arms shake; to blend: by nose first and abacus later. Lin's fingers turn orange from the dried tangerine peel.
4. Mr. Tsai shows her that the spices are not just for smell: star anise for sweetness and warmth, cinnamon for depth, Sichuan pepper for a tingling lift, licorice for roundness. "A blend," he says, "is a small orchestra. Nobody plays too loud." Lin, who has never liked music class, thinks she might like this.
5. She makes her first pouch. It smells almost right. Mr. Tsai sniffs it, nods, and says one more thing: "Almost is the word for a beginning." He gives her the abacus. "Not for the blend. For the rest of your life. Count everything, then trust your nose anyway."

## 12. Sky Mall
Synopsis: The new mall opens and the lane empties overnight.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 54. Bowls sold: 19. Sky Mall: open. Ama's mood: quiet.*
1. Sky Mall opens with flashing signs, a rooftop garden, free bubble tea for the first thousand customers and an air-conditioned food court with numbered tickets, sleek white counters and screens. Auntie Ping tells Lin, with shining eyes, that it looks exactly like the future. Lin remembers her folded redesign and feels a small sick feeling.
2. Lantern Lane empties like a bathtub. Stall 19 sells 19 bowls. Uncle Feng's omelet pan stays quiet for two whole hours. Old Mr. Gao sits in his shrimp-fishing booth telling terrible jokes to an empty pond. Jun's dad, who used to have the fried-chicken stall, is seen in a Sky Mall apron, carrying trays in the food court.
3. Yi-Chen shows up with her mother, holding a shiny paper bag from the mall. She says it's a shame, really, all this, and that the food court has "everything in one place". Lin, before she can stop herself, says the food court has everything except a grandmother. Yi-Chen looks surprised. For one second Lin feels great and then not great at all.
4. Auntie Ping says quietly that she has been offered a booth at Sky Mall. Everyone, without turning, listens. She says she won't decide until after the Lantern Festival. But her eyes, as she looks at the bubble tea machine, seem to belong to somebody else already.
5. Ama says nothing all night. She has stopped humming. She wipes the same table three times and the pot goes cold. Lin, watching, thinks about the paper in her pocket and understands with horror how much of what she wished for is now standing at the end of the lane, glowing.

## 13. Words
Synopsis: Lin says something she cannot take back, and runs away to the temple.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 55. Bowls sold: 21. Words said: 1 (too many).*
1. The next night, with the lane still nearly empty, Lin proposes changes to Ama: a small menu sign, a modern cash system, maybe a smaller bowl. "Customers like fast," she says. She takes out the folded redesign and unfolds it on the counter.
2. Ama looks at it for a long time: the white counters, the number tickets, the screen. Her voice is very soft: "You want it to be like everywhere." Lin, panicking, blurts the thing she has been holding for months: "I'm embarrassed, Ama. The smell. The stools. Jun said I smell like soup and I wanted to disappear. I wish, sometimes, we were different."
3. There is a terrible quiet. The sizzle of Uncle Feng's oil is very loud across the aisle. Ama puts down the ladle. She does not shout. "I see," she says, and turns away to face the pot, and her shoulders look smaller than Lin has ever seen them.
4. Lin runs. She runs down the empty lane, past the shrimp pond and the balloon-darts booth, all the way to the little temple to Mazu at the end. It is quiet inside: red lanterns, coils of incense smoke drifting up, oranges on the offering table. She kneels on the cool stone floor, not sure how to pray, and says out loud, "I didn't mean it. I did mean it. I don't know."
5. The temple keeper, a quiet man sweeping the floor, says nothing for a long time. Then he says, "Every family has a smell. Some of us hide it. Some of us hang it on a lantern." He hands her a paper wish tag and a pen. Lin holds it for a long time. She writes nothing. She walks home very slowly, and at the corner she sniffs her sleeve, and this time she cries.

## 14. What Ama Never Said
Synopsis: Ama tells Lin the story of her own recipe and the river of tradition.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 56. Bowls sold: 30. Stories told: 1. Ama's mood: talking.*
1. The next day is Sunday, and Lin does not leave the apartment. She sits at the apartment table, shoes still on, until Ama comes in from the balcony, where she has been sitting since dawn with her wrapped wrist in her lap. Ama says: "Come. Bring your notebook." Lin says she doesn't want to write anything down. Ama says: "Bring it anyway."
2. Ama tells the story, in her slow mixture of Mandarin and Hokkien, that Lin has to lean forward to catch: when Ama was eleven, the same age as Lin, her mother sold soup from a cart, and it was made with pork bones because beef was too expensive. Ama was ashamed of the cart, too. She hid behind the stove whenever girls from school walked by.
3. She tells how, at thirty, after Agong's factory closed, she opened Stall 19 and added beef when she could afford it, because customers asked; how she changed the broth every year, more ginger the winter the whole lane had colds, less sugar the year sugar cost too much. "There is no recipe," she says. "There is only a river. My mother stepped in first. I stepped in second. Somebody has to step in third, and the river will not look the same, and that is how you know it is still running."
4. Lin says nobody told her. Ama says nobody told Ama either. She holds out the tremble of her right hand and says the truth of it: the wrist will heal but her hands are not what they were, and she is afraid the stall will not survive her, and she was afraid that if she let Lin change anything, it would stop being hers. "I was wrong," Ama says. "A thing that cannot change is a thing already gone."
5. Lin puts her notebook on the table and, for the first time, does not write in it. She says she is sorry. Ama says she is sorry too. They sit in the warm noon light with the smell of star anise in the room, and Ama begins, very quietly, to hum.

## 15. One Hundred and Twelve Lanterns
Synopsis: Lin and her friends plan a Lantern Night to save the lane, and the whole market joins in.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 57. Bowls sold: 34. Lanterns made: 112. Volunteers: everyone.*
1. Lin brings the idea to Ms. Wu, then to the whole class: the city inspector, Ms. Xu, will visit on the night of the Lantern Festival. What if that night, Lantern Lane is the most beautiful place in the city, lit with hundreds of handmade lanterns, each stall telling a story, games for children, and a Lantern Trail with a riddle at each stall? Ms. Wu says she has always wanted to teach a lantern class. She pulls a bag of bamboo strips out of the cupboard as if she had been waiting.
2. Class 5-2 makes lanterns: bamboo frames, tissue paper and glue, a hundred sticky hands. Jun makes a rooster and it looks like a chicken who has met a tragedy; he is delighted. Yi-Chen, after watching a long time from the window, says quietly that she could draw the designs, and produces a hundred pictures with such care that the class falls silent.
3. The stall-keepers join in one by one: Uncle Feng promises a golden omelet lantern the size of a wheel; Old Mr. Gao makes a shrimp lantern and invents nine terrible riddles; Auntie Ping, after a long silence, says she will run a Lantern Tea tent, and she does not mention the mall. Mr. Tsai, on his last week, makes small spice sachets to hang beside every lantern.
4. Ms. Wu asks each child to write a wish on a paper tag to tie to the lantern strings. Lin looks at the temple keeper's tag in her pocket. She writes: I want to be proud, out loud. Then she folds it up small and ties it to the branch of the temple tree with the others.
5. Yi-Chen, packing up, asks if she can help with the stall. Lin, looking at the pink vest covered in glue and tissue paper, says: "Can you carry bowls?" Yi-Chen says she has never carried a bowl in her life. Lin gives her one. It is hot. Yi-Chen, holding it in both hands like a treasure, does not spill a drop.

# PART FOUR: LANTERN NIGHT

## 16. Nine Broths
Synopsis: Lin tries to make a vegetarian broth in Ama's method and fails eight times before the ninth.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 58. Bowls sold: 33. Broths tested: 9. Broths thrown out: 8.*
1. Lin decides the Lantern Night needs one new thing: a broth Hui-Ting can eat. Ama, in a brace, lends her a corner of the kitchen and says, to everyone's surprise, "Taste first. Talk later." Lin fills a small pot with water, dried shiitake mushrooms, a strip of kelp and a chunk of daikon, and simmers it for two hours.
2. Broth one is thin as tea. Broth two is fishy because she boiled the kelp too hard. Broth three tastes like a swamp. Ama tastes each one, nods, and says only "Almost", which is the word for a beginning. Lin's fingers are orange. Her hair smells like a soup pot again. She does not mind.
3. Hui-Ting comes to taste, honest to the bone. "Too sweet," she says of six. "Weird," of seven. "Like a swimming pool," of eight. Jun, who is carrying trays, tastes broth eight and says "This is the worst soup in the history of soup" and Lin laughs so hard she has to sit down on a stool.
4. Lin remembers Mr. Tsai's words about the blend being an orchestra. She thinks about what the broth is missing: not salt, not sugar, but something round and dark and slow. She tries roasted tomatoes and corn on the cob, charred a little on the flame, and puts in Ama's eight-spice pouch, which has no onion or garlic in it at all.
5. Broth nine. Ama tastes it. She closes her eyes. She tastes again. She says nothing for a long moment, then adds a single curl of tangerine peel and hands back the spoon. Hui-Ting takes a sip, and her eyes go wide, and she says, in a very small voice, "It tastes like your stall." Lin calls it the Lantern Broth. She writes down every measurement in her notebook and then, in the margin, writes: (or whatever the weather wants).

## 17. The Night Before
Synopsis: The final preparations, a wrist brace comes off and the lane holds its breath.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 60. Bowls sold: 40. Days until the review: 2. Wrist brace: off.*
1. Two days before Lantern Festival: the lane is a workshop. Uncle Feng builds a ladder of bamboo, Auntie Ping paints paper lanterns in rainbow colors, Mr. Gao's grandchildren tie tags. Ba arrives home early from his route with his truck full of bamboo poles he has picked up for the lane and says he took the long way round to be sure of getting them.
2. Ama's wrist brace comes off at the clinic. She flexes her hand: a little stiff, a little shaky, but a hand. She looks at the great pot, then at Lin. "You lift it," she says. It is a test and a gift at once. Lin lifts, wobbling, with Ba behind her, and Ama, on the bench, laughs, a sound like a kettle finding its voice.
3. That night Lin and Ama sit together on the plastic stools and prepare both broths, Ama's classic and Lin's Lantern Broth, side by side. Ama shows her how to skim the foam from Lin's pot and Lin shows Ama how to roast the corn. They work without speaking, in the steam, and it feels like an old song.
4. Ms. Xu, the inspector, visits the lane in the afternoon, in sensible shoes, with a notebook and a serious face. She asks a lot of questions, and does not eat anything, and leaves a business card with Mr. Tsai. Everyone in the lane watches her go. Uncle Feng whispers she is going to close us, and Auntie Ping whispers no, she isn't, and neither of them believes their own whisper.
5. The night before the Festival, the forecast says rain. Lin and Hui-Ting sit on the temple steps looking at the lanterns still unlit. Hui-Ting says her grandmother told her that a lantern is a wish you can see. Lin says she is scared of hoping too loudly. Hui-Ting says, "Then hope in a small voice. The lantern will do the rest."

## 18. Rain
Synopsis: Rain threatens the lanterns and the whole lane rallies.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 61. Bowls sold: 0 (rain). Umbrellas borrowed: 63. Lanterns saved: all but one.*
1. On the eve of the Festival, thick rain arrives at four in the afternoon, so hard the gutters run brown. The paper lanterns, hung all along the lane, begin to sag; the tissue starts to run red and yellow down the frames. Lin stands in the middle of the lane with a half-melted rooster lantern in her hands and does not know what to do.
2. Ama, without a word, starts pulling the tarps off her stall and covering the lanterns. Uncle Feng bellows for ladders. Auntie Ping rings every umbrella in the market. Jun, Yi-Chen and Hui-Ting run a chain of hands to pass lanterns down from the strings to dry indoors, in Mr. Tsai's shop, in the temple, even in the arcade.
3. For three hours the whole lane works together, in the rain, laughing and soaked: shop owners and school kids, Yi-Chen's mother, even Jun's father, still in his Sky Mall apron, who joins the chain. Ama, at the end of a line of hands, in her plastic sandals and floral vest, is handed a lantern, and passes it on, and looks at Lin, and smiles.
4. By eight, the rain thins into mist. Sixty-three umbrellas lean in a pile against the temple wall. All the lanterns are saved except one: Jun's rooster, which has melted into a mild abstract shape. Jun says it looks better this way. Nobody argues.
5. Lin, drenched, with a wet fringe in her eyes, goes home and does not sleep. She lies on the balcony with the rain-washed air smelling of wet stone and star anise, and holds the old brass ladle. She knows one thing for certain. Tomorrow she will serve two bowls of soup to a stranger, and both of them will be hers.

## 19. Lantern Night
Synopsis: The Lantern Festival: the lane blazes with light, and Ms. Xu tastes both broths.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 62. Lantern Festival. Bowls sold: I lost count. Weather: lanterns.*
1. At sunset the clouds break, and the lane lights up all at once: hundreds of lanterns, red and gold and rooster-shaped, hanging in long rivers over the stalls; riddles pinned to strings; children running the Lantern Trail with their stamp cards; the smell of tangyuan, oyster omelets, sugar and star anise carried on the warm air. Lin has never seen it so beautiful, and she has lived here all her life.
2. The crowds arrive slowly and then all at once: families from Sky Mall and from across the city, tourists, old people, whole classes. Stall 19 has a line that curls round the corner. Ama stands at the pot in her floral vest. Lin ladles beside her. Yi-Chen carries bowls. Jun runs trays. Hui-Ting and her grandmother take the first table and order two bowls of Lantern Broth.
3. Ms. Xu arrives at eight in her sensible shoes. She watches for a while, then sits at table two. Lin brings her two bowls: Ama's classic beef noodle soup and her own Lantern Broth. Ms. Xu, who has not eaten anything in the lane all week, tastes the first, closes her eyes, then tastes the second. "They are not the same," she says. "No. They are family," says Lin.
4. Ms. Xu asks who taught her. Lin says, "My Ama. And Mr. Tsai. And a river." Ms. Xu smiles slowly and writes something in her notebook. When she is done, she stands up, and to Lin's astonishment, bows a little to Ama across the steam, and says she will have a third bowl.
5. At the end of the night, Ama takes the old brass ladle, dents and all, and holds it out to Lin, and for the first time in forty years stands back from the pot. Lin ladles the last bowl of the night, a bowl of Ama's classic, with a curl of tangerine peel. She hands it to Ama. "Chia̍h-pá-buē?" Lin says. Ama laughs and says, "Not yet. I was waiting for you to ask," and eats it standing at her own stall as if it were the first bowl she had ever tasted.

## 20. Night Sixty-Three
Synopsis: The city's decision, the lane's future and the last ledger entry.
Header: *Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 63. Bowls sold: 90. Lane: kept (for now). Ama's mood: (she told me to write "proud"; I wrote it myself).*
1. The morning after the Festival, Lantern Lane looks like a party's morning: paper scraps, drooping lanterns, smoke, sleepy dogs, and sticky floors. The whole lane is asleep on its feet. Ms. Xu's letter arrives at the association at noon, and everyone, still in their aprons, crowds round it in the middle of the aisle.
2. The city has decided: Lantern Lane will stay open for a year, on trial, and will hold an official Lantern Night every year on the Lantern Festival. Ms. Xu also writes that she would like the market's cooks to record their recipes for the city's food heritage archive, "in whatever way suits them." Uncle Feng cheers so loudly that a lantern falls off a string.
3. Auntie Ping announces she will take the Sky Mall booth on weekday afternoons and keep her tea stall at night. "Both," she says. "A person can have both." Mr. Tsai, on his last day, hangs a small lantern in his empty shop window and says that he and Lin will roast Ama's spices together in the back of Stall 19 every month, and that he will sit in the boss chair and supervise. Jun's father, at the edge of the crowd, asks Uncle Feng if there might be room for a fried-chicken cart at the end of the lane. Uncle Feng says he thought he would never ask.
4. Lin and Ama sit on the plastic stools in the emptied stall, counting the night's takings. Ama tells Lin that she is going to teach her Hokkien properly, one word a night, and that Lin should write it in the ledger. Lin says there is only one column left. Ama says: "Then make a new one." Lin adds it, with a ruler, and writes at the top of the column: A word a night.
5. That evening Lin writes the last line of the season in the ledger: Night 63. Bowls sold: 90. Lane: kept (for now). Ama's mood: proud. Then she adds, in smaller letters: Lin's mood: the same. Her hair smells of star anise. She sniffs her sleeve one last time, and smiles.
