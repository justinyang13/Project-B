# LIN AND THE NIGHT MARKET LANTERNS — story bible

Warm, funny, sensory family story for ages 10-12, set in a night market in southern Taiwan over the sixty-odd nights between the Winter Solstice and the Lantern Festival. Eleven-year-old Lin helps run her grandmother's beef-noodle stall. Nothing spooky, no violence, no romance, no real villain. The stakes are family, pride and belonging: a shame Lin feels about the stall, a wrist injury that puts her in charge of it, a shiny new mall that empties the market, and a city review that could close the lane. The book should make a reader hungry, curious about Taiwan and proud of where they come from.
IMPORTANT (user rule): the words "Taiwanese", "Chinese", "Japanese", "Korean" must NOT appear in the title, subtitle, cover text, tagline or blurb. Culture comes through names, food, places, customs and language in the story itself. (The story may name Taiwan or a city once or twice as a place, naturally.)

## Voice
FIRST PERSON, PAST TENSE, narrated by LIN (Chen Yun-Lin), 11, looking back on that winter. Warm, sensory, funny and a little bossy: a girl who notices smells, sounds and steam first, loves lists and numbers, and explains her world to the reader as if the reader has just walked into the market beside her ("You have to understand that..."). She compares things to food. She is quick to be embarrassed and quicker to laugh at herself. Short, vivid sentences; real feeling arrives plainly. Not tourist-guide voice: nothing is exotic to Lin, it is just home, and what she explains she explains with love and jokes.
Every chapter opens with one italic line from AMA'S STALL LEDGER, on its own line, then a blank line, in this exact form: "*Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 1. Bowls sold: 84. Weather: cold and steamy. Ama's mood: fine.*" The entries are terse and funny, the night number rises (Night 1 = Winter Solstice; see below), the bowl counts rise and fall with the plot, and the last entry answers the first. Night 1 = Winter Solstice, Lunar New Year's Eve = about Night 47, Lantern Festival = Night 62, final entry Night 63. The Header line is added by the script: never write it on any page.
Voice sample:
"*Ama's Stall Ledger, Night 1. Bowls sold: 84. Weather: cold and steamy. Ama's mood: fine.*

Before you see the Lantern Lane Night Market, you smell it. First the smoke from Uncle Feng's oyster omelets, then sugar burning on Auntie Ping's tea machine, then, underneath everything, a deep brown smell like a warm coat: star anise, soy sauce, ginger, beef, and forty years of my grandmother refusing to write anything down. That last one is our stall.

I was eleven that winter, and I want it on the record that I loved the market. I only didn't love smelling like it at school."

## The premise
Chen Yun-Lin (Lin) lives with her Ama (paternal grandmother), Ma and Ba in a narrow apartment above a shuttered shop, one lane from the Lantern Lane Night Market in a southern Taiwanese city. Since before Lin was born, Ama has run stall number nineteen: Chen Ama's Beef Noodles, four tables, one huge pot, no written recipe. Lin works the stall after school and secretly wishes it were "modern": at school her classmate Jun teased that she smells like soup, and she now scrubs her hair three times. On Winter Solstice night the market association posts news: a new mall (Sky Mall) is opening, and after the Lantern Festival the city will review whether the lane should stay open. Then Ama sprains her wrist lifting the great pot, and Lin, with help from the market's stall-keepers, has to run the stall. She starts a notebook to measure Ama's "a handful" recipe (her own way), a vegetarian friend (Hui-Ting) shows her the stall has nothing for people like her, Mr. Tsai the spice merchant announces he is retiring (his blend is the stall's heart), Sky Mall opens and the lane empties, Lin says something hurtful to Ama about being embarrassed, and Ama tells the story of how her own recipe was never fixed: it changed every year, and every cook stepped into a river someone else began. Lin and her friends (and the whole lane) plan a Lantern Night for the last weekend; Lin makes a vegetarian "Lantern Broth" in Ama's method, serves Ama's classic and hers to the city inspector, and the lane is kept.

## Cast (keep looks and habits EXACTLY consistent)
- **Lin (Chen Yun-Lin)**, 11: small, round-faced, chin-length straight black hair with straight bangs, bright quick eyes, a dark green padded winter jacket and, at the stall, a red-and-white checked apron; a small spiral notebook and a pencil behind her ear. Loves lists, numbers and tasting; bossy, funny, easily embarrassed. Habit: counts bowls, steps and seconds; sniffs her sleeve.
- **Ama**, Lin's paternal grandmother, 70s: small and sturdy, short silver hair in tight curls, a floral padded vest over a long-sleeved shirt, an apron, socks with plastic sandals, a towel over one shoulder, strong squarish hands (one later in a wrist brace). Sharp-tongued and tender, hums old songs while she cooks, never measures ("a handful", "until it is right"), says "Chia̍h-pá-buē?" (Have you eaten yet?) as a greeting to everyone. Refrain: "Taste first. Talk later."
- **Ma (Lin Mei-Ling)**, the receptionist at a dental clinic: tired, funny, neat, glasses, hair in a clip. **Ba (Chen Zhi-Hao)**, a long-distance delivery-truck driver: big, quiet, home on weekends, hums badly; he speaks Taiwanese Hokkien with Ama.
- **Uncle Feng**: round, loud-laughing oyster-omelet cook with a bandana, forearms like ham; **Auntie Ping**: runs the bubble-tea stall, glitter nails, a headband with two tiny pom-poms, always on her phone, loves new ideas; **Mr. Tsai**, 80s: runs the tiny herb-and-spice shop across from the temple, thick glasses, a cardigan, a wooden abacus, speaks slowly; makes Ama's eight-spice pouch; retiring. **Old Mr. Gao** runs the shrimp-fishing pond and ring-toss games, wears a straw hat, tells terrible jokes. **Ms. Xu** (from the city's culture bureau): brisk, kind, notebook, sensible shoes; fair; she has never eaten at the market.
- **Hui-Ting**, 11, Lin's best friend: tall, glasses, two long braids, a soft voice, a Buddhist grandmother; eats vegetarian on the 1st and 15th of the lunar month (and often more). **Jun**, 11: loud, skinny, spiky hair, a class clown who teased Lin; his family's fried-chicken stall closed last year and he is ashamed; ends up carrying trays. **Yi-Chen**, 11: a transfer student from Taipei in neat clothes and a pink puffy vest, lives near the new mall; snobbish at first, lonely, becomes a friend. **Ms. Wu** (Wu laoshi), the class teacher: warm, brisk, cardigan, pencil in her bun.
- Places: Lantern Lane Night Market (a long lane of stalls under strings of red and yellow lanterns, opens at five, busiest seven to ten, closes near midnight); Stall 19; the shrimp-fishing pond; the little temple to Mazu (sea goddess) at the end of the lane; Mr. Tsai's shop; the apartment with the small ancestor shelf; Lin's school; Sky Mall (glass, air conditioning, food court).

## Cultural notes (each web-verified 2026-09-25; keep the book consistent with these)
- Night markets: usually open about 4-5 pm, busiest 7-10 pm, vendors close after 11 pm to around midnight; stalls set up carts, tarps and plastic stools. Classic foods: beef noodle soup (national dish; red-braised style with soy sauce, star anise, cinnamon, doubanjiang chili bean paste, ginger, garlic, onion, daikon; braised at least 2 hours), oyster omelet, stinky tofu with pickled cabbage, scallion pancakes (traditionally with lard), braised pork rice (lu rou fan), bubble tea (invented in Taiwan), black pepper buns, fried chicken cutlet, tangyuan. Two beef-noodle styles: red-braised (hong shao) and clear broth (qing dun). Families each have their own version.
- Night market games: ring toss, balloon darts, air-gun shooting, goldfish/shrimp fishing (rent a rod; shrimp caught may be grilled; goldfish scooping uses paper scoops that tear), pinball-style games, prize raffles.
- Winter Solstice (Dongzhi): families eat tangyuan (glutinous-rice balls, pink/white/green, in sweet soup); roundness = togetherness; you become "a year older"; offerings to ancestors.
- Lunar New Year: reunion dinner on New Year's Eve at home (fish for surplus, dumplings for wealth, sticky rice cake for higher achievement); red envelopes (hongbao) from elders to children, given/received with both hands, even amounts (2, 6, 8 lucky); red = luck.
- Lantern Festival: 15th day of the first lunar month, ends the New Year; lanterns, riddles, games, wishes written on lanterns; tangyuan / yuanxiao eaten; Pingxi sky lanterns (wishes written on them).
- Mid-Autumn: barbecue is a modern Taiwanese custom; pomelo (also a hat!). (Not used unless mentioned in memory.)
- Buddhist vegetarian food (su shi) in Taiwan: devotees may eat vegetarian on the 1st and 15th of the lunar month, at temple festivals, or permanently; strict Buddhist vegetarian cooking also avoids the five pungent alliums (garlic, onions, chives, scallions/green onions, leeks). So Hui-Ting's grandmother's vegetarian food has NO garlic, onion or scallion; a vegetarian broth for her must skip them (rely on dried shiitake, kelp, daikon, roasted tomato, corn, ginger and Ama's spice pouch, which is free of alliums).
- Mazu: sea goddess, Taiwan's most widely worshipped; big pilgrimage (Dajia) in spring; a small Mazu temple at a market's end is realistic. Temples: incense, bowing, offerings of fruit.
- Language: Ama and Ba speak Taiwanese Hokkien; Lin understands it and speaks Mandarin, with some Hokkien; Ama = grandma (a-má; Ama), Agong = grandpa (a-kong). Greeting "Chia̍h-pá-buē?" (Have you eaten yet?) is how Hokkien speakers say hello. Mandarin: xièxie (thank you), lǎoshī (teacher). Use few, gloss naturally in context, and never invent words.
- Names: family name first in Taiwan (Chen Yun-Lin); market people call elders Ama/Auntie/Uncle. Chen is a very common surname.

## Vocabulary and learning (build in naturally, never as a lecture)
- Target English words (in context, ~3 per chapter): aroma, simmer, ladle, ledger, vendor, proprietor, generations, heirloom, ancestor, tradition, adapt, ingredient, ferment, blend, apprentice, inspector, fragrant, steam, sizzle, seasoning, wok, pinch, "a handful", stubborn, humble, festival, custom, review, permit, lease, gradually, reluctant, resourceful, humid.
- Facts the reader learns: how a broth is built (simmering extracts flavor; aromatics; why ginger and star anise), what tangyuan and the solstice mean, how the lunar calendar sets festivals, red-envelope manners, how a night market runs, a few Hokkien/Mandarin words, why a Buddhist family may eat vegetarian, that recipes and traditions change and are alive.
- Kid-doable idea at the end (optional): a simple broth or tangyuan idea, described in story, not as a recipe box.

## The plan of the story
Ch1-5: the market and Ama's stall on Solstice night; school shame; Ama does not measure; the wrist; Lin's first night in charge. Ch6-10: Lin's measuring notebook; Hui-Ting and the vegetarian idea; games and Jun's secret; Lunar New Year reunion dinner and the red envelope; Mr. Tsai retiring. Ch11-15: Mr. Tsai teaches Lin his blend; the mall opens and the lane empties; the hurtful words and the temple; Ama's story (the recipe was always changing); the Lantern Night plan. Ch16-20: the vegetarian Lantern Broth; the rain; the inspector; Lantern Night; the lane kept; Night 57.

## Motifs
The Stall Ledger headers; steam; Ama's "a handful" and "Taste first, talk later"; Lin's measuring spoon and notebook; Chia̍h-pá-buē?; the old brass ladle (dented, Ama's mother's); lanterns; round things (tangyuan, moon, pot); Ama humming; Lin sniffing her sleeve.

## The lesson (dramatize; NEVER preach)
Honor where you come from and make it your own: be proud of your family's ways, and let them grow through you. Tradition is a river: someone stepped in first, and you may step in too and carry it further. One plain line near the end at most (Ama's river line). Shame is not solved by hiding the thing; it is solved by loving it out loud. The ending is honest: the lane is kept on trial, not saved for ever.

## Things to avoid
No spooky content (no ghost month, no ghost stories). No violence, romance or real villain (Ms. Xu and the mall are not evil; Sky Mall simply exists). No exoticizing or tourist-guide voice, no stereotypes or jokes at anyone's expense; treat Ama's Hokkien, food and temple customs with respect and precision. Do not put the words Taiwanese/Chinese/Japanese/Korean in the title, cover or blurb. Never invent Hokkien or Mandarin words: use only the verified list above. No stacked similes; avoid "It wasn't X. It was Y."; avoid "heart hammered", "stone in the throat".
