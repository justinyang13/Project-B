# PART ONE: LIGHT WIND FROM THE WEST

## 1. Smallest Sparrow in the Hedge
Synopsis: Pip, the smallest sparrow in Thistlewick Hedge, is told again that small birds stay low; she decides on the top twig that she will be a Storm-Sparrow.
Header: *Sky Report: light wind from the west, clear, one very small bird with very big ideas.*
1. Open with the voice-sample opening: Pip, the smallest sparrow in Thistlewick Hedge, on the top twig with Uncle Dorn's daily "smallest she'll stay," weighing less than a walnut, looking at the sky and thinking "I'll have that one."
2. Morning in the hedge: a busy, funny sketch of sparrow life, the bickering, the seed-hunting, the gossip of Mother Wren; Pip's crooked left tail feather from the nest fall; how the fledglings measure themselves in wingbeats. Cinder, large and glossy, does a show-off loop and everyone applauds. Pip's own loop comes out as a spin and a landing in a puddle. Laughter.
3. Pip's history in a few warm strokes: hatched a week late, the runt; her parents lost in a storm two autumns ago; Uncle Dorn, plump and gloomy, raising her with more grumbling than any bird ever grumbled and more love than he could say. "Small birds stay low," he says, as he tucks her under his wing at night. Pip, half asleep, doesn't argue. She has stopped arguing. She has started planning.
4. Nettle, her best friend, skinny and freckled with too-big feet, tells her the news in a whisper: the Elders are announcing the Trial of Three Winds at sundown. Only the strongest fledglings are ever chosen as Storm-Sparrows, the daring scouts who ride the autumn gales ahead of the flock. Pip's heart flips over like a leaf.
5. That evening at the Gathering Rock, Elder Marra announces the Trial. Every fledgling may enter, she says, "except those the Elders judge unready." Her eye passes over Pip and stops for the tiniest instant. Hook: Pip says quietly to the wind, "I'm entering." The wind, for a wonder, seems to lift a feather in reply.

## 2. Small Birds Stay Low
Synopsis: Everyone tells Pip she can't. She decides she will anyway.
Header: *Sky Report: rising wind, gusty, a hedge full of opinions.*
1. The morning after the announcement, the whole hedge talks. Mother Wren says Pip should stick to worm-finding. The older fledglings hop around her, telling her she's too light and will be blown into next week. Pip smiles and says, "Tweet-truth? Then I'll go there."
2. Uncle Dorn corners her behind the honeysuckle, ruffled and pale. He says she can't go in the Trial: the wind is too rough, the sky is too big, and the Storm-Sparrows are the ones who don't come back. It is the first time she has seen him afraid. "Small birds stay low," he says, more gently than ever. "Low is safe." "Low is small," says Pip, and is surprised at herself.
3. She goes to Elder Marra. Marra, large, grey-streaked, with a torn wing-tip and a voice like a cart on gravel, listens to Pip's request with her head cocked. She says the Trial isn't for chicks who weigh less than a walnut. "Storm-Sparrows are built, not wished." She isn't unkind, just certain. Pip's chest goes tight as a drum.
4. At the seed-patch, Cinder, glossy and chestnut and the best flier of his year, hears about it and laughs. "Pip? In the Trial? The wind would take you off like a dandelion seed. Tweet-truth, you should hold on to a twig and pray." He does an easy barrel roll to show what a real flier does. The others watch him, admiring. Pip watches too, because it is the best barrel roll she has ever seen, and she is memorizing it.
5. Nettle finds her that night, huddled in the dark of the hedge. "Everyone says you can't," Nettle whispers. "Do you think you can?" Pip looks at the stars for a long time. Hook: "I don't know," she says. "But I know I can't if I don't try, and that's the only thing that's true for sure."

## 3. The Bird in the Lightning Oak
Synopsis: Pip finds Old Tuck, a one-eyed retired Storm-Sparrow, who does not say she can't.
Header: *Sky Report: still, warm, a strange hush before something.*
1. At dawn Pip sneaks out to the edge of the hedge, where the great oak split by lightning stands alone and nobody goes. She has heard that a very old Storm-Sparrow lives there. She hops up the bark step by step, and calls into the hollow. Silence, then a dry cough, then a voice like paper being crumpled: "Go away. I'm not home."
2. Old Tuck emerges: a ragged sparrow with a patch over one eye, half his tail feathers gone, and a glint in the other eye that could cut wood. He looks at Pip a long time. "You're the runt." "I'm Pip." "Hmph. You're not the runt. I was the runt. You're a runt-in-training." He says he does not train fledglings and never has.
3. Pip explains that she wants to be a Storm-Sparrow, that she isn't allowed to enter, that everyone says she can't. Tuck says nothing for a very long time. Then he says: "Who said you can't?" "Everyone." "Have you asked the sky?" Pip blinks. "The sky doesn't answer." "Doesn't it?" He cocks his good eye. "You'd be surprised."
4. He makes her a deal: one lesson, at first light tomorrow, at the top of the oak. If she can stay in the air for one hundred wingbeats without landing, he'll consider a second. She'll be flying into the morning updraft, which throws big birds around and, he says with a grim gleam, "likes small ones." Pip says yes so fast her tail feather twitches.
5. Hook: on the way back, Pip finds her own reflection in a puddle: a tiny brown bird with a crooked tail. She looks at it a long time. It looks back. "Small birds stay low," she says to it. And then, quietly, to the sky: "Well. Let's find out."

## 4. One Hundred Wingbeats
Synopsis: Pip's first lesson with Tuck: she fails, laughs, and learns to ride the wind instead of fighting it.
Header: *Sky Report: sunrise updraft, gentle, growing.*
1. At dawn the top of the Lightning Oak is bathed in gold. Tuck perches beside her, spits out a beakful of bark, and points at the void. "One hundred wingbeats. Count them out loud. Go." Pip leaps, wings whirring, counting: one, two, three...
2. She fights the air with all her strength and by wingbeat twenty-two the updraft has whipped her into a tumble. She fetches up in a bramble with a squeak and a mouthful of leaves. Tuck, from the branch, says "Twenty-two. Again." She tries again: nineteen. Then sixteen. She is furious, breathless, scratched, and hilarious, and after the fifth crash Tuck, despite himself, chuckles.
3. Tuck flies down and lands next to her in the bramble. "You're fighting it. The wind's not your enemy, Pip. It's a river. You're a leaf, and leaves don't fight rivers. Leaves ride." He shows her: he spreads his ragged wings and lets the updraft carry him up, a slow spiral, no effort. Pip watches with her beak open. It looks as easy as sleeping.
4. She tries it. She stops flapping so hard. She lets the air lift her, tilts her crooked tail, and for the first time in her life feels the wind under her, not against her. She rises, gently, a little at a time, her tiny heart pounding, until she can see the whole of Thistlewick Hedge below like a rumpled green quilt. "Sixty-eight!" she shrieks. "Seventy!"
5. At ninety-nine, something makes her look down, and she sees Cinder on the fence post, watching. She loses the rhythm; the wind throws her sideways, but she recovers, flapping twice, and she is still up when the hundredth beat arrives. She drops to the branch, gasping. Tuck says, deadpan: "Hmph. Second lesson tomorrow." Hook: from the fence, Cinder's voice, low: "Who's your teacher, Pip?"

# PART TWO: RISING WIND

## 5. Learning the Sky
Synopsis: A week of lessons. Tuck teaches Pip to read wind; she gets better by tiny degrees. Nettle joins in secretly.
Header: *Sky Report: fair, steady wind from the northwest, good for learning.*
1. Each dawn Pip slips away to the oak. Tuck teaches her the language of wind: the smell of a thermal, the shiver of a gust front, the way a hill breathes air upward. He makes her fly blindfolded with a leaf over her eyes. She bumps into things. She learns.
2. Nettle follows her one morning, hiding in the ivy, and Pip finds her. Nettle confesses that she reads clouds better than any bird in the hedge (she can tell a wind shift an hour ahead) but is too shy to say so. Tuck tests her: "What's coming?" Nettle whispers, "Rain, from the sea, in two sunups." She is right. Tuck grunts with respect. Now there are three of them.
3. Tuck teaches Pip his motto: "Ride it, don't fight it." He also teaches her what small birds have that big ones don't: they are so light that a small gust lifts them where a big bird's wings must labor, so they can ride winds the heavy ones can't even feel. "It isn't that you can't fly like Cinder," he says. "It's that you can't fly like Cinder and shouldn't. You'll fly like Pip."
4. A week of small wins: Pip reaches two hundred wingbeats. Then three. She learns a tight spiral in a thermal, a slip through crosswinds, a dive-and-recover that makes Nettle shut her eyes. She keeps a private count in her head, "yesterday I did that, today I did a bit more." Her tail feather, crooked as ever, turns out to steer her like a rudder.
5. Hook: one evening, coming home, they pass the Gathering Rock, where the Elders are calling the names of the Trial's entrants. Marra reads the list in her booming voice. Pip's name isn't on it. Nettle's isn't either. But Cinder's is first, and he grins at the whole hedge.

## 6. Cinder's Loop
Synopsis: Cinder mocks Pip at the practice field; she flies badly in front of everyone, but Tuck's lesson lands.
Header: *Sky Report: bright, blustery, a good day to be looked at.*
1. At the Windmill Fields, where the Trial entrants practice, Pip and Nettle watch from a fence. Cinder leads a formation of the strongest fledglings in a spiral so smooth it looks like a ribbon being unrolled. The whole hedge cheers. Pip's wings itch.
2. Cinder spots them, swoops down, and asks loudly if Pip has come to be a windmill. The others laugh. He invites her to try. "One loop, small one. Let's see it." She knows it's a trap. She knows the wind is wrong. She does it anyway, because she will never learn what she can do if she only flies where nobody's watching.
3. The wind is high and tricky, and she botches the first loop: a lopsided tumble that drops her twenty feet. Everyone laughs. She lands hard in a thistle. Her face burns. Then she remembers Tuck's voice: don't fight it. She stops thrashing, spreads her wings, and lets the air lift her back up, in a long curving arc.
4. It isn't a loop. It's a spiral, a rising, unlikely curl she didn't plan, and the wind carries her higher and higher, above the mill, above the fence, above Cinder's head, and she hangs there, effortless and shining, as the fledglings stop laughing. It looks like the tiniest, strangest, loveliest kind of flying. Nettle whoops.
5. Cinder, squinting, says stiffly, "Lucky gust." Pip lands, shaking. "Maybe," she says. "But it was my gust." She turns and flies off with Nettle. Hook: from the shadows of a nearby hawthorn, a large chestnut shape watches her go. Cinder is not laughing any more. He is thinking.

## 7. Disqualified
Synopsis: Marra catches Pip and forbids her from entering the Trial. Uncle Dorn learns everything and is heartbroken.
Header: *Sky Report: overcast, a lid of grey, whispers on every branch.*
1. By nightfall the whole hedge knows that Pip flew at the Windmill Fields. Uncle Dorn is waiting on her branch with his feathers on end and a look Pip has never seen. "Where were you?" She tells him everything, about Tuck, the oak, Nettle, the lessons. He says nothing at all for a long time.
2. Dorn says, in a voice that cracks, that he lost her parents to a storm; that Storm-Sparrows fly into the edge of storms; that he cannot lose her, too. "I'm not saying you're not good, Pip. I'm saying you're mine." Pip presses against him. She loves him fiercely. She still can't stop. "If I stay low all my life, I'll be safe. And I won't be me."
3. Elder Marra summons her to the Rock at dawn. Grave, tired, she says she has heard the reports. She saw the spiral herself: remarkable, she admits, for a chick of this size. But the Trial is dangerous and the rules are old. "Storm-Sparrows are built, not wished. You are not built for it, Pip. You will not enter." Pip asks what she has to do to be built. Marra can't answer.
4. Pip walks away with her chin high and her eyes wet. At the oak she tells Tuck. He listens, whittling a twig with his beak. Then he says, "So? The rule says you can't enter. Did the rule say you can't fly the course?" Pip stares at him. He shrugs his ragged shoulders. "I've broken worse."
5. Hook: Nettle, who has been listening in the ivy, says in a very small voice, "The Trial is the day after tomorrow at sunrise. The course is the three winds: the Windmill Fields, the Salt Marsh, and the Cliffs of Calder. If we go before the entrants..." Pip looks at her, then at the sky. Dawn is coming, thin and pink. "We fly it first."

## 8. The Course
Synopsis: Pip, Nettle and Tuck plan the secret dawn flight of the Trial course. The first wind is the Windmill Wind.
Header: *Sky Report: cold, clear, a rising dawn wind, three friends and a plan.*
1. The night before the Trial, in the hollow of the Lightning Oak, Tuck draws the course in the dirt with a claw: the Windmill Wind, a gusty, spinning current over the fields; the Marsh Wind, a wet, heavy, sinking draft that pulls birds down into the reeds; and the Cliff Wind, a savage updraft that flings the weak into the rocks and lifts the wise.
2. Tuck describes each wind like an old enemy or friend. The Windmill Wind is a bully who backs off if you spin with him. The Marsh Wind is a sneak who grabs your tail when you're tired. The Cliff Wind is a great, generous, terrible thing that will hurl a heavy bird against the rocks but lift a light one clear of the world. He looks straight at Pip. "That last one's the only one you'll like."
3. Nettle wants to come along, to read the clouds. Tuck says she'll fly the marsh leg beside Pip as her eyes. They practice signals: a wing tilt for danger, a chirp for drift. Uncle Dorn, unable to sleep, walks the hedge below and sees three small shapes heading to the oak in the dark. He does not follow. He sits with his beak tucked, watching them go, and does not sleep.
4. Before dawn, they slip to the first marker at the Windmill Fields. The mill's great sails turn slowly in the grey. Pip's stomach is full of moths. Tuck says only, "Ride it, don't fight it. And Pip? Whatever anyone says, the sky isn't listening." She spreads her wings and leaps.
5. The first wind is a bully: it spins her, slaps her, pushes her toward the mill's whirling sails. Pip does what she practiced: she turns with it, letting it carry her round, and it flings her out the far side like a pebble from a sling. She whoops. "One wind!" Hook: ahead across the fields, the pink light shows the long silver line of the Salt Marsh, and something big and dark circling above it that shouldn't be there.

## 9. The Marsh Wind
Synopsis: The second wind and the first appearance of Skua Grey. Pip and Nettle slip past.
Header: *Sky Report: humid, sinking air, a cold shadow overhead.*
1. The Salt Marsh: silver reeds, shining pools, herons like question marks. The Marsh Wind lies low and wet, a heavy air that sucks at wings. Nettle flies beside Pip, tiny eyes narrowed, reading the sky. She sees the dark shape circling above: a huge sea hawk with steel-grey feathers. "Skua Grey," she whispers, and her voice shakes.
2. Skua Grey is legend: a cold, patient, enormous falcon that hunts the coast, never rushes, never misses a bird that flies the middle of the sky. The Storm-Sparrows keep low along the edges of marsh and cliff, out of her sight. Tuck's rule: fly the edge, not the middle. But the Marsh Wind is dragging Pip toward the open middle, and she feels her wings tire.
3. Nettle, terrified, rises above her fear and reads the signs: a small shift, a hedge of reeds that leans; there is a thermal coming off the sun-warmed mud. She calls it out in a clear voice. Pip catches it and rockets upward, out of the sinking draft, hugging the edge of the reeds where a shadow can't find her. The great hawk turns its head slowly, scanning. It does not see the two brown specks in the shadows.
4. They pass the marsh and land in the reeds at the far side, shaking and laughing, hearts pounding. "Two winds," says Nettle, "and you didn't fall out of the sky once." "You saved me," says Pip. "You saw the thermal." Nettle blinks, astonished. "I did." She has never said something and been listened to, and it goes to her head a little, in the good way.
5. Hook: they look ahead. Beyond the marsh the land ends in a great white wall of rock: the Cliffs of Calder, with the ocean roaring at their feet and a fierce, warm, upward wind screaming along them like a giant breathing out. The last wind, the Cliff Wind, the one that throws the weak into the rocks and lifts the wise. Pip stares. It is the most beautiful and frightening thing she has ever seen.

# PART THREE: STORM WARNING

## 10. The Cliff Wind
Synopsis: The third wind. Pip's tiny weight lets her ride the Cliff Wind higher than any bird has ever gone. She is seen.
Header: *Sky Report: strong onshore wind, a rising updraft, salt on the tongue.*
1. The Cliffs of Calder: white as bone, a hundred wingbeats tall, with gannets wheeling and the sea roaring below. The Cliff Wind screams up the face like a river turned upside down. Tuck's voice is in her head: "Heavy birds fight it and get hurled at the rocks. A small bird can go with it." She takes a breath and leaps into it.
2. It hits her like a giant hand and flings her upward. She isn't fighting; she's floating. She tilts her crooked tail. She spirals. The white cliffs blur past. The wind carries her up, up, up, higher than the gulls, higher than the clouds' underbellies, above the top of the world. For a moment she is nothing but a tiny brown feather riding a giant breath, and she is so happy she cannot breathe.
3. At the top, she circles once and sees the sea, endless, grey-green, and beyond it a smudge of light: the Warm Reaches, where the flock will fly. It is the first time she has seen where they are going. She shouts to the wind, "I can see it!" And she thinks she hears something answer, a low, kind rumble in the sky.
4. She lands on the cliff-top grass, feathers wild, and finds herself surrounded. Elder Marra, all the Elders, half the flock, Uncle Dorn among them, have come for the sunrise start of the Trial and have seen her ride the Cliff Wind higher than any bird in living memory. No one speaks. Cinder's beak is open.
5. Marra clears her throat. It takes her three tries. "Pip," she says. "That was... against the rules." A long pause. "It was also the finest flying I have seen in forty seasons." Hook: Marra looks at the sky, then at the small brown bird, and the Elder's stern face does something complicated. Then the sky darkens. To the west, a long thin cloud like a bruise is rolling in, hours before it should.

## 11. The Trial of Three Winds
Synopsis: The official Trial goes ahead, but the storm-front approaches. Pip is allowed to fly last, and does.
Header: *Sky Report: falling glass, swirling gusts, sky the color of an old bruise.*
1. Marra, after conferring with the Elders, announces that the Trial will go on, since the season is short, but that Pip may enter, "on the strength of what we've just seen." A cheer goes up from a few fledglings, a grumble from others. Uncle Dorn says nothing. He just moves closer.
2. The official Trial begins. Cinder flies first, and he is magnificent: the Windmill spiral like silk, the Marsh Wind sliced clean, the Cliff Wind fought with muscle and roar, landing exactly on the marker. It is the best flight of any fledgling in a decade. The hedge roars for him. He glances at Pip with an expression she can't read.
3. Pip flies last. The wind has changed: the storm's outer breath is stirring everything, and the winds are angrier, twisting into each other. She flies the Windmill Wind, this time spinning with it; the Marsh Wind, using Nettle's signals; and finally the Cliff Wind, soaring higher than Cinder, but a gust rips her sideways and she has to land far off the marker. It's not a clean flight. She finishes last in the marks.
4. Marra confers with the Elders in a low knot. Everyone waits. Pip, exhausted, doesn't care about the scores. She's done it: she flew the course, in front of everyone, on the day. Uncle Dorn lands beside her without a word and presses his feathers against hers. It is the first time he has ever touched her without warning her of something.
5. Marra announces that the new Storm-Sparrows are Cinder and two others. Pip is not chosen. The words hit her like a stone. Marra adds gruffly that Pip has "shown remarkable skill," and that the Elders will consider her again "in a season or two." Hook: Tuck, from a rooftop far above, murmurs, "Ah, well. They'll be needing you sooner than they think." Behind him the western sky turns the color of iron.

## 12. The Storm Comes Early
Synopsis: The great storm arrives early and the flock must flee across the sea, and gets scattered.
Header: *Sky Report: violent gale from the southwest, torrential rain, all birds to shelter.*
1. The storm doesn't wait for the migration. At noon the flock is called to a hurried Gathering in a wind that flattens the hedge. Marra shouts that they must leave now, before the storm is at full strength; the other option is to be blown apart in the hedge. The Storm-Sparrows are to lead. Cinder puffs himself up.
2. The flock rises as one enormous, ragged cloud of wings, a thousand sparrows into the tearing sky. Pip flies near Uncle Dorn and Nettle. Tuck, too old to make the crossing, stays behind on the oak. He tells Pip, "Ride it, don't fight it," and nudges her into the air. His single eye shines. "Small birds go where they like." She wants to say goodbye. There is no time.
3. Over the Grey Sea the storm strikes fully. Lightning cracks. The wind becomes a wall. The formation breaks; the Storm-Sparrows shout directions that are torn away. Cinder, in front, tries to fly through the worst of it in a straight line and is hurled back. Birds vanish into spray and cloud. Pip loses Nettle. She loses Uncle Dorn. She is alone in the roaring dark.
4. She spins, buffeted, blinded. The old fear closes over her: too small, too light, blown away like a seed. Then she notices something odd. In the middle of the chaos there are lanes: ribbons of air that flow more smoothly than the rest, threading between the storm's biggest gusts. Big birds can't feel them. She can. She lets go of the fight and slides into one.
5. Hook: she rides the lane, swept along, terrified and tender, tiny voices calling from far away in the murk. Someone is crying, somewhere below. Pip tilts her crooked tail, and for the first time in her life flies toward a storm instead of away from it.

## 13. Alone Over the Water
Synopsis: Pip finds and rescues the first scattered birds using the wind-lanes.
Header: *Sky Report: gale, thunder, rain horizontal, visibility nil.*
1. Pip follows the crying and finds it: three fledglings from the hedge, clinging to a bit of floating driftwood on the roaring sea, too exhausted to fly. She flies low over the water. The spray stings. She calls, "Up! Up! Follow me!" They can't. Their wings are soaked and useless.
2. She thinks fast. She spots the lane of smoother air she's been riding, curving over the waves like a road. If she can carry them one at a time on the updraft... she can't carry them, she's a walnut. Then she realizes she doesn't have to. She only needs to bring them to the lane so the wind can lift them. She dives, nudges one fledgling off the wood with her beak, flaps beside him and shouts, "Ride it, don't fight it!" The wind takes him.
3. One by one, she brings them up. It takes all her strength and there's terror in every wingbeat. The third, a tiny yellow-billed bird named Fern, is frozen. Pip talks to her the whole time, telling her nonsense, counting wingbeats. "One hundred, Fern. Just one hundred." Fern makes it. The three are riding the lane in a shivering ribbon, trailing her.
4. They come across two more, an old sparrow with a broken wing feather and a young one gone silent in fear. Pip's voice is hoarse, and she has stopped noticing whether she is small or large. She is simply the bird who knows the lane. Soon a small ragged line trails behind her: seven, eight, nine birds, holding to her shadow as if she were a lantern.
5. Hook: a shape in the sky ahead. Huge, grey, hanging in the storm like a hook. The steel-grey wings do not fight the wind; they lean on it. Skua Grey turns her head, and her yellow eye finds the line of small birds. She is hunting in the storm, where every bird is weak, and the middle of the sky is where a hawk waits. Pip's heart stops, and then starts again, twice as fast.

## 14. Skua Grey
Synopsis: The hawk stalks the line of birds. Pip must think of something.
Header: *Sky Report: gale easing to squalls, rain in sheets, a cold predator overhead.*
1. Skua Grey drifts above the line of exhausted sparrows, patient as time. She does not dive yet. She waits until one bird lags. Pip counts nine behind her and knows the hawk can take any one of them in a heartbeat. She can't fight; she can't outfly. What's left?
2. She thinks of Tuck: what do small birds have that big birds don't? She remembers the Marsh Wind, the reeds, the way the hawk's eye did not find her in the shadows. Skua Grey is huge; huge things need clear air to dive. Where the storm's lanes are narrow and the wind is chopped, a hawk can't fold her wings without being flung. Pip tilts her crooked tail and leads them down into the roughest, narrowest, ugliest air she can find.
3. It is terrifying: the gusts hurl them together and apart, spray blinds them. But in the choppiest air the great hawk falters. She dives once, and the wind slaps her aside like a hand, and she skids away with a screech, snapping at spray. She comes again. Pip, ahead, calls each shift like a drum: "Left! Down! Now, up!" The line follows her like a flock of leaves.
4. Cinder appears from a cloud, wings bloody-edged, lost, his beautiful chestnut feathers plastered. He has been flying straight into the storm's face, as a Storm-Sparrow should, and he is finished, sinking. Skua Grey sees him. She folds her wings. Pip, without a thought, turns and dives after her, shouting the thing Tuck taught her, at the top of her voice: "Cinder! Stop fighting! Ride it!"
5. Cinder hears. For a heartbeat, the proudest bird in the hedge lets go. He goes limp in the gust, and the wind carries him sideways, out from under the hawk's stoop, and drops him beside Pip. Skua Grey's talons close on nothing. She swerves away with a long cold cry. Cinder, gasping, stares at Pip. "Tweet-truth?" he says, in a whisper. It is the first time it isn't a jeer.

# PART FOUR: CLEARING SKIES

## 15. The Lantern Isles Wind
Synopsis: The sparrows are exhausted. Nettle appears, reading the clouds, pointing the way to the Lantern Isles.
Header: *Sky Report: storm easing, cloud breaking in the east, a very tired bird.*
1. Pip leads a line of a dozen sparrows and one shaken Cinder through the last of the squalls. Every wing is failing. The sea below is huge and grey. There's no shore, no branch, no island. Pip's own wings feel like wet paper. "Nettle," she whispers into the wind, "where are you?"
2. And out of the clouds, above, a small voice: "Pip!" Nettle is there, clinging to a tiny lane of air, with Uncle Dorn beside her, ragged, both alive. They fly together. Dorn's eyes are wild; he presses his beak against Pip's head for one long moment and says nothing at all. Pip's heart is so full it hurts.
3. Nettle points: she has been reading the clouds for hours. There is a rift in the cloud bank, a golden line to the east where the storm is thinning. A warm wind rises from an island out of sight: the Lantern Isles, the flock's stopover, twelve hundred wingbeats away. "If we ride the rift, we'll reach it," she says. She has never spoken so clearly in her life. Everyone listens.
4. A council in mid-air. Cinder, humbled, offers to fly point. "You read the wind," he says to Pip, stiffly. "I'll do the shouting." He does: he calls out Nettle's cloud signals, and Pip flies the lanes, and Uncle Dorn brings up the rear, gathering stragglers with his gruff voice. For the first time all five, all twelve, all thirty of them fly as one flock.
5. Hook: across the breaking storm the flock begins to spot other wings, small dark specks on the wind, all lost, all following the sound of their voices. One by one, the scattered flock finds itself. Somewhere behind them, the storm gives a last rumble like a great beast rolling over in its sleep. And ahead, glimmering like a scattering of lanterns on the sea, are the Lantern Isles.

## 16. Landfall
Synopsis: The flock reaches the Lantern Isles. Gullet the gull helps. The count of who's missing brings dread.
Header: *Sky Report: light rain clearing, first sun on wet rock, a lot of exhausted birds.*
1. The Lantern Isles: a scatter of low green islands with little lighthouses that glow all night. The flock crashes onto the rocks in heaps. Birds sleep where they fall. Gullet, a giant, friendly, dim seagull, waddles among them, offering fish-heads. Nobody is hungry enough to refuse.
2. Marra arrives at last, her torn wing-tip bleeding, leading a straggling third of the flock. She sees Pip, the small brown bird in the middle of a great heap of sparrows, and her mouth opens and closes. She does not say a word. She sinks onto a rock. Someone tells her what happened. She listens with her eyes closed.
3. The count begins. Sparrow by sparrow, the Elders count the flock at dawn, sweep after sweep, faces grave. Some are missing. Some are found. Fern is counted. The old sparrow with the broken feather. The nine. Cinder, who won't stop apologizing to anyone. Numbers murmur like prayer. Pip stands with Nettle, who is sleeping on her feet.
4. Uncle Dorn finds her. He is standing very stiff. He says he has known she could fly, he has always known; he was afraid, and it wasn't her fault. "I'm not sorry I said it. I'm sorry I said it so many times." He turns his face away, which is what old sparrows do when they cry. "Small birds go where they like," he says, and Pip presses against him.
5. Hook: the last count comes in. All but three are here, and those three are known to be on the far shore, safe. Everyone is alive. A long sigh goes through the flock like wind through leaves. Then Marra stands, on a rock, in the low golden light, and says, in her gravelly voice, "I have something to say. It will not be easy for me. Sit."

## 17. What Marra Said
Synopsis: Marra publicly reverses her rule. Pip is named a Storm-Sparrow, the first of the small wind.
Header: *Sky Report: clear, a gentle breeze from the south, all the time in the world.*
1. Marra clears her throat, and clears it again. "For forty seasons," she says, "I have said that Storm-Sparrows are built, not wished. I said it to children. I said it to Pip. I believed it. I was wrong."
2. She tells the flock what she saw: a sparrow who weighs less than a walnut leading thirty birds through the worst storm of the century, reading the wind like a book none of them had learned to read. "It is not that the rule was cruel," she says, "It's that the rule was small. We measured wings. We should have measured the sky."
3. The new Storm-Sparrows are called: Cinder and the two chosen at the Trial step forward. And then Marra says, "Also, Nettle." Nettle's mouth falls open. "For reading the clouds when no one had ever asked her to. And..." Marra's voice does something odd. "Pip." A silence like a held breath, then a sound like the whole flock lifting off.
4. Cinder is the first to speak. He hops forward, feathers awkward, and says to the whole flock, "I told Pip she couldn't. I was wrong, and I'm... it was a bad thing to say." He looks at Pip. "I'd like to learn the lanes. If you'd teach me." Pip, astonished, says only, "You have to stop fighting the wind." He says, "I'm working on it."
5. Hook: Pip, who has waited her whole life to hear the words, finds that what she feels is not triumph but something quieter, a small warm lamp lit somewhere inside her. She glances at the sea to the west, where the storm is a smudge of gray, and past it, faintly, the edge of the Lightning Oak's hedge. "Tuck," she whispers. "We did it."

## 18. A Letter in the Wind
Synopsis: Pip thinks of Tuck. A bird arrives with news and Tuck's gift.
Header: *Sky Report: fair, warm, a whiff of far-off smoke and old oak.*
1. Days on the Lantern Isles. The flock rests and heals. Pip flies every dawn, learning the island winds. Cinder trails her, awkward and grateful. Nettle, cloud-reading, has become a legend. But Pip keeps looking west, across the sea, worrying about the ragged one-eyed bird who stayed behind.
2. On the sixth morning a carrier gull arrives from the mainland, huge-winged and windblown, a message tied to his leg: a scrap of oak bark scratched in a familiar claw. "Still here. Storm knocked a limb off the oak. Fine. Chest a little sore. Hope you didn't fight it." Pip laughs and cries at the same time. Underneath, Tuck has added a line: "Told you the sky would answer."
3. Marra, reading it over her shoulder, says quietly, "That's Tuck. I exiled him myself for breaking a rule I no longer remember." Pip stares at her. "He flew a course nobody else would, and wouldn't say sorry." Marra looks at the smudge on the horizon. "I think it's time someone sent him an apology. In the biggest wind we can find."
4. The whole flock gathers to send Tuck an answer. They can't fly back; the season is short. But Nettle reads the sky and finds a great homeward wind, and the flock releases a hundred feathers into it, tail-feathers, down-feathers, small brown ones and glossy chestnut ones, and the wind carries them across the water toward the far-off oak. Pip's crooked feather goes last.
5. Hook: that night, on the highest rock of the Lantern Isles, Pip and Uncle Dorn sit side by side. "Where do you want to go next?" says Dorn. It is the question she's waited to be asked. "Everywhere," she says, and her uncle, who has grumbled all his life, laughs out loud. They look at the stars and the road of wind between them and the Warm Reaches.

## 19. The Long Way South
Synopsis: The flock crosses the last stretch of sea. Pip leads the way, and the old habits are dropped.
Header: *Sky Report: bright, a firm following wind, all the good weather you could ask for.*
1. The flock sets out for the last stretch to the Warm Reaches. Marra names the leaders: Pip and Nettle, with Cinder and the others, riding just behind. Uncle Dorn brings up the rear, grumbling about the good old days, and telling everyone, at length, how brave his niece is.
2. The crossing takes two days. They ride the lanes, singing the old wind-songs. Pip leads with her crooked tail, sliding through pockets of air no one else can feel, and the whole flock follows in a long silver ribbon. Cinder, still learning, drifts, corrects, laughs at himself. It is the first time he has laughed at himself in his life.
3. On the second dusk, a familiar steel-grey shape appears at the edge of the sky. Skua Grey has followed them. This time Pip doesn't run. She flies out to meet her, alone, a tiny brown speck against the huge hawk's eye. She says, in a voice that doesn't shake, that they are not prey; that the flock is under her wing. The hawk regards her for a long time, then wheels and glides away over the water. Perhaps it is respect. Perhaps it is only that the small bird is unafraid.
4. A warm smell comes across the sea: wet earth, green things, fruit, flowers. The Warm Reaches: a long green shore glowing gold in the low light, with trees heavy with fruit and the sound of a thousand unknown birds. The flock cheers and dives.
5. Hook: Pip circles once, above the shore, above the flock, above the trees, and feels the whole sky open under her like a hand. She thinks: I'll have that one. And then, for the first time, she thinks, and the next, and the next.

## 20. Small Birds Go Where They Like
Synopsis: Landfall in the Warm Reaches, and a last look at how far Pip has come.
Header: *Sky Report: warm, gentle winds from the south, clear all the way up.*
1. The flock settles into the Warm Reaches: the fruit trees, the warm rain, the first night without fear. Pip sleeps in a mango tree beside Nettle and Uncle Dorn and, unexpectedly, Cinder, who has decided he is Pip's shadow.
2. In the morning the Elders hold the naming ceremony at last, under a giant banyan. Marra gives Pip and Nettle their Storm-Sparrow feathers: a single silver-blue feather each, to be worn tucked in the wing. Pip looks at hers. It is bigger than she is. Everyone laughs. She tucks it under her crooked tail feather and it sits there like a crown.
3. Marra asks if there is anything Pip wants. She thinks. She says she'd like the Trial changed, so that every sparrow, whatever its size, is allowed to try. Marra, gruff, says she'll consider it. Then, drily, "Consider it done." Pip flies a small joyful loop, and this time it comes out as a perfect one, and nobody laughs, everybody cheers.
4. Weeks later a small brown bird arrives from the north on the last of the autumn winds: Old Tuck, weathered, one-eyed, half his feathers gone, having flown the sea by himself at seventy seasons. He lands on Pip's branch and says, "Hmph. Took you long enough." Pip crashes into him in a hug that knocks them both off the branch. Uncle Dorn, watching, is heard to say, "Well, I never."
5. Last image: a new season. A tiny bird tilts her head on the highest branch of the tallest tree, wind ruffling her crooked tail, a silver feather in her wing, looking at the biggest sky in the world. Next to her sits a very small, very new sparrow, a fledgling of the Warm Reaches, no bigger than a walnut. "Do you think I could?" the little one whispers. Pip smiles. "Let's go find out," she says, and they leap.
