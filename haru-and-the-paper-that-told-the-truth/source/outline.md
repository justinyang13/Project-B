# PART ONE: INK

## 1. The Ink Froze in the Pot
Synopsis: On the coldest morning of the year the ink freezes in the Owl Alley print shop, and Haru introduces her family, the shop and her job as its ears.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 1. Today's news: the ink froze in the pot.*
1. The coldest morning: the ink in the pot freezes solid and Kichi's brush stands up in it like a flagpole. Haru comes down the ladder from the loft with smoking breath and says it is the truest thing she has ever seen him say. Everyone laughs but Kichi, who has not eaten.
2. The shop described through Haru's eyes: one long room, a low carving table, blocks of cherry wood on shelves, sheets of damp paper hanging on lines like laundry, the smell of ink and wood shavings. Mother warms the ink pot over a small charcoal brazier and laughs with her hand over her mouth; Father Sota carves without looking up.
3. Grandfather Tobei by the brazier, rubbing his bad shoulder, a folded towel around his neck. He was the best printer on Owl Alley; now he teaches Haru with the baren, a disc of coiled cord in a bamboo sheath. He explains in his dry way what the shop makes: calendars, greeting sheets, notices, picture sheets for children and, when something truly happens, a news sheet.
4. Haru's job: she is the shop's ears. Each morning she takes her paper booklet on its string to the well, the bathhouse steps and Okiku's noodle stall, and writes down what she hears. Father's rule when she comes home: "Print what happened, not what people say happened." "Did you see it with your own eyes?"
5. Haru suggests today's headline: the ink froze in the pot. Father Sota almost smiles and starts to carve it. Outside, across the alley, the door of the Turtle House slides open and someone begins to shout something about foxes.

## 2. The Fox Wedding on the Bridge
Synopsis: The sellers Bunta and Gohei cry a thrilling sheet from the rival shop, the whole alley buys it, and Haru knows it is not true.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 2. Today's news: foxes were not seen at any wedding.*
1. Bunta, round and big-voiced in a deep straw hat that hides his face, reads the Turtle House sheet aloud to a growing crowd: a wedding of foxes on Willow Bridge, lanterns and all. Thin Gohei watches the street corners for the magistrate's men. Haru explains why sellers wear the hats and go in pairs.
2. Haru buys a copy with a coin she has been saving. The picture is beautiful: the carving is fine, the foxes wear tiny robes, the lanterns glow. She is angry that it is so well made.
3. Haru crossed Willow Bridge herself last night, on the way home from the bathhouse. She saw one cat, two lanterns and a great deal of fog. She tells Father Sota, who says quietly that most people know a story when they see one. "Not everyone," says Haru.
4. Jiro stands on the Turtle House step in his yellow silk kimono and tall geta, fanning himself in the cold, and tells Haru that the Turtle House sold out by noon and the Owl Alley Sheet sold eleven. Kichi does the counting and adds a pun. Haru does not laugh.
5. Grandfather Tobei tells Haru that a fast paper and a true paper are different animals: one runs, one lasts. Haru lies in the loft that night with her booklet and the fox picture, thinking about what a true paper would need to beat a fast one.

## 3. Soot-Sweeping Day
Synopsis: The whole alley scrubs away the year's soot, Haru hears a rumor at the bathhouse steps about Okiku's noodles, and she decides to find out for herself.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 3. Today's news: one hundred years of soot came down.*
1. Soot-sweeping day: bamboo brooms, damp cloths, mats carried out, ceilings scraped; Haru's job is to clean the block shelves without touching a single block. Neighbors cheer and fling one big helper into the air in the old way. Rice cakes for everyone.
2. Neighbors introduced at the alley well: Okiku with her grey towel around her hair, big cheerful Umekichi the tofu maker with his wife Osen, gruff Hachi from the bathhouse with a towel over his shoulder, and small sooty Shin, who yawns through the whole day.
3. At the bathhouse steps Haru overhears: Okiku's soba has gone grey this week, and somebody says she must be cutting it with something worse than flour. Two neighbors nod. A third says his cousin heard it too. Haru writes it in her booklet, then crosses it out: nobody there saw anything.
4. Haru tells Father Sota what she heard. He asks three things: did she see it, who said it first, and what does the person gain by saying it. She has no answers. He tells her that if she wants to write about noodles she should go and watch noodles.
5. At dusk Haru stands at Okiku's stall with her booklet closed. Okiku, who has heard the whispers, does not look at her. The broth steams. Haru takes a long, slow breath and asks, "May I watch you make them?"

## 4. Buckwheat and Water
Synopsis: Haru watches Okiku make soba, proves the rumor false with her own eyes, and begs to print it; Father shows her how a block is carved and printed.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 4. Today's news: noodles were found to be noodles.*
1. Okiku hands Haru a stool and lets her watch: buckwheat flour, a little wheat flour, water, kneading, rolling, folding, cutting, all in a rhythm that makes Haru's neck prickle. Okiku shows her the new sack of flour: this season's, stone-ground, darker and greyer. That is all. Haru weighs, sniffs, tastes.
2. Haru asks Okiku who first said the noodles were bad. Okiku sighs and says it does not matter, and that a whisper is a hard thing to sweep away. Haru notices Okiku's hands are shaking as she pours the broth.
3. Haru runs home. She wants to print it tonight. Father Sota says a sheet is only three things: a picture, a headline and the truth, in that order of difficulty. Haru draws Okiku with a bowl of noodles and steam in a swirl. She writes: "Okiku's noodles are grey because the flour is new. They are buckwheat, wheat and water. Nothing else."
4. Father carves the block as Haru watches: he cuts away everything around the lines so the picture stands up, and the wood curls away in thin pale ribbons that smell of cherry. He carves two small notches at the corner, kento marks, so every sheet will sit in exactly the same place.
5. Kichi wets the paper, lays it on the inked block, and Grandfather Tobei guides Haru's hand on the baren: even pressure, no hurry. She peels back the paper: the first sheet, damp and a little crooked, with Okiku's face in it. The picture is clear enough to cry over, and Haru nearly does.

## 5. A Small Coin for the Truth
Synopsis: Haru and Kichi sell the noodle sheet themselves, Bunta reads it aloud for free, the alley returns to Okiku's stall, and Jiro says nobody reads true things.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 5. Today's news: the alley went back for noodles.*
1. Sheets hang on the drying lines all night. At dawn Haru and Kichi carry them in a basket to the canal end, where Haru finds she cannot shout the news. Her voice comes out small. Kichi shouts it for her, and then adds a pun about noodles and news that makes a woman laugh and buy two.
2. Bunta, hearing it, stops and reads the whole sheet aloud in his big voice, all of it, without being paid, because he likes what it says. The crowd hushes for a moment. Gohei keeps watch for officials.
3. Okiku reads the sheet and stands still for a long time. Then she cuts a slab of noodles into a bowl and will not let anyone pay. The neighbors line up. Haru is not sure she has ever tasted anything so good.
4. The whisper dies. Even the neighbor who said his cousin heard it comes by and buys a bowl and does not meet anyone's eyes. Grandfather Tobei says a rumor takes a day to travel a street and a year to leave it, and that today is the first day of leaving.
5. Jiro is watching from the Turtle House step. He says nobody reads true things, they read exciting things, and that in a month she will see. Haru thinks he might be right. She holds her booklet against her chest. She does not believe it.

# PART TWO: FAST PAPER

## 6. The Year-End Rush
Synopsis: Calendars, greeting sheets and rice cakes fill the shop; the Owl Alley Sheet becomes known, and Haru gets proud and quick.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 6. Today's news: the shop has not sat down since dawn.*
1. Year-end: everyone wants calendars and New Year greeting sheets at once. The shop hums: Father carving, Kichi printing, Mother mixing color, Haru hanging paper as fast as she can. Kichi eats standing up.
2. Mother Okiyo teaches Haru to mix a color: vermilion from a small heap of orange powder in a shell, a drop of water, a drop of paste, and the way it changes. She says colors, like people, need patience. Haru is too busy to hear her properly.
3. People start to come to Haru with news: Where does the man with the cart stop? Who took the lantern? A woman at the well says Haru's sheet made her feel that her own street was worth writing about. Haru glows. She starts to write in her booklet faster and faster.
4. The Turtle House hangs a huge new sheet from its eaves: a ghost in the canal at midnight. Sellers cry it. It is completely false. Haru knows a boy who was fishing there all night: there was no ghost, only a broken barrel.
5. Haru decides that her next sheet will say so, loud and clear. Grandfather Tobei is dozing by the brazier. Father Sota looks at her over the block he is carving and says nothing at all.

## 7. The Girl Who Was Right
Synopsis: At the temple school Jiro claims the fox wedding was real, and Haru corrects him in front of everyone.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 7. Today's news: a liar was called a liar in front of the class.*
1. The terakoya in a cold temple hall: children in rows on straw mats, brushes, inkstones and sheets of practice paper; Teacher Ishida, thin and grey, with round glasses and an abacus at his elbow, calls out names. Haru is second in her row, Jiro in the front.
2. The class copies out a lesson in the old careful strokes. Haru's brush is quick and her characters slant; Jiro's are perfect. Teacher Ishida praises Jiro. Haru grips her brush until her knuckles go pale.
3. In the break Jiro tells a knot of children that the fox wedding was real, that his own father's carver saw it. Haru stands up. She says loudly that she crossed Willow Bridge herself and saw a cat and fog, and that the Turtle House sells lies and Jiro is a liar. The whole room hears.
4. Silence. Jiro goes red to his ears and then white. A small girl giggles nervously; then everyone does. Jiro walks out of the hall and does not look back, with his tall geta clacking on the boards.
5. Teacher Ishida sets down his abacus. He tells Haru she is right about the bridge. Then he asks her, quietly, whether the class learned anything about truth or only about Jiro. Haru has no answer. She is right, and the whole room feels wrong.

## 8. The Sharp Sheet
Synopsis: Haru prints a sheet that mocks the Turtle House, it sells brilliantly, and Master Kuroda storms into Owl Alley.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 8. Today's news: the Turtle House was called a fox.*
1. That night, still stung, Haru writes a new sheet. She keeps her facts straight: no foxes, no ghost, one broken barrel. But she adds a line about a certain yellow-sleeved boy whose stories were as thin as his patience. Father Sota is at the bathhouse; Kichi and Grandfather Tobei do not read it properly.
2. Kichi carves the extra line and prints two hundred sheets at dawn. Bunta reads it out, grinning, in his biggest voice. People laugh. It sells faster than anything the shop has ever printed.
3. Haru has never been so popular. A stranger pats her head. Kichi is tearful with pride. Mother Okiyo reads the sheet, goes quiet, and puts it down.
4. Master Kuroda of the Turtle House arrives in a gold-brown kimono, so angry that he forgets to take off his geta. He says the alley will regret this. Then he looks at his own son, standing behind him with his eyes down and his ears red, and says something in a low voice that Haru does not hear and that Jiro does.
5. Father Sota puts down his knife. He reads the sheet from top to bottom. "Every line of this is true," he says. "Except the one that is not about the truth." He does not shout. Haru finds it much worse.

## 9. The Edge and the Hand
Synopsis: Grandfather Tobei teaches Haru with a carving knife that truth is an edge and kindness is the hand, and Haru tries to say sorry and cannot.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 9. Today's news: a knife was put in a child's hand.*
1. Grandfather Tobei takes Haru to the low carving table late that night. He gives her a small block of cherry wood and a knife wrapped in cloth, and asks her to make one clean line. She makes it and it wanders. He says: the knife does not care what it cuts. The hand must.
2. He tells her about a sheet he once printed when he was young, a true one, about a merchant who had cheated; it was right, and the merchant's wife wept in the street. He does not say what he felt. He says, "The edge is truth. The hand is kindness. An edge with no hand cuts the wrong thing."
3. Haru asks if it is wrong, then, to tell the truth. Grandfather Tobei laughs, a short dry laugh, and says it would be far worse to stay silent. The point is to have both edge and hand, and it takes a lifetime to learn how.
4. The next morning Haru walks to the Turtle House with her hands cold and her apology written on a slip of paper. She stands on the step. She cannot go in. The door curtain hangs, glossy and closed. She folds the slip small, small, and puts it in her sleeve.
5. Jiro comes out and sees her. Neither speaks. He looks at her for a long moment, nods once, very slightly, and goes back in. Haru is not sure what the nod meant, and it bothers her for the rest of the day.

## 10. Soba at the End of the Year
Synopsis: On the last night of the year the whole alley eats soba together, and a warning arrives that unlicensed sheets are frowned on.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 10. Today's news: the old year ended with noodles.*
1. New Year's Eve: the alley gathers at Okiku's stall for the long noodles that carry the old year out. Bowls are passed hand to hand; Umekichi and Osen bring warm tofu; Hachi brings a huge kettle of hot water from the bathhouse; Shin falls asleep with his bowl in his lap.
2. Neighbors tell each other the best and worst of the year. Okiku holds up her bowl to Haru and says, "This year's best thing." Haru feels her face go hot, and then hears her own name in the same breath as Jiro's and feels it cool.
3. Ward headman Master Ito arrives, neat and stern in a dark formal coat, and speaks to Father Sota quietly, apart from the crowd. The magistrate's men have noticed unlicensed sheets on Owl Alley, and both shops. He is not angry. He is worried. Nobody may print the news without permission, and it will not be overlooked for ever.
4. Father Sota comes back to the stall and says nothing, and puts an arm around Haru's shoulder for a moment. Kichi, who overheard, says nothing either, and it is the longest silence he has ever kept. Grandfather Tobei says only, "So. The sheet has teeth."
5. The temple bell begins to toll the old year out, slow and deep, over the roofs. Haru counts the strokes as they sound, and thinks about the knife, and about her sleeve with the folded apology in it.

# PART THREE: THE SHARP KNIFE

## 11. A Thumb of Cherry Wood
Synopsis: New Year's Day arrives and Father teaches Haru to carve properly; she hurries, cuts her thumb, and understands the knife.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 11. Today's news: a thumb was bandaged.*
1. New Year's Day: pine-and-bamboo gate decorations, a stacked rice cake in the doorway, the smell of clear soup with rice cake, small gifts of money in paper folds for the children. The shop is closed for three days and Haru's fingers itch.
2. Father Sota, with the shop quiet, sits Haru at the low table with a small block and a small knife. "Draw a line. Cut beside it, never on it. Let the wood tell you which way it goes." He shows her the grain.
3. Haru cuts and cuts, faster and faster, wanting to be done. On the twelfth stroke the knife jumps and slides into the base of her thumb. It bleeds startlingly. Mother Okiyo wraps it in a clean cloth and does not scold.
4. Grandfather Tobei looks at the bandage and says nothing about the knife. He says, "Now you know what the edge is." Haru holds her hand and understands, all at once, why he chose a knife to teach her.
5. Later, with her thumb throbbing, she cuts one more line, slowly. It goes straight. She does not say anything. Father Sota looks at it for a long time, and then at her, and says, "Yes."

## 12. Yaki-imo for a Rival
Synopsis: Haru finally crosses to the Turtle House with a roasted sweet potato, and Jiro tells her why he tells stories.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 12. Today's news: a sweet potato was carried across the alley.*
1. Haru buys two hot roasted sweet potatoes from the yaki-imo seller with the last of her New Year coins. They are so hot she has to toss them from hand to hand. She takes a breath and walks across the alley.
2. Jiro is alone on the step in his yellow silk kimono, blowing on his fingers. She holds out a potato. He looks at it as if it were a live crab. "Is it poisoned?" "It is a sweet potato," says Haru, which is true, and he laughs in spite of himself.
3. They sit on the step. She says sorry, badly, in a rush; he says his father said he was a "soft-hearted fool" for letting a girl make the Turtle House a joke. Haru says the fox wedding was still not true. Jiro says he knows. He says he wanted it to be.
4. Jiro tells her that his father only smiles at sheets that sell. He tells her he has never carved a line his father praised, so he writes the wonders instead: foxes, ghosts, two-headed carp. "Nobody can say I got it wrong," he says. "It never happened." Haru says nothing. It is the first time she has heard a liar explain himself, and it makes him seem lonelier than a thief.
5. Jiro gives her a small copper tag from his sleeve: the Turtle House mark. "In case you need to get in fast," he says, and looks away. Haru holds it in her palm all the way home.

## 13. The Bathhouse Steps
Synopsis: Haru listens at the bathhouse, notices a cracked chimney cap and sleepy Shin tending the furnace, and writes it in her booklet.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 13. Today's news: a boy fell asleep on a wood pile.*
1. The bathhouse in the cold afternoon: steam, chatter, the smell of wet cedar and hot steam. Haru sits on the steps with her booklet and a warm cup of amazake bought from a passing seller, and listens the way Grandfather taught her, to what people say and what they leave out.
2. She hears that Hachi's wife has been ill for a week, that the owner has been running the counter alone, and that Shin has been doing two boys' jobs, feeding the furnace from before dawn until the last bather. Shin's eyes have big dark rings under them.
3. Haru walks around the back and finds Shin curled asleep on a heap of wood shavings beside the furnace, black to the elbows, holding a pair of iron tongs like a doll. She looks up and sees the chimney: a tall brick stack with a cracked clay cap, tilted a little to one side.
4. Shin wakes with a jolt, scrambles to his feet and says he was only resting his eyes. Haru shares her amazake. He drinks it in two gulps and tells her, half asleep, that the ash door sticks and that Master Hachi says the chimney can wait until spring.
5. Haru writes it all in her booklet: the crack, the tired boy, the sticking door, the wood shavings by the furnace. She looks at the sky over the roofs, hard and clear and cold, and feels a small cold wind on the back of her neck.

## 14. The Wind That Would Not Stop
Synopsis: A dry north wind blows for three days; Grandfather remembers a great fire, and the alley prepares.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 14. Today's news: the wind has not stopped for three days.*
1. The north wind arrives: dry, howling, rattling every shutter. Sheets on the drying lines snap like flags. The ink dries too fast; Kichi has to keep damping the paper. Everyone's lips are cracked.
2. The alley checks its water barrels, and puts buckets by each door. From the fire watchtower the bell is tried, a single short test stroke that makes everyone jump. Mother Okiyo packs a bundle: warm clothes, a lantern, dried food, the family's papers, tied with a cord and put by the door.
3. Grandfather Tobei, wrapped in his blanket, tells the story of a great fire when he was a boy, the roar of it, the sky the color of a persimmon, the way the blocks were carried out in a barrow with the neighbors' children walking beside. He says it in a plain voice, without any drama, and Haru shivers.
4. He says a printer's blocks are his whole life; a house can be rebuilt but a block cannot. The family decides that if the bell rings, Father takes the block chest and Kichi the tools; Mother the bundle; Haru will help Grandfather down the ladder. She does not like her job. She says so. Nobody argues.
5. Haru goes up the loft ladder that night with the wind shouting at the roof. She lies on her mat and says over and over, in her head, "Nothing will happen." She has always trusted what she has seen. Tonight, for the first time, she hopes she is wrong.

## 15. Captain Daigoro's Pole
Synopsis: Haru meets the neighborhood firefighter and learns how fires are fought in a city of wood.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 15. Today's news: a firefighter came to see the print shop.*
1. Captain Daigoro, square and cheerful in his hooded coat and headband, comes through Owl Alley on his rounds carrying his tall standard with its paper tassels. He stops by the shop and asks to look at the blocks. "A fire will eat a wooden shop in the time it takes to drink tea," he says, happily.
2. He tells Haru how the neighborhood fights fires: not with water, which is scarce, but with hooks and ladders and the pulling down of houses in the path of the fire, so that the fire has nothing to burn. Haru is horrified. "Someone's house?" she says. "Someone's house," he says. "So that fifty others will stay."
3. He shows her the watchtower and lets her climb up beside the great bell, high above the roofs, and look over the whole ward: a sea of tiles and chimneys and thin smoke. The bathhouse chimney is a small dark finger on the far side of the alley.
4. Haru asks Captain Daigoro, half joking, what he would want to be printed about a fire. He thinks. "Where it started, and how people helped. And who needs help afterwards. Not who to blame." He says a fire has a cause, and it is nearly always smaller than people think.
5. Haru writes that in her booklet: "not who to blame." She has a strange feeling as she climbs down, that she has just been handed something she does not yet know how to use.

# PART FOUR: THE NIGHT OF THE NORTH WIND

## 16. The Bell in the Night
Synopsis: The fire bell rings; the alley runs; Haru sees, with her own eyes, exactly where the fire begins.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 16. Today's news: the bell rang in the night.*
1. Deep in the night Haru wakes to a sound she has never heard so close: the fire bell, fast and hard, over and over. The whole loft glows faintly orange at the shutters. Grandfather Tobei is already at the ladder, saying "Go, go, go." She goes.
2. The alley is chaos: people carrying children, bundles and buckets; the clack of geta on frozen mud; Captain Daigoro's voice ringing over everything; Father Sota carrying the block chest on his back with Kichi beside him carrying a bundle of knives and a cat in his other arm.
3. Haru sees the fire with her own eyes, from the bridge end of the alley: it is at the bathhouse. A thin red line runs along the base of the chimney, lifted by the wind, and a spark, a single bright spark, leaps from the cracked cap and lands on the shavings pile behind the furnace. The shavings burst into flame.
4. Shin runs from the back door with a bucket, sooty and terrified, his eyes huge in his black face. He throws water on the shavings, and the wind throws flames back. He does not stop until Hachi drags him back by his collar.
5. Haru stands frozen for one breath. Then she runs to help Okiku pull her stall cart away from the wall. She is holding the cart's handle so hard her hands hurt. Behind her the roof of the bathhouse goes up in one long orange sheet, and the wind carries it toward the tofu shop.

## 17. The Tofu Shop Comes Down
Synopsis: The firefighters pull down the tofu shop to save the alley, and at dawn the neighbors gather in the wet ash.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 17. Today's news: a tofu shop was sacrificed for a street.*
1. Captain Daigoro's crew, in their hooded coats, swing hooks and pull on ropes. Umekichi stands in the road with Osen, holding a pot and a bundle. Daigoro asks him with his hand on his shoulder. Umekichi looks at the shop where he has made tofu for twenty years, and says, "Do it."
2. The shop comes down in a roar of timber and dust. The fire reaches the gap and cannot cross. The wind drops, a little, as if it too is tired. By morning the alley is standing, gray and steaming and safe. Not one person is hurt. The bathhouse is a black frame; the tofu shop is a heap of planks.
3. The neighbors gather in the wet ashy road at dawn. They share what they have: Okiku serves hot broth from her cart to everyone without payment, and Mother Okiyo passes out rice balls from the bundle. Umekichi sits on a pile of planks with a tofu mold in his lap, looking at it.
4. Haru sits on the bridge rail with the paper booklet in her lap and the ashes still falling. She writes what she saw, the spark, the cracked cap, the shavings pile, the wind, in careful letters. She thinks: "I will print this. I will print it today."
5. Then Bunta's big voice comes down the alley, cracking with early morning cold, reading a sheet aloud to the crowd. Haru freezes. It is the Turtle House sheet, already printed, already wet.

## 18. The Wrong Sheet
Synopsis: The Turtle House sheet blames Umekichi's brazier; the alley turns on him; Haru knows it is false.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 18. Today's news: the wrong sheet reached the street first.*
1. Bunta reads: "Great Fire on Owl Alley! Sources say it began with the tofu maker's careless brazier!" Haru's stomach drops. Bunta is not smiling; he has seen who is standing in front of him. His voice slows.
2. People look at Umekichi. Somebody mutters. A woman steps back. Osen's hand goes to her mouth. Umekichi, who has lost his shop, now hears that it was his fault, and stands as if someone had hit him. Nobody shouts. That is the worst of it.
3. Haru knows exactly who is wrong. She looks at the alley: nobody else knows what she knows. She walks quickly to the Turtle House, where Jiro is alone, sitting on the step with an unsold stack of sheets. He has not slept. He says his father wrote it in the night, from what somebody said in the crowd, and that he tried to say it was not confirmed.
4. Jiro says nothing more. She sees he is ashamed, really and truly ashamed, and it startles her. She says, "Help me print the truth." Jiro swallows and says his father will never allow it. She says she is not asking his father.
5. Haru walks home through the ash. Father Sota reads her booklet from beginning to end without a word. Then he asks, "Is that everything?" She says yes. "Is that the whole truth?" She stops. It is not. There is Shin.

## 19. What Haru Saw
Synopsis: The whole truth includes a small boy's mistake; Haru must choose how to tell it, and goes first to the bathhouse owner.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 19. Today's news: the whole truth is heavier than half of it.*
1. In the shop, in a hush, Haru tells the family what she saw: the spark, the cracked cap, the shavings pile, the ash door left open, the boy with the bucket. She remembers Shin's face. She says that if she prints all of it, everyone will know Shin left the ash door open. She says if she does not, her sheet will be half a truth.
2. Grandfather Tobei says that half a truth is still a lie, only quieter. Mother Okiyo says the whole truth does not have to be told in the cruelest way. Father Sota says nothing, and puts a knife into her hand. "Cut away what does not belong," he says, "and leave what is true standing." Haru understands: the sheet can name the crack, the wind and the sparks, and leave out a child's name.
3. But Haru knows it is not enough to leave a name out and hope nobody asks. Hachi, the owner, will hear the word "furnace" and be ashamed and angry, and he might blame Shin in private. She decides to tell Hachi first, before anything is printed, with her own mouth, and to stand in front of him while she does it.
4. She goes to the black shell of the bathhouse alone. Hachi is sitting on a beam with his face in his hands. Haru's voice comes out small, then steadier. She tells him: the crack in the chimney, the sticking door, the tired boy, the wind. "It was a bad mix of small things. It was not one person's fault." She says it does not have to be one person's fault at all.
5. Hachi lifts his head. His eyes are red. He says, in a rough voice, that he has known about the chimney since the autumn. Behind him Shin, who has been hiding by the wall, steps out with his sooty jacket and says, "It was me. I left the door open. I was so tired." He is trembling. He is also standing very straight.

## 20. The Sheet That Told the Truth
Synopsis: Hachi, Shin, Jiro and the whole alley print and spread the true sheet together, the tofu shop rises again, and spring comes.
Header: *The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 20. Today's news: the ink did not freeze.*
1. Hachi puts his hand on Shin's head and says the fault is his own for asking a child to do a man's work. In front of the whole alley he says so. He also says the tofu maker did nothing wrong. A murmur goes through the crowd, and Umekichi lifts his face. Osen, who has been standing very straight, finally cries.
2. The print shop works all night: Father carving the correction, Grandfather Tobei's hand on the baren, Kichi printing until his arms shake, Mother Okiyo mixing black, and Jiro, who arrives in his yellow silk kimono with the Turtle House blocks under his arm and says, "Use ours. Ours are faster." The two shops print side by side. Master Kuroda stands in the doorway, sees the sheet, reads it, and eventually, silently, takes off his geta and comes in.
3. At dawn Bunta and Gohei cry the true sheet all through the ward, Bunta's voice booming in the cold, and it says: what began the fire, what stopped it, who lost a shop, who needs help. It does not blame anyone for a wind. The ward headman, Master Ito, reads a copy and says he will stand behind it, and that from now on he will read the Owl Alley Sheet first. Haru understands that this is the price of being allowed.
4. In the weeks after, the neighbors raise a new tofu shop board by board, Captain Daigoro lifting the biggest beam. Hachi mends the chimney and hires two boys; Shin is paid for a half day and goes to school in the afternoons. Jiro and Haru trade sheets across the alley every few days, and his are wonderful now: true stories that sound like wonders.
5. On the eve of spring the alley throws beans at the door and shouts "Demons out, good fortune in!" Haru, with a thumb that has healed, carves her first small block: an owl. Grandfather Tobei says, plainly, that truth is the edge and kindness the hand, and Haru has both at last. She hangs the first sheet on the line. It is a warm morning. The ink in the pot is soft and black. It did not freeze.
