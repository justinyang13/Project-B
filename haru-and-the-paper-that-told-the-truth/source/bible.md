# HARU AND THE PAPER THAT TOLD THE TRUTH — story bible

Warm, funny, quick-witted historical story for ages 10-12, set in a small family print shop on a back street of Edo (the great city that is now Tokyo), one winter long ago, from the year-end house-cleaning to the eve of spring. Eleven-year-old Haru lives above the Owl Alley print shop and has a nose for news. Her family prints calendars, greeting sheets and, on days when something really happens, a small one-page news sheet. Across the alley a bigger rival shop prints thrilling, made-up "news" that sells five times as fast. Haru wants the truth to win, learns that a true sentence used like a stick hurts people, and in the end, on the night of a windy fire, must tell a hard truth both bravely and kindly. Nothing grim: nobody is hurt in the fire (a tofu shop is pulled down as a firebreak and the neighbors rebuild it). The stakes are a neighbor's good name and one small boy's mistake. The book should make a reader smell ink and cherry-wood shavings, love a carving family, and want to check a rumor before passing it on.
IMPORTANT (user rule): the words "Japan", "Japanese", "Chinese", "Taiwanese", "Korean" must NOT appear anywhere in the title, subtitle, cover text, tagline or blurb, and should not appear in the story text at all. Culture comes through names, food, customs, work and places inside the story. Say "Edo" naturally.

## Voice
THIRD PERSON, PAST TENSE, close on HARU. Warm, brisk, gently funny, sensory (ink, wet paper, cherry-wood shavings, woodsmoke, frozen breath), like a very good read-aloud. Haru is curious and quick, a listener who notices what people say and what they do not say, and she has one big flaw: she says the true thing at once, sharp and loud, and does not see who it lands on. Humor comes from Kichi's eternal hunger and bad puns, Grandfather Tobei's dry sayings, the sellers' comically loud street voices, and the rival shop's outrageous headlines. Feelings show in hands, ears and ink-stained fingers, not named and then explained. Short and long sentences mixed; the real emotion arrives plainly.
Every chapter opens with one italic line, the headline of that day's sheet, on its own line, then a blank line, in this form: "*The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 1. Today's news: the ink froze in the pot.*" The number equals the chapter number. Headlines are short, true-sounding, a little funny, and each tells the reader what the chapter is about; the last chapter's headline answers the first ("the ink did not freeze"). The Header line is added by the script: never write it on any page.
Voice sample:
"*The Owl Alley Sheet, No. 1. Today's news: the ink froze in the pot.*

On the coldest morning of the year, the ink in the Owl Alley print shop froze solid, and the first person to find out was Kichi, who put his brush into the pot and got it stuck there like a spoon in a bowl of ice.

"It is not my fault," Kichi announced to the whole room, which was already awake and already cold.

Haru, who had come down the ladder from the sleeping loft in her thickest kimono with her breath smoking in front of her, looked at the pot, then at Kichi, then at the brush sticking straight up out of the black ice like a tiny flagpole. "That," she said, "is the truest thing I have ever seen you say."

Everybody laughed except Kichi, who had not eaten yet, and so was not at his best."

## The premise
Haru (age 11) lives in the family print shop on Owl Alley, a crooked back street of wooden houses near a canal and a small bridge in Edo. Her father, Master Sota, carves the blocks; her mother, Okiyo, mixes colors, keeps the money and mounts prints; her grandfather Tobei, a retired master printer with a bad shoulder, teaches Haru to use the baren; the apprentice Kichi (14) prints. They make almanac pages, New Year greeting sheets, notices, picture sheets for children and, when something really happens in the neighborhood, a small one-page news sheet called the Owl Alley Sheet, sold by street sellers who read it aloud. Family rule (Father's): "Print what happened, not what people say happened." Haru's job is to be the shop's ears: each morning she goes to the well, the bathhouse steps and the noodle stall with a little booklet of folded paper and writes down what she hears, and then Father asks, "Did you see it with your own eyes?"
Across the alley stands the Turtle House print shop (bigger, with two carvers and a shiny door curtain) whose sheets are wild: fox weddings on bridges, two-headed carp, ghosts in the canal. They sell out by noon. The owner's son Jiro (12) stands on the step in a yellow silk kimono and grins at Haru.
PART ONE: Haru proves her rule with a small true sheet about Okiku's noodles (a rumor says the noodles are cut with sawdust; Haru watches with her own eyes and it is untrue) and feels how good true news feels. PART TWO: Haru, proud and quick, prints a sharp true sheet exposing the Turtle House fox wedding and mocking Jiro, and calls him a liar in front of the whole terakoya class. The truth was right; the way she told it was cruel. Jiro is humiliated by his own father; Father is displeased; Grandfather teaches her with a knife: "The edge is truth. The hand is kindness. An edge with no hand cuts the wrong thing." An official warning arrives that unlicensed sheets are frowned on. PART THREE: Haru learns to carve (cuts her thumb: hurry), makes peace with Jiro (he confesses the fox wedding was a story he wished were true and that his father only praises sheets that sell), and notices the bathhouse: the chimney cap is cracked, tired little Shin (9) tends the furnace alone for long hours, and the north wind rises for three days. PART FOUR: on a windy night the fire bell rings; the fire starts at the bathhouse (a spark carried by the wind from a cracked chimney cap; Shin, half asleep, had left the ash door ajar); the neighbors run; the firefighters (carpenters) pull down the tofu shop as a firebreak, which saves the rest of the alley. Next morning the Turtle House sheet already reads that the fire was caused by tofu-maker Umekichi's careless brazier. It is false; the neighbors turn on Umekichi and Osen. Haru saw what really happened and has to tell it against the whole street. The true story includes a child's mistake, and telling it brutally would ruin Shin; leaving it out would make her sheet a half-truth. She chooses: she goes FIRST, alone, to the bathhouse owner Hachi to tell him what she saw (courage and kindness), Shin steps forward and says it himself, Jiro brings the Turtle House blocks and helps print the correction, the sellers read it aloud all over the ward, the ward headman (nanushi) stands behind Umekichi, the bathhouse owner apologizes, and the neighbors rebuild the tofu shop. The sheet's official standing is honest: from now on the ward headman reads it first. Epilogue on the eve of spring (Setsubun): beans, "Demons out, good fortune in!", Haru's first carved block (a small owl) and the headline "the ink did not freeze."

## Cast (keep looks and habits EXACTLY consistent)
- **Haru**, 11: small, quick, warm light skin, black hair pulled up into a tight small topknot bun tied with a bright vermilion cord, short bangs; a rust-orange cotton kimono, its sleeves tied back with a white cord, an indigo apron over it, straw sandals with red thongs, a folded-paper booklet on a string at her belt; ink on her fingers and often a smudge on her cheek. Habit: taps her writing brush against her teeth when she is thinking (never a pencil: there were none); says "I saw it with my own eyes" a lot (until she learns that seeing is not the same as telling well).
- **Father, Master Sota**, 40s: broad, calm, patient, a neat topknot, a white headband, a dark brown short work coat with the sleeves rolled up, dark trousers, straw sandals, small nicks on his hands; carves with a set of knives and chisels rolled in a cloth. Few words. Rule: "Print what happened, not what people say happened."
- **Mother, Okiyo**, 40s: neat bun with a tortoise-shell comb, a deep plum-purple kimono with a white apron, quick and warm, runs the counter and the money, mixes the colors; laughs with her hand over her mouth.
- **Grandfather Tobei**, 70s, Father's father: thin, a little bent, a bald crown with white hair pulled into a small white topknot, white bushy eyebrows, a soft blue-grey kimono with a folded towel around his neck, a wooden walking stick, rubs his bad right shoulder. Old master printer. Dry sayings. Teaches with the baren (a flat disc of coiled cord in a bamboo-leaf sheath). Refrain: "The edge is truth. The hand is kindness."
- **Kichi**, 14, the apprentice printer: gangly, cheerful, always hungry, a moss-green short coat, a blue-and-white checked towel tied around his head, sleeves rolled, straw sandals; terrible puns.
- **Jiro**, 12, the rival's son: neat and proud, smooth hair tied in a small topknot, a bright yellow silk kimono with a black diamond pattern, white split-toed socks and tall wooden geta; clever, lonely, hungry for his father's praise.
- **Master Kuroda** of the Turtle House, Jiro's father: heavy, loud, a gold-brown kimono with a black jacket over it; sells excitement.
- **Bunta** and **Gohei**, the two sellers (yomiuri): Bunta is big-voiced and round, in an indigo-and-white striped kimono hitched up, with a deep straw hat that hides his face, a folding fan; Gohei is thin and watchful, in a brown kimono, also in a straw hat, always looking over his shoulder for officials.
- **Okiku**, 60s, runs the noodle stall at the canal end: round, grey hair wrapped in a white towel, a faded pink quilted jacket, a grey apron.
- **Umekichi**, tofu maker: big, cheerful, white apron and white headband, huge forearms; and his wife **Osen**, small, quick, a dark green kimono and a white headscarf.
- **Hachi**, the bathhouse owner: gruff, thick eyebrows, a dark navy short coat, a towel over his shoulder, a loud voice, secretly ashamed. **Shin**, 9, the furnace boy: skinny, big eyes, hair cropped short, a soot-grey short jacket, bare arms black with soot, always yawning.
- **Teacher Ishida**, the terakoya (temple school) teacher: thin, a long grey face, grey short coat over a grey kimono, round glasses, a small abacus (soroban) always near.
- **Captain Daigoro**, the neighborhood firefighter (a carpenter): square, cheerful, a leather-hooded firefighter's coat trimmed in black, a headband, carries a long pole standard with a paper-tassel top (a matoi); Haru's friend after ch 15.
- **Ward headman (nanushi), Master Ito**: neat, elderly, stern, dark formal coat over a grey kimono; fair.
- Places: Owl Alley (a crooked back street of wooden houses with tiled roofs and shared wells, near a canal and a small wooden bridge); the Owl Alley print shop (one long room with a low carving table, a printing bench, shelves of cherry-wood blocks, drying lines hung with paper like laundry, a sleeping loft above reached by a ladder, a doorway curtain in indigo with a small white owl); the Turtle House (bigger, a glossy door curtain with a turtle); Okiku's noodle stall at the canal end; the tofu shop; the bathhouse with its tall brick-and-tile chimney; the terakoya in a temple hall; the fire lookout tower with its bell; the wooden bridge; the ward headman's house.

## Cultural notes (each web-verified 2026-09-26; keep the book consistent with these)
- Kawaraban ("tile prints"): cheap one-sheet news broadsheets, printed on cheap paper by woodblock in large quantities, about newsworthy events (disasters, murders, strange happenings, sometimes satire), printed soon after events, published without government authorization; the shogunate repeatedly restricted printing to prevent rumors and political commentary but could not stop them. An estimated million copies in about 300 kinds circulated in Edo alone. Publishers chose topics more to entertain and satisfy curiosity than to educate. Sellers were called yomiuri ("read and sell"): they read headlines and passages aloud to sell copies, hid their faces with braided straw hats and worked in pairs to escape the eyes of officials; some carried a shamisen. In THIS book the shops are small, fictional and anonymous: no shop name on the sheet, and the family is worried by an official warning but never in danger.
- Woodblock printing: four roles (publisher = hanmoto, artist, carver, printer). Carvers used chisels on blocks of smooth cherry wood, cutting away everything around the lines so the design stands up in relief; a separate block for each color; two registration marks (kento) carved into each block keep colors aligned; dampened washi paper (strong, fibrous, mulberry-based) is laid on the inked block and rubbed with a baren (a flat hand-held tool of twisted cord in a bamboo sheath); a color print needs a minimum of about ten impressions; a well-kept block can yield as many as 8,000 prints; blocks could be reused by planing. Pigments come from plants and minerals. A news sheet is simpler: mostly one block, black ink, cheap paper. More than 600 rental bookshops in Edo lent woodblock-printed books; literacy was high.
- Terakoya: private schools for commoners' children (attendance about 70% in Edo by 1800), taught reading, writing (calligraphy: copying models), and the abacus (soroban); a teacher (shishō) and a room were enough; boys and girls both attended (girls learned sewing and other arts too); textbooks (ōrai) about names, seasons, community rules, commerce; students sat seiza and greeted the teacher quietly; attendance was voluntary.
- Town life: row houses (nagaya) with shared wells and toilets, neighbors helping each other; food stalls (soba, tempura, big sushi); rice, miso soup, pickles, tofu as staples; seasonal festivals and fair days. Public bathhouses (sentō) were the great gossip places: people chatted in the changing area; a sento was a neighborhood fixture because most homes had no bath. Winter street foods: yaki-imo (roasted sweet potatoes, a hit in Edo from 1793) and amazake (sweet rice drink carried in portable stoves by street vendors).
- Year-end and New Year: soot-sweeping (susuharai) on December 13 (in the old calendar, the 13th day of the last month); whole households cleaned, with rice cakes and celebration, and groups tossed a helper in the air (dōage). Rice-cake pounding (mochitsuki) began around the 15th; New Year decorations kadomatsu and kagami-mochi; osechi dishes; children given otoshidama. Eating soba on the last night of the year (toshikoshi soba) grew out of merchants' habit of eating soba on the last day of each month (mid-Edo). Setsubun: scattering beans (mame-maki) with the call "Oni wa soto, fuku wa uchi!" ("Demons out, good fortune in!").
- Fires: "Fires and quarrels are the flowers of Edo." Wooden houses, charcoal heating and strong dry winter winds made fires frequent; fire watchtowers (hinomi-yagura) with a bell (hanshō) stood near the fire station and lookouts sounded the alarm; townspeople's own volunteer brigades (machibikeshi, mostly carpenters and builders) fought fires in the townspeople's quarters; water was scarce, so they pulled buildings down to stop the spread. People kept clothes and lanterns by their pillows and used mud-walled storehouses (dozō) for valuables. (Firebreak demolition is part of the story; the tofu shop is chosen because it stands next to the bathhouse.)
- Officials: the machi-bugyō (town magistrate, a samurai official) ran policing; ordinary townspeople were organized under a ward headman (nanushi, himself a townsman) who reported upward. In this book only the nanushi appears; officials elsewhere are just "the magistrate's men".
- Time: bells (toki no kane) told the hour. Use "the temple bell" and "the morning bell" only; never numbered hours.
- Clothing (safe, generic): kimono, obi, aprons, straw sandals, wooden geta, split-toed socks, headbands, straw hats; men with topknots; girls of ten or eleven with hair up in a small bun or bob. Children's kimono had shorter sleeves.
- Language (use only these, glossed in context, never invent others): kawaraban, yomiuri, baren, kento, washi, hanmoto (rarely), terakoya, soroban, nagaya (rarely), sentō (rarely, "bathhouse" mostly), hikeshi/machibikeshi (rarely), matoi (rarely), susuharai, dōage, mochi, kagami-mochi, kadomatsu, osechi, otoshidama, toshikoshi soba, yaki-imo, amazake, nanushi, Setsubun, "Oni wa soto, fuku wa uchi!", geta, kimono, obi, tofu, soba, shogun (once at most).

## Vocabulary and learning (build in naturally, never as a lecture)
- Target English words (in context, ~3 per chapter): rumor, headline, evidence, witness, accurate, exaggerate, correction, apology, reputation, gossip, sharp, steady, trace, spark, embers, bucket, precision, apprentice, craftsman, rival, humble, courage, kindness, honesty, warning, permission, register (of marks), damp, engrave, fibrous, cautious, hesitate, reckless, sensational, verify.
- Facts the reader learns: how a woodblock print is made (carve, ink, dampen, lay paper, rub with the baren, hang to dry); how the news traveled before newspapers (sellers reading aloud); what a temple school taught; what a year-end in Edo looked like (cleaning, rice cakes, soba); why fires were so common and how neighbors fought them; how to check a rumor (did you see it? who said it? who gains by saying it?); the difference between a true sentence and a kind, useful one.
- Kid-doable idea (optional, woven into the last chapters): "Haru's three questions" before you pass on news: Did I see it, or was I told it? Who gains if people believe it? Who could it hurt, and can I tell it without hurting them?

## The plan of the story
PART ONE: INK (ch 1-5): the frozen ink and the shop; the Turtle House fox wedding and the sellers; soot-sweeping day and the noodle rumor; making the small true sheet; selling it. PART TWO: FAST PAPER (ch 6-10): year-end rush; school and Haru's public cruelty to Jiro; her sharp sheet and the fallout; New Year's Eve soba and the alley together; New Year's Day and the official warning. PART THREE: THE SHARP KNIFE (ch 11-15): Haru carves and cuts her thumb; peace with Jiro; the bathhouse, the cracked chimney and tired Shin; the north wind; the firefighters and the plan for a fire night. PART FOUR: THE NIGHT OF THE NORTH WIND (ch 16-20): the fire bell and the fire; the morning-after sheet that blames the tofu maker; what Haru saw and what she decides; Hachi, Shin and the printing of the correction; the eve of spring.

## Motifs
The frozen ink pot; the baren; "Print what happened, not what people say happened."; Haru's paper booklet ("Things I saw with my own eyes"); the knife and the hand ("The edge is truth. The hand is kindness."); cherry-wood shavings; the sellers' voices; Jiro's yellow sleeve; Kichi's hunger and puns; the north wind; the owl on the door curtain; drying sheets hanging like laundry.

## The lesson (dramatize; NEVER preach)
Truth needs courage and kindness together. Courage alone makes Haru right and cruel (she humiliates Jiro). Kindness alone would leave Umekichi blamed for a fire he did not start (a soft lie). The book must show all three stages: (1) truth as a weapon (the fox-wedding sheet and the classroom shout), (2) learning the steady hand (carving, Jiro's confession, the fire), (3) telling the hard truth first to the person it hurts most, without naming a child in print, and standing beside the person who must say his own part. No one becomes perfect; Jiro and Hachi change too. One plain line near the end at most (Grandfather Tobei). Do not make the Turtle House villains: Master Kuroda sells excitement and Jiro wants his father's praise. Do not preach in the narration.

## Things to avoid
No violence, no injuries, no deaths (the fire destroys one shop and damages a bathhouse roof; everyone is safe). No romance. No exoticizing, tourist-guide voice or stereotypes; treat the shop, the neighbors and their customs with respect and ordinary warmth. Never invent Japanese words: only the verified list above. Never write "Japan", "Japanese", "Chinese", "Korean" in the story text. Do not describe real historical people or real disasters by name. Officials are firm, not cruel; there is no danger of arrest or punishment of a child. No modern speech ("okay", "OK", "cool", "guys"). No stacked similes; avoid "It wasn't X. It was Y."; avoid "heart hammered", "stone in the throat", "butterflies". No em dashes. IMAGE lines: always name the character (Haru, Father Sota, Mother Okiyo, Grandfather Tobei, Kichi, Jiro, Okiku, Umekichi, Osen, Hachi, Shin) and restate their clothes; paper sheets and door curtains in pictures are plain and blank (no writing), the print shop always has drying lines hung with blank paper.
