# PART ONE: EDGE

## 1. Ninety-Two Steps
Synopsis: Sana, nine days into life at Lowell Court, is impatient with everything and hides from the moving boxes behind the loading bay.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 1: Growing: nothing. Waiting: everything.*
1. Sana confesses she found the garden while hiding from moving boxes; Lowell Court is eleven concrete blocks around a car park, and after nine days in Block C she knows the lift takes forty seconds and the stairs are ninety-two steps, and does not know a single person.
2. The family in the flat: Mama the night nurse asleep in the daytime with a pillow over her head, Baba who leaves for the bakery at 3 a.m. and brings home warm bread, and Yusuf, six, missing his front teeth, narrating everything at top volume. Sana wants the flat to feel like home by Friday.
3. Sana's flaw, shown funny: a bean seed planted in a yogurt cup on the windowsill four days ago; she checks it every hour with a ruler and has dug it up twice to see if it's working. It isn't.
4. She meets Mr. Osei, the caretaker, tall, grey-moustached, keys on his belt, mopping the lift: he says "Nothing you plant will grow any faster for being looked at." She says she knows. She doesn't. He looks, briefly, at the back wall of the loading bay, as if remembering something, and goes on mopping.
5. Fleeing the boxes, Sana ducks behind the loading bay past a row of bins and a broken pigeon feeder and notices something odd: a strip of chain-link fence, pulled back at the bottom, and behind it a thick green smell (wet earth, mint, something like lemons) that has no business at the edge of concrete. She crawls through.

## 2. The Green Behind the Fence
Synopsis: Sana finds an overgrown walled garden, a hedge door and a very old rusted seed tin.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 2: Growing: a suspicion.*
1. On the other side of the fence: a wall of brambles, a gap, and beyond it an enormous overgrown garden, walled on three sides, filled with tall grass, a pear tree, a crumbling brick path and a bench under ivy. It is completely silent, which after Lowell Court is like walking into a held breath.
2. Sana measures things, because that is how she copes: the path is 14 steps, the wall is three of her tall, the pear tree has a hundred and six pears, none ripe. Nothing here is on a schedule. It bothers her and calms her at the same time.
3. She sits on the bench, and it hums, a low warm hum through the boards like a cat purring under the seat. She jumps up. It stops. She sits again, embarrassed, and says (to nobody) that she doesn't have any friends here, and the hum comes back for a second, gently.
4. Under the bench she finds a rusted tin the size of a shoebox, and on the lid, scratched with a nail, four rules. She reads them twice. "One seed a day. Plant it with one true thing, said aloud. Water morning and dusk. Never hurry a root."
5. Inside the tin: forty-one seed packets in faded handwriting (names like Patience Pea, Honest Basil, Whisper Sweetpea) and a tiny brass trowel. As she lifts one packet, from the tall grass at her ankle, a voice very slowly says: "Mm. Not... yet."

## 3. A Tortoise Named Gherkin
Synopsis: The voice belongs to a tortoise; Sana learns the garden is old and that it has a rule about impatience.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 2, later: Growing: a tortoise. Also a headache.*
1. She screams politely and jumps a foot. The tortoise is the size of a hat with a shell like a mossy cobblestone and eyes like wet raisins. It says its name is Gherkin, and it takes a very long time to say it, and Sana counts: eleven seconds for "Gherkin," nine for "I am three hundred years old, more or less," and forty for "and you have picked up the wrong seed."
2. Gherkin is unbelievably slow and completely unhurried; Sana keeps finishing his sentences and he keeps waiting for her to finish so he can say something else. "You are the sort," he says, "who digs up a seed to see if it is working." Sana, stung, says she has done it only twice.
3. Gherkin tells her, very slowly, that the garden has been waiting a long time for somebody to come through the fence. Not for a gardener; anybody would do. It has only one requirement: patience. Sana, who has none, says that's possibly a problem. "Mm," says Gherkin. "Possibly."
4. He explains the tin: the four rules are real. The garden doesn't punish anybody; it does something worse. "It exaggerates." A seed planted in a hurry grows in a hurry, and wrong. A true word makes it grow right.
5. Sana, pretending she isn't interested, asks what happens if she plants one now. Gherkin says it will not grow overnight; nothing here does. He says she may plant one seed a day and no more. She picks the Patience Pea because it seems like the seed she needs, laughs at herself, and pushes it into the dirt with a brass trowel. It is her first mistake, because she forgets the second rule.

## 4. One True Thing
Synopsis: Sana learns the second rule the hard way when her first seed grows into a very fast, very wrong plant.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 3: Growing: a problem.*
1. Sana returns after breakfast and finds the pea already a foot tall, a long crooked stem, blue-green and twitching, growing another inch every time she blinks. She forgot the true thing. Gherkin, munching a lettuce leaf in slow motion, says "Mm. Ah." It takes him fifteen seconds.
2. The pea vine grows very fast and very wrong: it wraps around a garden fork, coils up the pear tree, spirals across the brick path and starts to pull the tin toward the hedge like a fishing line. Sana runs about, tape measure out, measuring a vine that grows in front of her. Sixty centimeters, ninety, one meter twenty.
3. She asks how to stop it. Gherkin says the only cure is a true word said properly, and that a true word is not the same as a good one. Sana tries "I'm patient" (it grows faster and gets thorns), "I like it here" (thorns), "I'm fine" (a small pea flower appears and immediately wilts).
4. Finally, out of breath, sitting on the bench (which does not hum), she says the thing she hasn't said to anyone: "I didn't want to move here. I was really angry at Mama and Baba, and I still am, a bit." The vine goes still. It shrinks, slowly, leaf by leaf, into a small, healthy, neat pea sprout about the length of her hand. It is her first honest sentence in nine days.
5. Gherkin says: "Mm. There. Now it's growing." He says it will take three weeks to flower. She groans. The bench hums. She goes home holding her breath because she knows she has promised, without deciding to, to come back every day, and to her enormous surprise she wants to.

# PART TWO: ROOTS

## 5. The Rules of the Tin
Synopsis: Sana settles into a routine, tries to hurry the garden, and wrecks something small.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 8: Growing: pea (4.5 cm). Patience (0.1 cm).*
1. A week of dawn and dusk visits: she sneaks out before school and after it, waters the pea from a bent watering can Gherkin says has always been there, and measures it. In seven days it grows four and a half centimeters. She writes it in the notebook Mama bought her for schoolwork and titles it Garden Log.
2. School: Sana sits in the back, never puts up her hand, and hates it. Theo Kowalski, the skateboard boy in Block C, catches her mid-yawn and says she looks like she's been up at dawn "doing crime." She says gardening. He laughs so hard he has to sit on his board. She hates him for exactly nine seconds.
3. To speed things up she tries fertilizer (a spoonful of Yusuf's cereal), a desk lamp on an extension lead, and singing. The result is a tomato plant that grows seventeen tomatoes in an hour and then falls over from exhaustion, and Gherkin's one-line review: "Mm. Show-off."
4. She plants a second seed with a true thing, a small one ("I get scared when people look at me") and a tiny blue flower like a bell appears three days later, and, when Sana whispers to it, it rings very faintly. It is the best thing she has ever made. It's also, she realizes, not made: it was grown.
5. She reads the rules again on the lid and sees, in smaller scratches at the bottom, a line she'd missed: "Someone must speak for it when the day comes." She asks Gherkin what day. "Mm," he says. "Not yet." She feels the back of her neck go cold, and cannot say why.

## 6. Yusuf Cannot Keep a Secret
Synopsis: Yusuf follows Sana to the garden and cannot keep the secret; the family and a neighbor learn something is up.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 15: Growing: pea (8 cm). Problems: Yusuf.*
1. Yusuf, six, missing front teeth, has been tailing Sana for three days with the stealth of a marching band. On day fifteen he squeezes through the fence behind her, shouts "THERE'S A TORTOISE," and is so thrilled he can't stop shouting.
2. Gherkin, unimpressed, says "Mm. Loud." Yusuf asks the tortoise a hundred questions in a minute; Gherkin answers one in a minute; Yusuf is delighted by both. Sana makes him swear by a spit-shake to tell nobody. He swears.
3. He tells Baba at breakfast (in a stage whisper), then Mama, then the man at the bakery, and then Mrs. Ilves on the fourth floor, who watches everything from her window and has been waiting for someone to mention it for thirty years. Sana finds out when Mrs. Ilves stops her on the stairs with a knitting bag and says, "So it's still there."
4. Mrs. Ilves says the garden used to be the pride of Lowell Court: Mr. Osei built it with his wife Ada, and it fed half the estate. When Ada died he shut the gate, and everybody stopped talking about it. She won't say more.
5. That night Mama, back from her shift with tired eyes, sits on the edge of Sana's bed and asks about the garden. Sana tells her some of it. Mama says only, "You're smiling when you talk about it. I hadn't seen that in a while." Sana lies awake feeling caught and, unexpectedly, kept.

## 7. Mr. Osei's Keys
Synopsis: Sana asks Mr. Osei about the garden; he refuses to say, and Theo turns out to have been watching.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 22: Growing: pea (13 cm). Questions: several.*
1. Sana finds Mr. Osei mending the lift and asks him, as casually as a person can with her heart going like a sewing machine, whether he knows about the garden behind the loading bay. He stops. He looks at his keys, and then at the concrete floor. "There is nothing behind the loading bay," he says gently. "Just a fence."
2. She says there's a bench that hums. He says, very quietly, "Ada liked that bench." Then he picks up his bucket and leaves, and the lift, with its awful timing, arrives, and Sana rides up alone with a hot face and no answers.
3. On the stairs, Theo Kowalski is sitting on the ninety-second step doing nothing, which, she realizes, is what he does when he's lonely. He says he followed her yesterday; he saw her go through the fence. He doesn't want to tell anybody; he wants to see. Sana says no. Theo says he knows about peas. She says nobody knows about peas. He says his grandmother grew forty types, in Kraków, on a balcony.
4. Sana, who has never let anyone see anything of hers, takes a huge breath and says yes. It's a decision she makes the way you make a decision to jump into cold water: the whole body at once.
5. In the garden, Theo stands in the doorway for a full minute with his hood down and his mouth open. He says, "It's not a dump. It's a garden." The bench hums, warm and clear. Gherkin says "Mm. Another one," and takes eleven seconds to finish it.

## 8. The Pumpkin That Ate a Bicycle
Synopsis: A comic disaster: Theo hurries a seed and the garden exaggerates in the most literal way.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 29: Growing: pea (19 cm). Also: a pumpkin. (See below.)*
1. Theo is not patient either. Given the day's seed (a small pumpkin) he plants it in two seconds, says "I'm... good at plants?" as his true thing, and steps back. Sana says the true thing has to be true. Theo says it is. Gherkin says "Mm. Is it."
2. The pumpkin vine erupts overnight like a green fountain: by the time they arrive next day it has swallowed a wheelbarrow, half the path, the tin and Theo's skateboard, which he left by the wall. The pumpkin at its center is the size of a small car and is, unmistakably, sulking.
3. They try everything: knives, tugging, negotiation. Yusuf, who has slipped in, lies down against the pumpkin and whispers "sorry" in its ear, which does nothing but is the sweetest thing anybody has said all week. Gherkin: "Mm. Not... that."
4. Theo, embarrassed, mutters the actual true thing: he's not good at plants at all; his grandmother died in March and he never learned, and he's been sitting on staircases for three months because it's the only place nobody asks him about it. The vine shrinks to a neat pumpkin the size of a cabbage, and sets Theo's skateboard on the grass as neatly as a waiter.
5. Nobody says anything. It is a very good silence. Sana, without thinking, says "I'm sorry about your grandmother," and finds she means it enough to feel it in her chest. Theo scuffs his shoe. "Yeah," he says. "Thanks." The bench, behind them, hums as if it has been waiting a very long time.

## 9. Ada's Roses
Synopsis: The garden shows Sana a room she wasn't supposed to find, and she learns the story of Mr. Osei and his wife.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 36: Growing: pea (26 cm). Discovering: a door.*
1. Bored of waiting for the pea to flower, Sana follows a thread of honeysuckle behind the pear tree and finds, hidden in the hedge, a small green door with a brass handle in the shape of a leaf. Gherkin says: "Mm. You found it," with unusual speed, which worries her.
2. Behind the door: a Spring Room, entirely spring in late autumn: blossom, bees, cold-morning sun. The far wall is covered in climbing roses in full bloom, hundreds of them, red and white, and at its foot stands a small stone marked with a single word: ADA.
3. Gherkin, in one of his longer speeches (it takes four minutes), tells her Ada was the first person to say a true word in the garden, on the day she met Mr. Osei, and that every rose on this wall is something she said. He says the roses bloom when someone in the garden tells the truth, and bloom in a burst the year Ada died, all at once, and have bloomed every day since. Nobody has told Mr. Osei.
4. Sana, hands shaking, says she wants to tell him. Gherkin says: "Not yet." Sana is furious; she has never been so furious at a tortoise. He explains that people cannot be made to enter a garden. They have to open the gate themselves. "And you cannot hurry a person any more than a root."
5. On the way home she meets Mr. Osei at the top of the loading bay stairs, with a bucket and a look of great tiredness, and she wants to tell him so badly she thinks she might burst. Instead she says, "Good evening," which feels like the biggest thing she has ever held back. He nods. "Good evening, Sana," he says, and, absurdly, that is the beginning.

# PART THREE: THE NOTICE

## 10. The Notice on the Door
Synopsis: A notice on the door of Block C announces the sale of the land and a car park in one year.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 60: Growing: pea (flowering!). Bad news: a notice.*
1. The pea flowers on day sixty: a single white flower with a blue center, exactly as small as a thumbnail, and Sana, Theo and Yusuf stand round it for ten minutes without saying anything, like people at a christening. Gherkin: "Mm. Nicely done." She has measured every centimeter for sixty days and would do it again.
2. On her way home, a crowd around the door of Block C: a notice on the glass, headed GREENFIELD HOMES PLC, announcing the "Lowell Court Regeneration Scheme": the derelict land to the rear of the loading bays is to be sold and cleared for a 140-space car park, work to begin this time next year.
3. Sana reads it three times. The phrase "derelict land" makes her want to throw the bin at the wall. Mrs. Ilves, at her elbow, says "Hm." The neighbors grumble about the parking, about the noise, about the whole notice. Nobody, she realizes, knows what's behind the fence, because nobody has ever looked.
4. In the garden, Gherkin, told the news, does not say "Mm." He says nothing for eight whole minutes. Then he says, very slowly, that the last line of the lid was true: someone must speak for it when the day comes. The day, it appears, is this one.
5. Sana says she can't. She's terrible at speaking; she's never put up her hand in a class in her life. Gherkin says: "Mm. Not... yet." And Sana, for once, understands that "not yet" doesn't mean "no."

## 11. The Summer Room
Synopsis: Sana explores the Summer Room and learns that watering isn't glamorous but nothing lives without it.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 74: Growing: tomato (2 m). Sweating: me.*
1. The second door, behind the hedge, is a Summer Room: heat, cicadas, a hundred green tomato plants staked in rows, a lemon tree, a vegetable bed the size of a school hall. It is high summer in November, and, dazzled, Sana forgets to be impatient for nearly a full hour.
2. The Summer Room needs water twice a day, morning and dusk, with a can that holds about four liters; one hundred plants take about forty trips. The first day is a joy. The fifth is a slog. The tenth day, she and Theo divide it in shifts and make it a game. Yusuf, appointed Chief Puddle, waters his own shoes.
3. Mr. Osei is seen more often at the loading bay lately, standing at the fence with his hands clasped behind him, staring at the strip of green. Sana watches him from her window, holding a watering can. He never comes through.
4. A scrap of a conversation on the stairs: Mrs. Ilves tells Sana that people who've lost somebody often can't go near the thing they loved because it hurts too much. "It isn't lack of caring," she says. "It's an excess of it." Sana carries that into the Summer Room like a full watering can, carefully, trying not to spill.
5. First fruit: a tiny tomato the size of a marble, warm from the sun, perfect. Gherkin lets her pick it ("Ask before you pick" is Rule 5) and Sana asks the plant, and it says nothing, and she picks it. It tastes like summer. She puts half in Yusuf's mouth and half in Theo's and eats the stem herself and thinks: this is what all the waiting was for.

## 12. The Party
Synopsis: Sana skips two waterings to go to a party, and the Summer Room wilts; her first real failure of responsibility.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 90: Growing: nothing. Wilting: everything. (My fault.)*
1. Nadia Okafor, a girl in Sana's class, invites Sana to her birthday sleepover on Friday. It is the first invitation Sana has ever received in this city. Sana says yes so fast she forgets to breathe, and only later remembers Friday dusk is a watering and Saturday dawn is a watering.
2. She tells Theo she'll do the waterings herself before and after; she tells herself the plants will be fine. At the party she is more nervous than she has ever been in her life, and, in the middle of a game of charades, she has an entire minute of pure happiness.
3. Saturday morning, walking home early from the sleepover in yesterday's clothes, she comes to the garden late and finds the Summer Room wilted: tomato plants brown and slumped, lemons dropped in the dust, the whole heat of the room gone thin and yellow. Theo is there, with a watering can, exhausted; he did all of it alone and it was too late for half.
4. She says she's sorry. Theo says it's fine, and his face says it isn't. Gherkin, slow as ever, says "Mm. This is what happens." He doesn't scold. He says the garden did not punish her; the water did not come, so the plants did not drink. It is only arithmetic.
5. Sana sits down in the dust with the broken tomato plants and cries the kind of cry where you don't make any noise. It turns out that responsibility does not feel like being good. It feels like being needed by something small that cannot ask.

## 13. Second Chances Are Slow
Synopsis: Sana and Theo replant the Summer Room and Sana speaks her true thing about failing.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 91: Planted: 12 seeds. Forgiven: 1 (not by me).*
1. She cancels everything, even Yusuf's park trip, and spends the whole day in the Summer Room with Theo, pulling dead stalks, replanting what can be replanted, and burying what can't. Gherkin drifts between the rows, watching, saying "Mm" in different tones.
2. She tries to plant twelve seeds at once. Gherkin says "One a day." She says the garden is dying; she can't wait. He says the garden is not dying, it's resting, and one at a time is the only speed there is. She plants one, and says a true thing: "I let it die because I wanted to be liked more than I wanted to be dependable." The lemon tree drops a single yellow leaf and puts out a bud.
3. Theo tells her that his grandmother said the worst thing you can do to a plant is to love it only when it's easy. He says it's the best thing she ever told him and he didn't know what it meant until this week.
4. That night Sana finds she can't sleep. She goes down the ninety-two steps at midnight, in her pajamas, and waters the plants by moonlight. It is not required; it's just what a person does once she knows it's wanted. She counts every can. Forty. Forty-one. Forty-two.
5. Mr. Osei is at the fence again when she comes out. Neither of them says anything. He steps aside to let her pass, and, as she does, he says, in a low voice like the humming of a bench: "Someone's been watering." She says, "Yes." He says: "Good." And walks off toward the light in the caretaker's office, and Sana stands there with a watering can trying to work out whether she has just been thanked.

## 14. The Meeting at the Hall
Synopsis: The first public meeting on the car park; Sana tries to speak and freezes.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 105: Practicing: speaking. Results: none.*
1. The Community Hall on a Thursday night: plastic chairs, a tea urn, a projector, three hundred residents, and Ms. Vandermeer, the planner from Greenfield Homes, in a neat grey suit, with charts. She is brisk, courteous and completely unaware of the garden. She says the scheme will bring parking, jobs and "a much-needed tidying." People clap. Sana grips the seat.
2. She had a plan: put up her hand, stand, say the garden exists, ask them to come and look. She has practiced in the mirror. She has written it on her hand. When the chair says "Any questions?" she puts up her hand, and it is up, and everybody turns, and her mouth opens and the whole speech leaves her head at once.
3. What comes out: "I... it... never mind." She sits down. Somebody coughs. Ms. Vandermeer gives her a kind, professional smile and says "Perhaps after the meeting, dear," which is the worst thing anyone has ever said to her. Theo, next to her, says nothing, and doesn't leave.
4. Mr. Osei is at the back with his cap in both hands; she sees his face as he looks at the projector slide showing the garden as a gray rectangle marked LAND. He looks like a man watching a boat leave. He doesn't put his hand up either.
5. Afterwards on the steps, Mrs. Ilves (who came in her slippers) says: "It's easier to bloom in the dark than under a lamp." Sana says she doesn't know what that means. Mrs. Ilves says she doesn't either, but it sounded good, and that she has known Sana for only a hundred and five days and already thinks she is very brave. Sana says she froze. "Yes," Mrs. Ilves says. "Then you'll thaw."

# PART FOUR: WINTER

## 15. The Winter Room
Synopsis: Sana finds the last door and learns what patience looks like when nothing is happening.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 130: Growing: nothing visible. (That's the point.)*
1. Snow, for real, on Lowell Court; the whole estate goes quiet and clean and, for one day, looks like somewhere else. The garden's outdoor beds are bare and white. Behind the last hedge door, Sana finds a glass house: the Winter Room, warm and dark and smelling of earth, with a hundred clay pots in rows and nothing growing in any of them.
2. Gherkin says, with an approach to enthusiasm, that this is the most important room in the garden. It is where things wait. Every seed here has been planted and nothing shows; the whole room is a lesson in believing in what you can't see. Sana says that sounds dreadful. Gherkin says: "Mm. Yes."
3. Her task: plant a seed in the Winter Room with a true thing, and tell no one, and come back every day for the whole winter, and water it, and do nothing else. It won't sprout until spring. She might never see it. She plants a small brown seed with a true thing she says only inside her own head, and Gherkin, kindly, pretends not to hear.
4. Days pass; nothing happens. She checks nothing. She measures nothing. On the fourth day she catches herself digging with a finger; she stops, puts the finger back in her pocket and goes to help Theo mend the fence. It is the first time she has beaten herself at her own game.
5. Mrs. Ilves' scarf, seen over the balcony rail, is now four meters long. It grows one row a day, she says. She started it on the day the garden was shut. She's been knitting it thirty years. She doesn't say who it's for.

## 16. Gherkin's Story
Synopsis: Gherkin tells the story of the first gardener and why he is the way he is.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 150: Listening: for about an hour. (Gherkin's fastest ever.)*
1. A cold night; the whole garden asleep under frost; Sana, unable to sleep, goes down with a torch and finds Gherkin awake, alone by the bench. It takes him twenty minutes to say "I want to tell you a story." She waits. It is the best twenty minutes of her week.
2. The story: three hundred years ago a boy planted an apple pip in a walled garden and was so impatient he dug it up every morning to see it. It died. He was so sorry he planted another, and this time he sat beside it and did nothing, every day, for a year. The apple tree grew. He grew old beside it.
3. "That boy," Gherkin says, "had a very long life, and never once hurried it. I was his tortoise. I was already old when he was born." He says that when the boy died the garden asked him to stay, and he has been waiting ever since, for someone to tell the truth in it and mean it.
4. Sana asks if he minds being slow. He takes a very long time to answer. "Mm," he says at last. "I mind the world's being fast. Not myself." He says: "It is not that you are slow, Sana. It is that you keep leaving before it grows."
5. She falls asleep against the bench at four in the morning with her jacket over Gherkin's shell, which he does not remove. When she wakes, in the pale blue light, the bench is humming, and a single early snowdrop is up by her hand.

## 17. Practicing on Plants
Synopsis: Sana learns to speak by talking to the garden, and the neighbors start to bring true words.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 190: Practicing: speaking. Results: getting better.*
1. Sana decides that if she can't speak to people she'll speak to plants. She stands in the Spring Room and delivers the speech she wanted to give at the hall, one sentence at a time. The roses do not laugh. The third time through, the wall of roses begins, very slowly, to bloom.
2. Theo listens and offers notes. He says: don't say "sorry" at the start; don't say "just"; and stop looking at your shoes. She says he's very rude for someone who's never spoken in class either. He says that's exactly why. They practice until the streetlights come on.
3. Word spreads through Block C the old-fashioned way: Yusuf. By the end of the week Mrs. Ilves, Nadia Okafor and her mother, three old men from Block A, and a family from the ninth floor arrive at the fence with hats and thermoses. Each has been told there's a tortoise. Each says it can't possibly be true. Each goes quiet at the door.
4. The garden's response is unmistakable and a little embarrassing: every visitor is asked to say one true thing about the place. The roses bloom, the tomatoes ripen out of season, the bench hums like a church organ. The old men from Block A discover that they were all children here, in the garden, before it was closed.
5. They start bringing true words as offerings: a woman says she wants to be told she can do it; a boy says he's frightened of the new school; an old man says nothing, and then says he misses his wife. Mr. Osei watches from the loading bay, from behind a pillar, then from a little closer, then, at last, from the gate, where he stands with his hand on the iron and his eyes shut.

## 18. Someone Must Speak
Synopsis: Mr. Osei enters his garden for the first time in years, and Sana sees what she's been waiting for.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 220: Growing: everything. Someone came.*
1. It is a rainy evening and everyone has gone home. Sana is watering the Winter Room when the glass door opens, and Mr. Osei stands there in his overalls with his cap in his hands, shaking his head as though he's waking from a very long sleep. He says: "I heard the bench."
2. He walks through the garden without speaking, slowly, touching things: the pear tree, the ivy, the wall. In the Spring Room he stops before the stone marked ADA and stands there a long time. The roses, all of them, bloom at once, in a shivering burst, though it is mid-January and snowing outside.
3. He says, in a voice like rust coming off a hinge, that he couldn't come because every time he looked at it he saw what was missing. Sana tells him what Mrs. Ilves said about caring too much. He says, "Yes," and puts his hand flat on the stone. "I built it so somebody would use it," he says. "I closed it so nobody could hurt it. I got it backwards."
4. Gherkin, from the path, says the longest sentence of his life, in only six minutes: "Mm. I have been waiting for you for thirty years, Osei. You are late. It is good to see you." Mr. Osei laughs and cries at the same time, which turns out to be how the whole thing goes.
5. He asks Sana what she wants him to do. She tells him the truth: the Council's meeting is in two weeks. They need someone who built the garden to stand up and say so, someone who was here. He says his English has never been good enough for meetings, and then, after a long time, says he'll try.

## 19. The Council Meeting
Synopsis: Sana and Mr. Osei speak at the Council meeting; the outcome is not what anyone expects.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 234: Speaking: yes. Shaking: also yes.*
1. The Council Chamber, high-ceilinged, cold, with wood panels and a dais; the entire population of Block C has come on the bus, in coats, carrying tomatoes, a pear branch and a tortoise in a shoebox, which the clerk pretends not to see. Ms. Vandermeer arrives with a laptop and a slightly bewildered expression at the size of the crowd.
2. Mr. Osei speaks first, in his slow careful English, holding his cap. He says he made a garden on the land behind the bins thirty years ago with his wife. He says it fed half the estate. He says, "I closed it because I was sad. I was wrong." He sits down to silence, and then to an enormous noise.
3. Sana's turn. Her knees are made of cotton; her mouth is a desert; the speech is in her hand and on the back of her hand and on her heart. She stands. She looks at the clerk and the microphone and the room. She says "My name is Sana Haddad and I am eleven, and I am shaking," and the room laughs kindly and she finds the rest of her sentence in the laughter.
4. She says what she came to say: that she planted a seed in the garden and it took sixty days to flower. That she learned more from waiting for a pea than from anything she's done in her life. That you cannot see a garden from a chart. That the paper calls it "derelict land" and it is not, it is the opposite. She invites everyone, including Ms. Vandermeer, to come and look for themselves, and to say one true thing, and to see.
5. The council doesn't cheer. It debates for forty minutes and votes to postpone the decision for a year, to allow a "trial period" and a formal site visit, and Sana, dizzy, understands this is neither a win nor a loss. It's a not yet. She almost laughs out loud: for the first time, she is grateful for the words.

## 20. Day 365
Synopsis: Ms. Vandermeer visits the garden; spring arrives; Sana's Winter Room seed sprouts and the year ends where it began.
Header: *Garden Log, Day 365: Growing: everything. Waiting: still. (Better.)*
1. A week later, Ms. Vandermeer comes to the fence in a raincoat, alone, at dusk, with no charts. She stands at the gap for a long time. Then she asks, awkwardly, whether she may come in. Sana, at the gate with a watering can, says the only rule is to say one true thing. Ms. Vandermeer, after a pause, says she has never once in twenty years of planning gone to look. She sits on the bench. It hums.
2. Spring comes to Lowell Court, ordinary and astonishing. The outdoor beds are green; the pear tree flowers; the whole estate wakes as if a lid has been lifted. The community of Block C, with a hundred and fifty volunteers, Mrs. Ilves' scarf finished at last (nine meters, given to Mr. Osei "for the cold"), and a trial-year agreement signed by Greenfield Homes and the Council for a community allotment.
3. In the Winter Room, in a clay pot in the third row, something green is coming up. Sana had forgotten what she planted, not the seed, but the true thing. She kneels. She says nothing. It is a tiny brown-green shoot, wet, very determined, about as tall as a thumbnail. She measures it, of course. It is 2 centimeters. It is enormous.
4. Yusuf, Theo, Nadia and Mr. Osei stand round her in a half circle. Gherkin, at her elbow, takes a full minute to say it, and it's the shortest thing he has ever said: "Mm. There." She says that it took a year. He says: "Mm. Yes. And?"
5. That night on the fourth floor, with the window open and the smell of wet earth coming up from the edge of the concrete, Sana writes the last Log entry of the year. She reads the first one back and laughs: Growing: nothing. Waiting: everything. She writes: Day 366. Planted: one seed. True thing: I can wait. She closes the notebook, goes down ninety-two steps in the dark and, before bed, waters it.
