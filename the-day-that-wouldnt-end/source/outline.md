# PART ONE: THE LAST SATURDAY

## 1. Forty-One Days of Dinosaur
Synopsis: At 6:47 on his last Saturday in Ohio, Max is roared awake by Gus in his T. rex costume. The house is boxes; Max announces to the reader that he plans to make this the worst day of his life.
1. Open on Gus's roar at 6:47 with the voice-sample energy: introduce Gus, the 41-day costume, and Max's blunt claim that this is the worst day of his life. Hook: the truck comes at 7:00 tomorrow.
2. Max's stripped bedroom: bare walls, pale rectangles where posters were, his bed the only furniture, a box labelled MAX'S STUFF (MISC.) in Mom's handwriting. He tells the reader about Dunmore, Ohio: the only town he's ever lived in, and Tucson, 1,700 miles away, "which I looked up, and it's mostly cactus."
3. Waffles the corgi steals Max's left slipper (7:13) and hides under the dining table; Max crawls after it and finds a lost sock, a Lego and a fossilized french fry. Max snaps at Gus when he roars again ("Roar at someone else!") and Gus's face goes small. Max sees it and pushes it away.
4. Kitchen at 7:22: Dad in his fish apron burns the toast, the smoke alarm screams, Dad waves a dish towel and delivers his first pun about anti-gravity. Max refuses to laugh on principle. Mom passes with her six-page clipboard checklist and pencil behind her ear, ticking things without looking up.
5. Max sits at the breakfast box-table with his charcoal toast and declares to the reader his plan: he will not enjoy one minute of today, and he will make sure everyone knows why. End on a hook: "It was a good plan. It was the only one I had. It turned out to be the worst plan in the history of plans, but I'm getting ahead of myself."

## 2. The Hot Dish and the Laminated Plan
Synopsis: Mrs. Abernathy brings her Saturday casserole, Jonah texts about a plan, Mom's kitchen-box crisis, and Max and Tessa have a bruising fight in the garage.
1. 8:05: Mrs. Abernathy, tiny in her floral gardening hat, arrives with a tuna noodle casserole with potato chips on top and says the line about the hot dish and the trivet. Max doesn't say thank you. Dad has to say it twice as loudly to make up for him.
2. 8:30: Max's phone buzzes with Jonah's text ("I HAVE A PLAN. Be at my house at 2. Wear shoes you can get wet."). Max stares at it, types "maybe," deletes it, and puts the phone face down. He tells the reader about Jonah: best friends since kindergarten, the bike helmet, the time they built a raft that sank in eight inches of water.
3. 9:15: The box marked FRAGILE/KITCHEN slips out of Dad's grip; Mom dives and catches it a hand-span from the floor. Everyone freezes, then laughs, all except Max, who walks out. Mom calls after him, tired: "Max. Please. Just today." He says "Just today is all I've got left" and hates how it sounded.
4. 10:30: In the garage, Max and Tessa (purple streak, drumsticks in her back pocket) grab the same last box, which holds her drum practice pad and old band posters and his soccer trophies. Fast, mean sibling argument. Tessa: "You think you're the only one this is happening to?" Max says something snide about her having no friends anyway. She goes still, then slams the door.
5. Max stands in the garage with the box. He tells the reader it was the meanest thing he'd said to anyone all year, and that he'd said it because it was true and because it was the easiest thing to reach. He hears her drumsticks tapping on the wall upstairs, fast, then not at all. Hook: the doorbell rings at 11:00, and there is a bike helmet on the front step.

## 3. The Clock Comes to Dinner
Synopsis: Jonah arrives with a laminated itinerary and Max blows him off. Lunch on paper towels. Grandma Bea arrives with the cuckoo clock and hangs it in the hall. Max skips Jonah's Extravaganza and hides on the garage roof.
1. 11:00: Jonah on the doorstep in his bike helmet with a laminated itinerary titled THE LAST DAY EXTRAVAGANZA (creek, Putt-Putt Palace, Scoops). Jonah reads each item with pride. Max says he "might" come. Jonah's grin wobbles for half a second and then he puts it back on. "Two o'clock. Wet shoes."
2. 12:30: Lunch is peanut butter sandwiches on paper towels standing in the empty kitchen because the plates are packed. Dad makes a pun about the bread; Mom is on page four of her list; Tessa doesn't come down; Gus growls at his sandwich before biting it. Max notices, and refuses to notice, that nobody is really eating.
3. 1:15: An enormous green Buick honks a tune in the driveway. Grandma Bea (tiny, huge round sunglasses, red lipstick, jingling gold bangles) climbs out, hauling a laundry basket with the cuckoo clock in it. She has owned it since 1961 and she has decided Max will keep it. Max says he doesn't want a clock, he wants to stay. She looks at him over her sunglasses and says, "Nobody asked what you want, caro. I'm asking what you'll do."
4. Grandma Bea hangs the clock on the last hook in the front hall, and introduces Gerald, the wooden bird with the crooked beak and angry eyebrows. At the quarter hour Gerald pops out, gives one furious cuckoo, and disappears. Gus roars back at him. Grandma Bea says Gerald has opinions. Max says clocks don't have opinions. Gerald pops out again, ten seconds off schedule, as if to disagree.
5. 2:00: Jonah's Extravaganza begins without Max. Max climbs out his window onto the garage roof with a bag of pretzels and watches Jonah pedal by, look up and down the street, and pedal on alone, the laminated itinerary flapping. Max eats a pretzel. It tastes like nothing. Hook: "I told myself I didn't care. I'm going to be honest with you for the rest of this book, so: I lied."

## 4. Eleven Fifty-Nine
Synopsis: The sprinkler soaks the mailman, the family's last dinner on the floor goes badly because of Max, and at 11:58 he makes his wish beside the clock.
1. 4:10: From the roof, Max sees Mr. Grady's sprinkler soak the mailman, who yells; it is the only thing all day that almost makes him laugh, and he swallows it like a pill. Mom's voice from below calls him for dinner: the last supper in the house.
2. 5:30: Pizza on the empty living-room floor, paper plates, family in a circle. Dad tries a toast: to the house, to Ohio, to new cactus adventures. Mom tears up over her clipboard. Gus has a slice through the T. rex mouth hole. Tessa, silent, doesn't look at Max.
3. Mom asks everyone to say one thing they'll miss. Dad: the kitchen. Tessa (barely): "My drum room." Gus: "Everything. Even the stairs." Max is asked last and says, "Nothing, because it's all going to be worse there." Tense silence; Waffles chews the corner of a pizza box. Mom asks him to go to bed early and he says gladly.
4. 9:00 to 11:00: On an air mattress in his empty room, Max can't sleep. He hears the family settle. He hears Dad snore, Mom's pencil scratching in the hall by flashlight, Tessa's drumsticks tapping softly on the wall. He tells the reader what he's afraid of: waking up to the truck, and never getting to be a person who lives here again.
5. 11:58: Max creeps downstairs. The front hall is dark except for the moonlight through the glass door, and the clock hangs on its hook ticking. He stands under it. He whispers, "I wish today would never end." Gerald's door pops open. One crooked cuckoo. It is 11:59. Max laughs, feels stupid, and goes to bed. Hook: he falls asleep smiling, "which should have been my first clue."

# PART TWO: SATURDAY, AGAIN

## 5. Roar, Again
Synopsis: Max wakes to Gus's 6:47 roar on the same Saturday, and works out that it isn't a dream: everything happens again exactly the same.
1. 6:47: The roar. A dinosaur on his chest. Max sits up, sees his stripped room, and thinks he's slept through Sunday and the truck. Then Gus says, exactly the same words as yesterday. Max's stomach drops all the way to the pillow.
2. 7:13: Waffles steals the left slipper. Same place under the table. Same lost sock and same Lego and the fossilized french fry. Max stands up slowly and says, out loud, "Nope." Mom asks who he's talking to. He looks at her clipboard. Same six pages. Same pencil.
3. 7:22: The toast burns. The smoke alarm. Dad's anti-gravity pun. Word for word. Max says the punchline over Dad's shoulder, before Dad can. Dad looks at him with real alarm: "How did you know that?" Max says, "I read it somewhere." He runs to the bathroom and locks the door.
4. 8:05: Max opens the door before Mrs. Abernathy rings, and says, "It's a hot dish, so be careful, you'll want a trivet." She drops her trivet. Mom tells Max not to tease the neighbors. Max starts a list in the margin of his hand: Things That Are Exactly The Same.
5. Max escapes out the back and runs down Larkspur Lane in his slippers till his lungs burn, hoping to break something. He runs to Weller Creek and lies on the bank. He falls asleep on purpose. Wakes up on his air mattress. 6:47. Roar. "That was when I understood I hadn't dreamed anything."

## 6. The Rules of Saturday
Synopsis: Max runs experiments to learn the rules: everyone resets, he remembers, nothing sticks. He starts a tally on his closet wall.
1. Saturday number three: Max tries staying awake all night on purpose, propped in the hall with a bag of pretzels. He sees Gerald at 11:59 opening and closing his little door with a soft click. He blinks, and it's 6:47. Roar. He starts making lists on the closet wall in pencil: the first tally mark.
2. Saturday number four: He breaks something on purpose (Mom's blue mug). At 6:47 the mug is on the shelf, whole and dry. He finds a splinter he'd got from the box the day before is gone. He eats seven pieces of pizza at breakfast and feels no stomach ache the next loop. He writes RULE 1: Everything resets except me.
3. Saturday number five: He tries telling Mom the truth at breakfast. "I'm living the same day over and over." Mom feels his forehead and asks if he's worried about the move. He tries Dad ("Dude, I get it, big changes...") and Tessa ("Take a lap."). RULE 2: Nobody believes me. Nobody remembers me trying either.
4. Saturday number six: Max tries to escape the block. He walks to the town line sign and turns around at 11:58 p.m. The clock stays in its place; Gerald opens his little door; 6:47. RULE 3: The day ends wherever I am. RULE 4: Nothing I do sticks. He looks at the list and says, "So basically I can do whatever I want." A slow, bad idea starts to grin at him.
5. He tells the reader: this is a good place to say what I knew and what I didn't. He knew every second of this day. He didn't know why. He didn't know how many Saturdays there'd be. He didn't yet know that a person can be in the same day a hundred times and still not have been in it once. End on a hook: a single, hopeful, foolish thought: what if he could just have fun?

## 7. Jonah Believes (Every Time)
Synopsis: Max decides to tell Jonah the truth and has to convince him from scratch every loop; Jonah's belief becomes the best part of each Saturday, and the hardest.
1. Saturday number seven: Max goes to Jonah's house at 11:00 instead of waiting, and Jonah answers in his bike helmet, surprised. Max says the day is repeating. Jonah asks kindly if he's had enough sleep, and offers him a granola bar. Max knows what Jonah will say before he says it and it is unbearably lonely.
2. Max tries proving it: he predicts the mailman's soaking at 4:10 (Jonah writes it in his notebook), and predicts what Mrs. Abernathy will say. Jonah is interested but polite and skeptical. Then Max plays the ace, the private thing only Jonah knows: "You cried at the end of that dog movie. The one with the golden retriever and the train station." Jonah goes white, then pink, then whispers: "Okay. I believe you."
3. Jonah is the best possible person to have a time loop with. He instantly wants a system: laminates a chart, proposes hypotheses (a wormhole, the clock, cheese), and tests each of them on Max's behalf with total seriousness. They spend the afternoon chasing the sprinkler on purpose. It's the best afternoon Max has had in the whole month.
4. At 11:59, Jonah's mom calls him home. Max realizes Jonah will be back to zero at 6:47, that this Jonah, who cried and laughed and laminated and believed him, will vanish and only Max will remember. He walks home slowly. He thinks about it for a long time. He has to prove himself again next morning, from the beginning.
5. Saturday number eight: same proof, same dog movie, and Jonah says, "Okay. I believe you," in exactly the same voice, at exactly the same moment. Max laughs and then finds his eyes are wet. He says out loud, to the ceiling, "It's the same Jonah every time and I get to be the only one who's different." Hook: he decides that if nothing counts, he's going to have the best time of his life.

## 8. The Day With No Consequences
Synopsis: A joyful, chaotic montage of Max spending loops on outrageous fun: pancakes, pranks, a giant cardboard slide, a cannonball. Everything is hilarious and nothing counts.
1. Saturday number nine: He eats a breakfast of fourteen pancakes with whipped cream and syrup and a bowl of cereal for dessert, and the family stares. He rides Tessa's scooter down Dead Man's Hill, wearing Jonah's helmet, and screams all the way to the bottom. Nothing hurts. He goes back and does it thirty-one more times over four loops.
2. Saturdays ten and eleven: Max and Jonah (re-convinced each time, faster now) build a cardboard slide from the moving boxes down the garage roof into Mr. Grady's inflatable pool. Then they set the record for most marshmallows in a mouth (Jonah: 19; Max: 27, and nobody dies). They give Mrs. Abernathy a tomato-plant makeover. She loves it every time, and she never remembers.
3. Saturday twelve: Max says every rude, wild, honest thing he can think of to the people around him, because who'll remember? He tells Mr. Grady his sprinkler is a menace. He calls the mailman "Sir Splashalot." He shows up at Putt-Putt Palace in a bathrobe and gets a hole-in-one. It's very funny. He is laughing until his stomach hurts.
4. Saturday thirteen: He eats all the ice cream at Scoops (fourteen flavors, one scoop each) with Jonah, who is by now happily on a first-name basis with his own personal science. Rules keep getting confirmed: nothing sticks, nothing costs. He tells the reader this is what he'd call the Golden Age.
5. Saturday fourteen: At 6:47 the roar arrives and this time it doesn't make Max angry. It makes him tired. He has done everything. He lies on the air mattress and thinks: what do I do with the next hundred Saturdays? Hook: nobody tells you that the trouble with a day you can do anything in is that you run out of things you want.

## 9. Ninety Seconds of Fun
Synopsis: The fun goes hollow. The jokes are stale, everyone is a copy of themselves, and Max feels more alone in a house full of people who don't know him.
1. Saturday fifteen: The pancakes are just pancakes. Dad's pun lands; Max's mouth says the punchline along with him without permission. Nothing surprises him. He notices for the first time how much of a family is surprise.
2. Saturday nineteen: Max tries doing something huge: he takes the Buick keys from Grandma Bea's purse, then puts them back, shaking, because even a time loop can't make a car safe. He learns nothing counts and the wrong thing can still be wrong. Something in him, quietly, says: even if nobody's watching, you're watching.
3. Saturday twenty-three: Jonah's belief is beautiful again for the seventh time, and this time Max notices it's not really Jonah's; it's a hello he's been reading off a card. He tells Jonah "I love you, man," and Jonah, deeply alarmed, offers him a granola bar and steps back. Max laughs, then he can't stop, then he isn't laughing.
4. Saturday twenty-eight: Max is alone on the garage roof at 3:00 with pretzels, watching the street he has memorized down to every crack. He tells the reader about the thing nobody tells you about a perfect anything: it's the loneliest place in the world. He is a boy at the very center of a town where nobody knows him.
5. At 11:58, he doesn't wish. He says to Gerald, "How do I make this stop?" Gerald is silent, of course. Max flops on the hall floor and stares up at him. He thinks: maybe fun isn't the point. Maybe the day just has to be perfect and then it lets me go. Hook: "I got out my pencil and turned to a clean page. I called it OPERATION PERFECT SATURDAY. This is the part where things get worse."

# PART THREE: OPERATION PERFECT SATURDAY

## 10. Operation Perfect Saturday
Synopsis: Max writes a minute-by-minute plan to make the day flawless, fixing every problem before it happens. It fails in new and funnier ways each loop.
1. Saturday twenty-nine: The plan, written in pencil in a notebook: a minute-by-minute schedule with columns for PROBLEM and FIX. He prevents the burnt toast by unplugging the toaster. Dad uses the oven broiler and sets off the alarm anyway. Dad makes a new pun. Max writes: PUN: UNSTOPPABLE.
2. Saturdays thirty to thirty-six: He stops the box drop at 9:15 by carrying the FRAGILE box himself, and drops it himself. He tries to stop the sprinkler, and Mr. Grady turns it on early. He stops the sprinkler by turning the tap off, and the mailman slips on a wet patch of other lawn. "The universe was a whack-a-mole game and I was a mole with a notebook."
3. Saturdays thirty-seven to forty-four: He and Jonah devise a spreadsheet, a schedule, and four backup plans. Something always slips: a bee at Putt-Putt Palace, a bike chain, Scoops runs out of the flavor Jonah wanted. Each time they reset, Jonah, unfailingly cheerful, cheers Max up, and Max snaps at him for the first time when Jonah's proof-of-belief takes too long.
4. Saturday forty-five: Max notes that the perfect Saturday is an unlucky number: things can be perfect in exactly none of the ways he imagined. The closet wall has forty-four tally marks. He counts them out loud and dislikes the sound. He fixes Gus's meltdown about the T. rex tail catching in the door, and Gus stops crying. Max feels good for about six minutes.
5. Then Gus reappears next morning at 6:47, as if none of it had ever happened, and roars. Max lies flat and says, "You're welcome." Hook: "It occurred to me that I had done a lot of fixing, and I still hadn't noticed a single thing about anyone but myself."

## 11. Everybody's Scared
Synopsis: While chasing perfection, Max finally notices his family's secrets: everyone is hurting about the move, and Max wasn't the only one.
1. Saturday forty-six: Instead of stopping the burnt toast, Max watches Dad's face after the pun. Dad's smile falls off for one second when he thinks no one's looking, and he stares at the calendar. Max realizes the puns come out fastest when Dad's most nervous. Dad has never lived anywhere but Dunmore either.
2. Saturday fifty-one: Max follows Mom's clipboard. She has a page marked WORRIES that she keeps folded into the fourth page. It says: Will the kids be okay? Will I be okay? Find the good pediatrician. Learn to make friends at forty-three. He reads it in the pantry with the fossilized french fry. He doesn't say anything.
3. Saturday fifty-four: The garage argument with Tessa; but this time Max stays and listens after she slams the door. Through the wall, he hears her drumsticks tapping and, under it, the sound of someone crying into a sleeve. She's leaving her band, the Kettle Drums, and starting tenth grade with nobody. Max sits on the other side of the door and doesn't say a word, and just stays.
4. Saturday fifty-eight: Gus. Max climbs in with him in the tent made of moving blankets and asks him what he's scared of. Gus, in a tiny voice: "Does the new house have a room for a T. rex?" The costume is not a costume. It's armor, like Max's hoodie. Max looks down at his red hoodie in June, at ninety degrees.
5. Saturday sixty: Max lies on his air mattress, and the ceiling stares back. He tells the reader: I'd spent sixty Saturdays thinking I was the only person in the world this was happening to. It was happening to everyone in that house. And I was the only one who had noticed, and I couldn't tell anyone. Hook: "So I did the only thing I could think of. I decided to fix them, too."

## 12. The Fixer
Synopsis: Max tries to make everyone else happy, and it works, briefly, and evaporates each reset. He is exhausted, and starts to resent them.
1. Saturday sixty-one: He gives Dad a real hug and asks him about Ohio; Dad talks for twenty minutes, misty. At 6:47 Dad doesn't remember. Max serves Mom breakfast in bed with a list called NOTHING TO WORRY ABOUT and she cries happy tears. At 6:47 she is back to the clipboard.
2. Saturdays sixty-two to sixty-six: Max drives a campaign for Tessa: he finds a way for her to drum at Larkspur Park. He asks Jonah's sister to lend a drum kit. Tessa plays for an hour and smiles big, the first smile since the move began. Max watches from the fence. It's the best thing he's ever done.
3. Saturday sixty-seven: Tessa, at 10:30 in the garage, slams the door again, exactly like the first day, because at 6:47 her happiness went back into the box it came from. Max sits on the other side of the door with the box of posters and says, "I can't do this again." He realizes he isn't fixing anyone; he's refilling a bucket with a hole in the bottom.
4. Saturdays sixty-eight to seventy: Max is worn out. He's a butler and a cheerleader and a therapist and he's eleven. He snaps at Gus. He snaps at Jonah. He snaps at Dad's puns. He notices with horror that the person who is unhappiest in the house is the one running the "Everyone's Happy" program. He asks for one thing back: to feel good himself.
5. Saturday seventy: He tells the reader about the lowest thing he thought during all this: that maybe the wish was punishment. Maybe he was being taught that he was the kind of person who wants things to stay the same and is wrong. It felt worse than being sad. Hook: "The next Saturday was the one I'm least proud of. I'll tell it fast."

## 13. The Worst Dinner in the Universe
Synopsis: On Saturday seventy-one, Max explodes at the family dinner and says the cruelest true things he knows. Nobody remembers it but him, and that is the worst part.
1. Saturday seventy-one, 5:30: Pizza on the floor. Dad's toast to the house. Mom's tears. Max has heard every word of this toast seventy times, and he can't hold it. Halfway through Dad's line about cactus adventures, he says, "Stop. Please just stop talking."
2. Max's voice climbs. He tells them none of them can do anything about anything, that Dad's puns are pathetic, that Mom's lists don't stop anything from being scary. Mom says, quietly, "Max." He's past listening.
3. He turns to Tessa: "And you. You're going to get to the new school and nobody's going to talk to you and you're going to cry in the bathroom, and you know it." Tessa's fork clatters. She stares at him. Nobody has told anybody this. He said it because he knows it, and that is exactly why it's unforgivable.
4. He turns on Gus: "And it's a costume, Gus. It's just a costume. It doesn't protect anybody." Gus's lip trembles inside the T. rex hood. Waffles whines. The circle of paper plates is very quiet. Mom is standing up with her hand on her mouth. Max, appalled at himself, runs out of the house without his shoes.
5. He sits on the garage roof in the dark. He waits for the clock, for 11:58, for anything. Mom finds him, and she doesn't yell. She says, "I don't know what happened tonight, but I love you." And then the roar. 6:47. Everyone is whole again and remembers nothing. Max is the only one who knows what he said. "You can't say sorry to someone who never heard you be awful. I found that out about seven seconds after the worst night of my life."

## 14. Grandma Bea and the Peach
Synopsis: Max goes to Grandma Bea and asks a question he could have asked at any time: what was her worst day? She tells him the peach story.
1. Saturday seventy-two: Max doesn't get up at 6:47. He lies still while Gus roars over him, and Gus climbs off, puzzled. He walks to the garage with his hood up and thinks about who in this town has never once been asked a real question by him. Waffles the corgi follows him and lies on his feet. It's the first comfort that's ever come to him without conditions.
2. 1:15: Grandma Bea arrives in the Buick with the clock in the laundry basket. He follows her into the hall. He asks, before the hook, before Gerald: "What was your worst day, Nonna?" (Grandma Bea, taken aback, takes off her sunglasses for the first time all book.) He tells her, without the loop, that he has been having a truly awful time.
3. She tells the peach story: at eleven, leaving Naples, in a bare airport in Ohio, furious at everything: the plane, the cold, her mother. A stranger, an old man in a raincoat, cut a peach in half and handed her one. It was the best peach she ever tasted. She sat on her suitcase and ate it and thought: this day is going to be terrible either way. I get to choose whether I'm terrible in it.
4. Max says, "That's it? That's the wisdom? A peach?" Grandma Bea laughs so hard her bangles jingle. "It's not the peach, caro. It's that I decided before the peach came. You think I was happy when I got the peach? No. I was happy because I ate the peach on purpose." She says: "Happiness isn't what you get at the end of a day. It's what you bring to the front of it." She hangs the clock and pats his cheek.
5. Max sits under Gerald in the empty hall and stares at the crooked beak for a long time. He thinks: I had lived seventy-two Saturdays, and I'd been asking the wrong question of the clock. It was never how do I make this stop; it was how do I want to be in it. Hook: "That night, at 11:59, I did not make a wish. I went to bed. And at 6:47..."

# PART FOUR: THE CHOOSING

## 15. The Burnt Toast Experiment
Synopsis: Max wakes at 6:47 and decides, before anything happens, to be happy on purpose. He tests it against the worst parts of the same old day. It works, mostly, in small ways.
1. Saturday seventy-three, 6:47: Roar. Max opens his eyes, before the roar has finished, and says to the ceiling: "I choose it. On purpose. Before anything happens." He feels stupid. He says it again. Then he looks at Gus in the dinosaur costume and says, "RAAAAWWWRRR!" back, as loud as he can. Gus falls off the bed laughing.
2. 7:13: Waffles takes the slipper. Max lets him. He crawls under the table and gives Waffles the other slipper, too, and they lie there together with the fossilized french fry. Waffles appears delighted. Max, for the first time, is looking at what's actually under the table: a peaceful dark cave with a dog. It's not bad.
3. 7:22: The toast burns. Max takes a piece, scrapes the black off, and puts peanut butter on it. It is the best toast he's ever had. Dad, alarmed by the noise of a happy child, delivers his anti-gravity pun. Max laughs at it. The real one. Dad glows. Max feels his own laugh come out of somewhere he'd forgotten he had.
4. 8:05: Mrs. Abernathy with the hot dish. Instead of saying the line over her, Max says, "Thank you, Mrs. Abernathy. Can I ask you something? Why do you bring one every Saturday?" She goes quiet, looks at the casserole, and says it's what you do when someone you loved stops needing dinner for two. She's cooked for the street since Mr. Abernathy died. Max hugs her (she smells of tomato leaves).
5. By noon he has to admit something: nothing outside has changed. Same day, same toast, same box. Only he's different, and the day seems to be a different day. He writes on the closet wall, next to the tally marks, one line. It says: THE DAY IS THE SAME. I DON'T HAVE TO BE. Hook: "I'd love to tell you it was easy. The next hour, Tessa slammed the garage door."

## 16. Sad and Happy at the Same Time
Synopsis: Choosing happiness doesn't mean pretending. Max learns to be sad and glad at once, and that feelings don't cancel each other out.
1. Saturday seventy-four: 10:30, the garage. Tessa. Max doesn't take the bait. He says, "I'm sorry about your band." Tessa is stunned: he's not supposed to know. "I'm sad about it too. I'm not going to play on the Dunmore Rockets again either." They sit against the wall and don't say anything for a while. Then, "You're weird today," she says. He says, "Thanks."
2. Saturday seventy-five: Max lets himself cry when he sees Jonah's helmet through the window at 11:00, then washes his face, then opens the door. He realizes he's doing both: crying that he has to leave, and glad that Jonah is here. "They weren't fighting. They were riding in the same car."
3. Saturday seventy-six: The rest of the day goes wrong in the usual ways, but he tests a new rule: pick one good thing per hour, out loud. 9:15: the FRAGILE box, Mom's save, which is actually a really impressive dive. 12:30: paper-towel sandwiches, which taste better on the floor. 2:00: he picks the wet shoes.
4. Saturday seventy-seven: Max runs a real test: he lets the day be as bad as it wants to be. It rains at 4 (which it never does). The sprinkler goes off inside the rain. The mailman, soaked, laughs. Max has a bad, sad hour on the roof, but he says out loud: "I'm sad. And I'm going to have pizza anyway." He does. It's the first time pizza on the floor doesn't feel like a funeral.
5. He looks at the tally marks on the closet wall: seventy-seven now. He tells the reader the thing he'd been afraid of: that choosing to be happy meant pretending he wasn't sad, which is exactly what everyone had been doing for weeks, in different costumes. "It doesn't mean you don't feel the bad thing," he says. "It means you decide what's going to drive." Hook: he's ready to try the hardest apology of his life.

## 17. Sorry, to Someone Who Won't Remember
Synopsis: Max apologizes to his family for a dinner they don't remember; he learns you don't apologize to be forgiven, you apologize because it's true.
1. Saturday seventy-nine: Max finds Gus under the moving blankets, and tells him honestly, without the loop: "I'm sorry I told you it was just a costume. It's not. It's the bravest thing I know." Gus, six, listens, and hands Max his spare spiky tail. From then on Max wears it under his hoodie for the rest of the loops.
2. Saturday eighty-two: He finds Dad in the yard at dusk and asks about the moving job; Dad admits he's scared the new job is too big for him. Max tells him: "Your puns are the best part of the day." Dad stares at him for a long time and says, "Even the toast one?" "Especially the toast one." They hug by the dead grass.
3. Saturday eighty-four: Mom. Max asks her to read him the WORRIES page from her clipboard, without her hiding it. He listens to all of it, and he says he'll help her carry three. She says, "Which three?" "The doctor. The friends. The Will I be okay one." She cries into the clipboard and laughs.
4. Saturday eighty-eight: Tessa. The hardest one. He says, "I said something awful and you don't remember it and I do. I'm sorry." She says, "I don't remember it." "I know." A pause. "Then you're weirdly good at apologizing for things that haven't happened yet." "It happened." She looks at him for a long time and says, "Okay. Thanks for telling me." She hooks her pinky around his, a habit from when they were little.
5. Max finds out something: it doesn't matter that they'll forget. He said it, and he meant it, and it's true in him. He writes on the closet wall: SORRY COUNTS EVEN IF NOBODY KEEPS IT. Hook: "Which left one goodbye I hadn't done properly. The one with a bike helmet."

## 18. Jonah's Last Day Extravaganza
Synopsis: On Saturday ninety-nine, Max accepts Jonah's plan and goes. It rains, everything goes wrong, and it's the best day of his life.
1. Saturday ninety-nine, 2:00: Max is at Jonah's door at 1:55, in wet-able shoes. Jonah cannot believe it. "You came." He reads the itinerary aloud with less confidence than usual, and Max says, "I'm here for all of it. Even the part about the raft."
2. The creek: the raft, of course, sinks in eight inches of water. They are laughing so hard they can't stand. Jonah's helmet fills with creek water; he pours it over his own head in ceremonial style. Max tells him for the first time in any loop, without reasons: "You're my best friend, you know that?" Jonah says, "I know. It's on the laminate."
3. Putt-Putt Palace: it thunders, the storm the forecast never called for. They play the last hole in a downpour, with a plastic windmill spinning like a fan, and Max sinks a hole-in-one accidentally, and they scream. Nobody at the whole course is happy but them, and it doesn't matter.
4. Scoops: closed for the storm. Jonah is devastated for exactly 3 seconds and then lifts his arms: "Rain ice cream!" They stand in the deluge in the parking lot with their mouths open until Jonah's mom finds them and drives them home. Jonah's gift, wrapped in a plastic bag: two old walkie-talkies. "Range is two miles. So it's basically useless. Take it."
5. On the porch, Jonah asks, awkwardly, if Max's Saturday problem is fixed. Max says, "I stopped needing it fixed." Jonah nods like he understands, though he can't. They agree: Saturdays, 6:47 a.m., every week, one of them calls the other. Max says, "You won't remember agreeing to this." Jonah says, "Then write it on a laminate."

## 19. The Last Dinner on the Floor
Synopsis: The last evening, Saturday one hundred. Everything goes wrong (the truck arrives early to stage, the power goes out), and Max chooses to make it the best dinner of their lives. At midnight, he does not ask for more.
1. Saturday one hundred: The power goes out at 4 pm, the pizza place is closed, and the truck company calls to say they're coming at 6 a.m. instead. Mom starts to fall apart, checking off tasks that are no longer possible. Max takes her clipboard, gently, and writes at the top of page one: 1. Dinner. He puts a big check by it. "Trust me," he says.
2. Max leads a candlelit picnic on the living-room floor: candles in jars, Mrs. Abernathy's tuna noodle casserole warmed on Dad's camp stove. Dad's puns come in full, fond flow. Tessa drums on the empty pots. Gus roars at the candle flames, delighted. Waffles steals a slipper and no one minds.
3. Everyone says their one thing they'll miss, and this time Max goes first: "I'll miss this floor. And Waffles under the table. And Dad's toast. And the way every one of you is loud." He looks at each of them: "And I'll take it with me, because that's the whole trick. It goes in a pocket." Nobody says anything. Dad wipes his mustache with the fish apron.
4. 11:58: He goes down to the hall. He talks to Gerald without asking. He says, "Thanks. If you want to do a hundred and one, that's okay. I'd rather be here happy than there happy-later. But I think it's time." He thinks about wishing to end it. He doesn't. He tells the reader the truth: the day ends when you stop bargaining with it.
5. Gerald's door opens. One cuckoo, slightly crooked, almost gentle. Max says "Good night, Gerald," and goes up. On the stairs he sees the family asleep on air mattresses in the candlelight, in a heap. He is smiling, and it isn't the smile from the first Saturday. Hook: he closes his eyes, and it feels different. "I fell asleep on purpose, and I did not once ask what would happen next."

## 20. Sunday
Synopsis: Max wakes at 6:47 to a roar, and it's Sunday. The truck comes, the family goes, and the story ends in a new hall in Arizona with a clock, a cactus, and a Saturday call.
1. 6:47: The roar. Max's heart stops, and then he sees the sun, the wrong angle of it, and Gus's tiny voice: "Max! It's Sunday! The truck's here!" He sits up. He laughs, then he doesn't quite know why he's crying. Mom knocks with her clipboard: Sunday, page six, the last item. Dad's toast burns and Max eats it with peanut butter.
2. The truck. Movers, boxes, Mrs. Abernathy on her porch in her hat, waving. Max walks over to her before he leaves and says, "It's a hot dish, so be careful, you'll want a trivet." She bursts out laughing, hugs him, and says, "I'll send one to Arizona." Mr. Grady's sprinkler soaks the mailman one last time; Max waves.
3. Jonah runs down the street in his bike helmet, out of breath, holding a small laminated card. It says, in careful marker: SATURDAYS 6:47. He hands Max the walkie-talkie. Max hands Jonah the spare spiky T. rex tail. They don't say goodbye. They say, "See you Saturday."
4. The drive: 1,700 miles. Gus in the back, roaring at every state line. Tessa with her headphones and, after a while, her hand on Max's hand. Grandma Bea's Buick follows them out to the highway, her horn playing its tune, until she turns off and vanishes in the mirror. Dad makes a pun about cactus. Max laughs, and it's real.
5. Tucson: the new house, tan and flat. In the empty front hall Max hangs Gerald on the first hook. It's 11:59, of course, and the little door opens. One cuckoo. Max says, "Hey, Gerald." Nothing happens; the next day is going to come. He tells the reader: "Here's what I know. Happiness isn't what happens when the day goes right. It's what you bring to the day, on purpose, before it has a chance to prove you wrong. I learned that in a hundred Saturdays. You can have it in one." Last line: Saturday, 6:47, the walkie-talkie crackles.
